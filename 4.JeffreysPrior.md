# Jeffreys Prior and Reparameterization

A prior that is uniform in one parameterization is generally not uniform in another. Jeffreys' rule addresses this problem for regular models by constructing a prior measure from the likelihood's Fisher information. Its main attraction is **invariance**, not the impossible promise of representing complete ignorance. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

## 1. Why uniformity depends on the scale

Suppose theta is a probability and

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).
$$

If eta=logit(theta), then

$$
\theta=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta),
\qquad
\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|=\theta(1-\theta).
$$

The induced density is

$$
p(\eta)=p(\theta)\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|
\propto\theta(1-\theta),
$$

which is not uniform on the log-odds scale.

:::{admonition} Intuition
:class: tip
A density is measured per unit of its coordinate system. Equal-width intervals in probability do not correspond to equal-width intervals in log-odds. “Flat” therefore has no meaning until the coordinate system is named.
:::

## 2. Scalar Jeffreys rule

For a regular model with one parameter theta, the expected Fisher information for one observation is

$$
I(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{y\mid\theta}
\left[
\left(
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
\log p(y\mid\theta)
\right)^2
\right].
$$

Under standard regularity conditions it is also

$$
I(\theta)
=
-\mathbb{E}_{y\mid\theta}
\left[
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}
\log p(y\mid\theta)
\right].
$$

Jeffreys prior is

$$
\pi_J(\theta)\propto\sqrt{I(\theta)}.
$$

The square root of information defines a volume element that transforms correctly under a smooth one-to-one reparameterization. If phi=g(theta), deriving the rule in phi gives the same prior measure as transforming the rule derived in theta.

:::{warning}
Jeffreys prior does **not** generally put more weight where the likelihood is less informative. It is proportional to the square root of Fisher information. Its rationale is invariant measurement of parameter-space volume, not compensation for low information.
:::

## 3. Bernoulli and Binomial probability

For y~Bernoulli(theta),

$$
\log p(y\mid\theta)
=
y\log\theta+(1-y)\log(1-\theta).
$$

The Fisher information is

$$
I(\theta)=\frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)}.
$$

For N independent observations the information is multiplied by N, which does not change the prior after normalization. Therefore,

$$
\pi_J(\theta)
\propto
\theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2},
$$

or

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}\left(\frac12,\frac12\right).
$$

This Jeffreys prior is proper and U-shaped. It assigns more density near 0 and 1 than the Uniform Beta(1,1) prior. That shape is a consequence of the information geometry; it should not be described as a neutral belief that moderate and extreme probabilities are equally plausible.

### Demonstration: probability versus log-odds

Transforming the Jeffreys density to eta=logit(theta) gives

$$
\pi_J(\eta)
=
\pi_J(\theta)
\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|
\propto
\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}.
$$

Deriving the Fisher information directly in eta gives

$$
I(\eta)=\theta(1-\theta),
$$

and therefore the same result. The densities have different shapes because they use different coordinates, but they represent the same prior measure.

````{dropdown} Question: Does invariance mean the density curve looks identical after transformation?
No. The curve changes because density is measured per unit of the new coordinate. Invariance means that deriving the Jeffreys rule directly in the new parameterization agrees with transforming the original prior using the Jacobian.
````

## 4. Normal-model examples

Jeffreys constructions must state which parameters are known and which are estimated.

### Unknown mean with known variance

For

$$
y\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2)
$$

with known sigma,

$$
I(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2},
\qquad
\pi_J(\mu)\propto1.
$$

The prior is improper and flat over the location parameter.

### Unknown variance with known mean

Writing v=sigma^2,

$$
I(v)=\frac{1}{2v^2},
\qquad
\pi_J(v)\propto\frac{1}{v}.
$$

Equivalently, the prior is flat in log(v).

### Unknown mean and variance together

For the two-parameter model, terminology matters:

- the full determinant-based Jeffreys rule gives
  $$
  \pi_J(\mu,\sigma)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^2},
  $$
  equivalently pi_J(mu,v) proportional to v^(-3/2);
- a commonly used independent/reference prior gives
  $$
  \pi_R(\mu,\sigma)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma},
  $$
  equivalently pi_R(mu,v) proportional to 1/v.

The second specification produces the standard posterior t interval for the Normal mean. It is often informally called “Jeffreys” in applied texts, but it is not identical to the full multiparameter Jeffreys rule. Multiparameter reference priors can depend on which parameter is treated as the primary inferential target. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

## 5. Sociological example: response probability

Suppose a pilot survey obtains 7 completed interviews from 10 sampled people. With Jeffreys prior,

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(0.5,0.5),
$$

the posterior is

$$
\theta\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{Beta}(7.5,3.5).
$$

This calculation estimates the response probability only under the model that the ten outcomes are exchangeable Bernoulli trials. It does not correct nonresponse bias: respondents and nonrespondents may differ systematically in the survey variables of interest.

:::{admonition} Demonstration block
:class: tip
Compare Jeffreys Beta(0.5,0.5), Uniform Beta(1,1) and weakly informative Beta(2,2) priors for the same 7/10 responses. Then repeat with 70/100 responses. The comparison shows how prior differences become smaller relative to the likelihood as information accumulates, while the interpretation of the response mechanism remains unchanged.
:::

## 6. Limitations and safe use

Jeffreys priors require care because:

1. they are often improper;
2. posterior propriety must be established;
3. multiparameter extensions are not unique in all practical senses;
4. improper priors cannot be used directly for prior predictive simulation;
5. ordinary Bayes factors are undefined when arbitrary normalizing constants from improper priors do not cancel;
6. invariance does not make a prior substantively appropriate for a particular population or decision.

For applied empirical work, a weakly informative proper prior is often easier to justify, diagnose and communicate. Jeffreys prior remains valuable as a reference analysis and as a demonstration of why parameterization matters.

## Guided practice

````{dropdown} Exercise 1: Derive the Bernoulli prior
Starting from I(theta)=1/[theta(1-theta)], take the square root and identify the corresponding Beta distribution.

**Answer.** The kernel is theta^(-1/2)(1-theta)^(-1/2), which is Beta(1/2,1/2).
````

````{dropdown} Exercise 2: Transform variance to precision
If v=sigma^2 has prior pi(v) proportional to 1/v and tau=1/v, show that pi(tau) is proportional to 1/tau.

**Answer.** Since v=1/tau and |dv/dtau|=1/tau^2,

$$
\pi(\tau)
=
\pi(1/\tau)\left|\frac{dv}{d\tau}\right|
\propto
\tau\frac{1}{\tau^2}
=
\frac{1}{\tau}.
$$
````

````{dropdown} Exercise 3: Reference does not mean harmless
Why should Jeffreys prior not be used automatically for a rare event probability?

**Answer.** Its U-shaped form assigns substantial density near both boundaries. A scientifically plausible rare-event prior may need almost all its mass near zero and should be calibrated through prior predictive event counts.
````

## Key takeaways

- Uniformity is parameterization-dependent.
- In regular one-parameter models, Jeffreys prior is proportional to the square root of Fisher information.
- Invariance means equivalence after the correct change of variables, not identical density shapes.
- For a Binomial probability, Jeffreys prior is Beta(1/2,1/2).
- One-parameter and multiparameter Normal-model results must not be conflated.
- Jeffreys priors are reference constructions, not substitutes for substantive prior predictive reasoning.
