## Why Power and Design Analysis Belong in Session 5

Bayesian analysis does not make sample size irrelevant. With too little information, the posterior will be wide, overly sensitive to the prior, and unable to distinguish scientifically important possibilities. The planning question is therefore not simply, *"How many observations does a Bayesian analysis need?"* It is:

> **What is the smallest feasible design that has an acceptable probability of achieving the study's stated inferential goal under scientifically plausible data-generating scenarios?**

In modern empirical science, research design is conducted via **Simulation-Based Research Design (SBRD)**: we create synthetic data from an explicit generative model forward, fit the Bayesian analysis intended for the real data, and inspect the repeated-simulation operating characteristics before data collection begins {cite}`gelman2021regression,kruschke2015doing,wang2002simulation`.

---

## 1. What Frequentist Power Answers (and What It Conceals)

For a prespecified hypothesis test, significance level $\alpha$ (conventionally $0.05$), sample size $N$, experimental design $d$, and a fixed assumed parameter value $\theta^*$, **frequentist statistical power** is defined as:

$$
\operatorname{Power}(N, \theta^*, d) = \Pr_{Y \sim p(Y \mid \theta^*, N, d)}\left(\text{reject } H_0\right).
$$

The standard ritual chooses $N$ so that power reaches an arbitrary threshold such as $80\%$ ($\beta = 0.20$). While useful when rejecting a straw-man null ($H_0: \theta = 0$) is genuinely the scientific decision, relying exclusively on a single power calculation conceals severe risks:

1. **$80\%$ is a historical convention**, not an optimal scientific decision rule.
2. **Fragility to effect assumptions**: Power is extremely sensitive to the assumed effect size $\theta^*$ and residual variance $\sigma^2$. If the true effect is $25\%$ smaller than assumed, power often plummets from $80\%$ to under $50\%$.
3. **The Interaction Trap**: A sample size powered at $80\%$ for a main effect is drastically underpowered for an interaction contrast of similar magnitude (which requires approximately $4\times$ the sample size).
4. **Silence on estimation accuracy**: Conventional power reports nothing about expected posterior interval width, sign error, or magnitude exaggeration after selection on $p < 0.05$.
5. **The Fallacy of "Observed Power"**: Post-hoc power computed from completed data using the observed point estimate is mathematically circular and uninformative; researchers should interpret the parameter estimate and posterior credible interval directly.

## 2. Is There a Bayesian Equivalent of Power?

There is no single universal replacement for power because Bayesian research designs are tailored to diverse scientific estimands. The general Bayesian object is the **probability of design success** $S_N(\theta)$ under a generative model, an analysis procedure, and a prespecified success criterion $g(\cdot)$:

$$
S_N(\theta) = \Pr_{Y \sim p(Y \mid \theta, N, d)}\left\{ g\left[ p(\theta \mid Y) \right] = 1 \right\}.
$$

| Inferential Goal | Prespecified Success Rule $g[p(\theta \mid Y)]$ | Simulation Planning Target |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Posterior Precision** | $95\%$ credible interval width for $\tau$ is $\le w^*$ | Probability of attaining useful scientific precision |
| **Directional Evidence** | $\Pr(\tau > 0 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ | Probability of establishing effect direction with high confidence |
| **Practical Relevance** | $\Pr(\tau > \delta_{\min} \mid Y) \ge 0.90$ | Probability of confirming a substantively meaningful effect |
| **ROPE Decision** | $95\%$ HDI falls completely outside (or inside) ROPE | Probability of ruling out (or accepting) practical equivalence {cite}`kruschke2015doing` |
| **Model Comparison** | Bayes factor $\text{BF}_{10} \ge 10$ or $\text{BF}_{01} \ge 10$ | Probability of compelling relative evidence vs. inconclusive outcome {cite}`schonbrodt2018bayes` |
| **Decision / Expected Utility**| Posterior expected loss or net utility meets threshold | Expected utility of the design under real-world costs |

### Core Course Terminology
* **Power**: The conventional frequentist probability of rejecting $H_0$ under a fixed parameter scenario.
* **Bayesian Design Success**: The probability of satisfying a prespecified posterior precision, directional, ROPE, or predictive criterion.
* **Operating Characteristics**: The complete multidimensional profile of a design across repeated datasets: success rate, posterior interval width, calibration/coverage, Type S/M risks, inconclusive rate, and computational failure.

## 3. Fixed Scenarios and Bayesian Assurance

### Fixed-Scenario Design Analysis
Rather than computing power at a single optimistic guess, analysts evaluate candidate sample sizes across a grid of plausible scenarios:
* **The Null / Negligible Scenario**: Evaluates false-positive rate and inconclusive decisions.
* **The Smallest Meaningful Effect ($\delta_{\min}$)**: The threshold below which an effect is practically trivial.
* **The Central Empirical Scenario**: Best external estimate from meta-analyses or previous literature.
* **The Adverse Scenario**: High measurement error, low compliance, or severe attrition.

### Bayesian Assurance (Pre-Posterior Power)
When the true effect size is uncertain, a **design prior** $p_D(\theta)$ represents that uncertainty. **Assurance** is the expected probability of success averaged over the design prior {cite}`ohagan2005assurance`:

$$
A(N) = \int S_N(\theta) \, p_D(\theta) \, d\theta = \Pr_{\theta \sim p_D, \; Y \sim p(Y \mid \theta, N, d)}(\text{success}).
$$

```{important}
**Design Prior vs. Analysis Prior:**  
Two priors have distinct responsibilities:
* The **design prior** $p_D(\theta)$ generates plausible true parameter values during planning (e.g. synthesizing historical pilot data).
* The **analysis prior** $p_A(\theta)$ is used inside the Bayesian model to compute the posterior for each synthetic or real dataset (e.g. a skeptical or weakly informative regularizer).

They need not be identical! For example, an analysis prior may be weakly informative $\operatorname{Normal}(0, 1)$ to prevent overfitting, while the design prior reflects past clinical trials.
```

## 4. What Does "Sufficient Sample Size" Mean?

There is no context-free universal sample size. A sample size is sufficient only with respect to a declared estimand, design constraints, and operating targets:

$$
d^* = \arg\min_{d \in \mathcal{D}} \operatorname{Cost}(d) \quad \text{subject to} \quad
\begin{cases}
\Pr(\text{interval width} \le w^*) \ge q_w, \\
\Pr(\text{wrong direction}) \le q_S, \\
\Pr(\text{success} \mid \theta_s, d) \ge q_s \quad \text{for required scenarios}, \\
\Pr(\text{sampler failure}) \le q_c.
\end{cases}
$$

If resources impose an absolute sample cap (e.g. $N \le 80$ clinical patients), reverse the question: **report the precision and effect sizes the feasible sample can reliably distinguish**, rather than claiming it is "powered."

## 5. The General Fake-Data Simulation Workflow

Simulation-based design follows a nine-step reproducible recipe:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   SIMULATION-BASED RESEARCH DESIGN WORKFLOW                 │
│ ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── │
│ 1. Define Estimand:         Target parameter, contrast, or prediction (scale)│
│ 2. Generative Model:        Draw θ^(m) ~ p_D(θ), simulate Y^(m) ~ p(Y|θ^(m))│
│ 3. Plausible Scenarios:     Null, minimum meaningful, optimistic, adverse   │
│ 4. Candidate Designs:       Vary N, cluster allocations, trial counts        │
│ 5. Freeze Analysis Rule:    Planned likelihood, priors, and success criteria │
│ 6. Repeated Simulation:     Fit Bayesian model across M = 1,000 datasets     │
│ 7. Quantify MC Uncertainty: Compute MCSE(p̂) = sqrt(p̂(1 - p̂) / M)             │
│ 8. Validate Finalists:      Check NUTS sampler diagnostics and coverage      │
│ 9. Select & Report:         Publish complete operating characteristic curves │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 6. Worked Case: The Educational Intervention (*FakeMidtermFinal*)

Following Gelman, Hill, and Vehtari (*Regression and Other Stories*, Chapter 16), consider evaluating an educational coaching program. Students take a midterm exam ($x_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(60, 20^2)$), half receive the intervention ($T_i \in \{0, 1\}$), and final exam scores follow:

$$
y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \tau T_i + \epsilon_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma^2 = 100).
$$

The true treatment effect is $\tau = 5.0$ points ($0.5$ residual SD). The theoretical standard error of $\hat{\tau}$ in a balanced randomized trial with pre-treatment covariate $x$ is:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\tau}) = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{2 \times 10}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{N}}.
$$

## 7. Beyond Power: Type S (Sign) and Type M (Magnitude) Selection Risks

When an empirical study has low precision ($N$ small, residual variance large), filtering findings by statistical significance ($p < 0.05$ or $|\hat{\tau}| \ge 1.96 \times \text{SE}$) introduces severe selection distortions {cite}`gelman2014beyond`:

1. **Type S (Sign) Error**: The probability that a statistically significant estimate has the **wrong sign** (points in the opposite direction of the true effect):
   $$\text{Type S} \equiv \Pr\left(\operatorname{sgn}(\hat{\tau}) \neq \operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{\text{true}}) \;\middle|\; |\hat{\tau}| \ge 1.96 \times \text{SE}\right).$$
2. **Type M (Magnitude / Exaggeration) Error**: The expected factor by which a statistically significant estimate **overestimates** the true effect size:
   $$\text{Type M} \equiv \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{|\hat{\tau}|}{|\tau_{\text{true}}|} \;\middle|\; |\hat{\tau}| \ge 1.96 \times \text{SE} \right].$$

## 8. Interactions Require Explicit Design Scenarios

A persistent fallacy in research design is assuming that a sample size powered for a main effect is sufficient to detect an interaction.

### The Balanced Gaussian $2 \times 2$ Factorial Design

Consider a $2 \times 2$ factorial experiment with cell means $\mu_{00}, \mu_{01}, \mu_{10}, \mu_{11}$ and total sample size $N$ ($N/4$ observations per cell). The interaction contrast is:

$$
\Delta_{AB} = (\mu_{11} - \mu_{10}) - (\mu_{01} - \mu_{00}).
$$

Under homoscedastic residual variance $\sigma^2$:
* The standard error of a main contrast (comparing two margins of $N/2$ units) is:
  $$\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{main}}) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{N/2} + \frac{\sigma^2}{N/2}} = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}.$$
* The standard error of the interaction contrast (summing variances across all four cells of $N/4$) is:
  $$\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\Delta}_{AB}) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{N/4} + \frac{\sigma^2}{N/4} + \frac{\sigma^2}{N/4} + \frac{\sigma^2}{N/4}} = \sqrt{\frac{16\sigma^2}{N}} = \frac{4\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} = 2 \times \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{main}}).$$

Because the standard error is **doubled**, matching the precision of a main effect requires **four times ($4\times$) the sample size**:

$$
\left(\frac{4\sigma}{\sqrt{N_{\text{interaction}}}}\right) = \left(\frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{N_{\text{main}}}}\right) \implies N_{\text{interaction}} = 4 \times N_{\text{main}}.
$$

Furthermore, if the true interaction effect is smaller than the main effect (e.g. half the size: $\Delta_{AB} = \frac{1}{2} \beta_{\text{main}}$), matching its signal-to-noise ratio requires **sixteen times ($16\times$) as many observations**!

### What Happens in Generalized Linear Models (GLMs)?

When moving to logistic or Poisson models, interaction design requirements become even more demanding:
1. **Link Scale vs. Response Scale**: $\beta_3 = 0$ on the log-odds scale can produce a nonzero difference in proportions $\Delta p$ on the natural scale. Researchers must explicitly declare which scale is their scientific estimand.
2. **Mean-Dependent Variance**: In logistic regression, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = p(1 - p)$. When an outcome is rare ($p \le 0.05$), Fisher information collapses, and the sample size needed to distinguish an interaction on the probability scale explodes from $4\times$ to **$16\times$–$64\times$**!

```{admonition} Teaching Rule
:class: important
$4\times$ and $16\times$ are worked scenarios from *Regression and Other Stories*, not immutable laws. Sample size requirements depend on balance, predictor distributions, base rates, and hierarchy. Always simulate the specific scientific scenario forward!
```

## 9. Compact Reporting Template for Design Analyses

When presenting a Bayesian design analysis in a manuscript or grant proposal, use this structured template:

> **1. Estimand:** We target [contrast/parameter] on the [declared scale] in [target population].  
> **2. Candidate Designs:** We evaluated candidate sample sizes of $N \in [40, 100, 250, 500]$ units.  
> **3. Generating Scenarios:** Scenarios were based on [external evidence] and varied over [range of effects/variances].  
> **4. Analysis Procedure:** Each synthetic dataset was analyzed using [likelihood, priors, posterior summary].  
> **5. Success Criterion:** Success was defined as [posterior precision / directional probability], guarded by [Type S risk $\le 1\%$, Type M risk $\le 1.10\times$].  
> **6. Simulation:** We ran $M = 1,000$ replications per design-scenario combination ($\operatorname{MCSE} \le 0.015$).  
> **7. Decision:** We selected $N = [N^*]$ because it achieved $\ge 90\%$ probability of meeting our target across all required scenarios.

## 10. Common Mistakes in Power & Design Planning

1. **Claiming Bayesians "don't need power calculations"**: Bayesian inference does not rescue an underpowered study from wide, uninformative posteriors.
2. **Treating pilot point estimates as known truth**: Noisy pilot estimates are subject to extreme Type M exaggeration; always propagate uncertainty via a design prior.
3. **Powering for a main effect and assuming the interaction is covered**: Factorial interactions always require explicitly planned sample sizes.
4. **Ignoring clustering or repeated measures**: Treating repeated trials as independent observations artificially inflates apparent sample size and produces severely overconfident designs.
5. **Confusing MCMC effective sample size (ESS) with empirical sample size ($N$)**: ESS measures sampler efficiency, while $N$ determines epistemic information about the population.
