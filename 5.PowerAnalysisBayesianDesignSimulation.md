## Why Power and Design Analysis Belong in Session 5

Bayesian analysis does not make sample size irrelevant. With little information from the new data, posterior conclusions can remain uncertain or depend strongly on the prior. A small study may be fine when strong external knowledge justifies an informative prior — or misleading when the prior conflicts with reality. The planning question is therefore not simply *"How many observations does a Bayesian analysis need?"* It is:

> **What is the smallest feasible design that has an acceptable probability of achieving the study's stated inferential goal under scientifically plausible data-generating scenarios?**

A design analysis specifies what we want to learn and asks how often a proposed study would let us learn it. We first define the posterior statement that would count as success for one dataset. We then evaluate how often that statement would be reached across hypothetical datasets under explicit generating assumptions. Finally, we examine how the answer changes when the true effect, nuisance quantities, or prior assumptions differ. A sample-size answer is meaningful only together with those choices.

**Simulation-based research design (SBRD)** is the most flexible way to answer this question: create synthetic data from an explicit generative model, fit the Bayesian analysis intended for the real data, and inspect the repeated-simulation operating characteristics before data collection begins {cite}`gelman2021regression,kruschke2015doing,wang2002simulation`. Simple analytical calculations remain valuable — especially as transparent benchmarks that a simulation must reproduce — and they are all we need in the tractable examples of this chapter.

:::{note} 🧭 How to read this chapter (practitioner's route)
This chapter uses **one running example** — a balanced two-group exam-score contrast — and revisits it as the scientific goal changes: *Is the effect positive? Is it large enough to matter? Can we rule out a negligible effect? How precise will the estimate be?* Worked numbers appear in tables; the derivations that produce them are folded into optional dropdowns. If you take away one skill, it is this: **write down your declaration rule before you compute any sample size.**

## 1. What Frequentist Power Answers (and What It Conceals)

For a prespecified hypothesis test, significance level $\alpha$ (conventionally $0.05$), sample size $N$, experimental design $d$, and a fixed assumed parameter value $\theta^*$, **frequentist statistical power** is defined as:

$$
\operatorname{Power}(N, \theta^*, d) = \Pr_{Y \sim p(Y \mid \theta^*, N, d)}\left(\text{reject } H_0\right).
$$

Power is a correct and useful quantity for a fixed test and scenario: it answers *"if the true effect is exactly $\theta^*$, how often would this analysis reject?"* The scientific limitation is that rejection probability alone is not a complete design objective. Relying exclusively on a single power calculation conceals real risks:

1. **$80\%$ is a historical convention**, not an optimal scientific decision rule.
2. **Fragility to effect assumptions**: Power is sensitive to the assumed effect size $\theta^*$ and residual variance $\sigma^2$. A transparent example: for a two-sided Normal test at level $5\%$, if the true effect turns out $25\%$ smaller than assumed while the standard error is unchanged, power drops from $80\%$ to approximately $55.6\%$ — not catastrophically, but enough to invalidate a precise sampling plan.
3. **The interaction trap**: A sample size powered for a main effect is underpowered for an interaction contrast of the same magnitude. Under a balanced, homoscedastic $2\times 2$ design with independent observations, matching the precision of a main effect requires approximately $4\times$ the sample size (Section 8 derives this and states its scope).
4. **Silence on estimation accuracy**: Conventional power reports nothing about expected posterior interval width, sign error, or magnitude exaggeration after selection on $p < 0.05$.
5. **Observed power is circular — but retrospective design analysis is not.** *Plug-in* observed power computed from the completed study's own noisy point estimate restates the test result and adds no information; interpret the posterior directly instead. However, looking *back* at a design question with externally justified effect scenarios ("what if the true effect were as large as the literature suggests — how selective would our significant finding be?") is informative and explicitly recommended by {cite}`gelman2014beyond`.

## 2. Is There a Bayesian Equivalent of Power?

There is no single universal replacement for power because Bayesian research designs are tailored to diverse scientific estimands. The general Bayesian object is the **probability of design success** $S(d;\theta^*)$ under a generative model, an analysis procedure, and a prespecified **declaration rule** $D(y)$:

$$
D(y) = \mathbf{1}\left\{ \text{the planned posterior statement holds after observing } y \right\},
\qquad
S(d;\theta^*) = \Pr_{Y \sim p(Y \mid \theta^*, d)}\left[ D(Y) = 1 \right].
$$

Two probability levels are involved, and confusing them is the most common design-analysis error:

:::{important} The two thresholds: $\gamma$ (after the data) vs. $q$ (before the data)
* The **posterior threshold $\gamma$** (e.g. $0.95$) tells us what conclusion to make **after seeing one dataset**: "declare the effect positive when $P(\tau > 0 \mid y) \ge 0.95$."
* The **design target $q$** (e.g. $0.80$) tells us **how often future datasets** should let us make that conclusion: $S(d;\theta^*) \ge q$.

These are different requirements, not competing confidence levels. A rule with $\gamma = 0.95$ can perfectly well have a design success of $28\%$ at a plausible truth — Example A below shows exactly this. The repeated-data probability $S$ is a frequentist-style operating characteristic of a Bayesian rule; evaluating it is not "mixing paradigms," it is asking how the rule behaves under repetition.
:::

### The Menu of Declaration Rules

| Inferential Goal | Declaration rule for **one** dataset | What the simulation reports |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Posterior precision** | $95\%$ credible interval width for $\tau$ is $\le w^*$ | Probability of attaining useful precision; expected width vs. $N$ |
| **Directional evidence** | $\Pr(\tau > 0 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ | Probability of establishing the direction; wrong-direction risk |
| **Practical relevance** | $\Pr(\tau > \delta_{\min} \mid Y) \ge 0.90$, with $\delta_{\min}$ defined **first** | Probability of a meaningful-effect declaration at each scenario (see the warning box below) |
| **ROPE decision** | $95\%$ interval entirely outside — or entirely inside — the ROPE | Rates of four separate outcomes: meaningful benefit, meaningful harm, practical equivalence, inconclusive {cite}`kruschke2015doing` |
| **Model comparison** | $\text{BF}_{10} \ge 10$ or $\text{BF}_{01} \ge 10$ | Rates of *strong correct* vs. *strong misleading* vs. *inconclusive* evidence under each scenario {cite}`schonbrodt2018bayes` |
| **Decision / utility** | Choose the action with highest posterior expected utility | Expected utility $\bar U(d)$ of the design under real-world costs (see below) |

Three of these rows deserve immediate qualification.

**ROPE decisions are four conclusions, not two.** "Accepting" practical equivalence is a decision relative to a tolerance region, not proof of an exact point null. Report meaningful benefit, meaningful harm, practical equivalence, and unresolved overlap as separate outcomes — a design that ends inconclusive $60\%$ of the time is reporting something different from one that decides $95\%$ of the time.

**Bayes factors measure evidence, not direction.** Strong evidence in either direction includes the possibility of *strong misleading* evidence under a given truth. A design evaluation records how often each kind of conclusion is reached — not just how often BF exceeds a threshold. (The advanced dropdown after Example A shows that a $96\%$ directional posterior probability can coexist with a Bayes factor that slightly *favors the null*.)

**Maximizing the probability that utility exceeds a threshold is not maximizing expected utility.** These sound similar but rank designs differently. A design with $90\%$ chance of utility $+1$ and $10\%$ chance of $-100$ has expected utility $-9.1$; one with $80\%$ chance of $+1$ and $20\%$ chance of $-1$ has expected utility $+0.6$. If decision quality matters, compute expected utility directly:

$$
\bar U(d) = E_{\theta, Y}\left[ u\left(a(Y, d), \theta\right) \right] - \operatorname{Cost}(d),
$$

where $a(Y,d)$ is the planned posterior-based action and the expectation uses the declared generating distribution {cite}`chaloner1995bayesian`. Probability-of-success optimization is the special case where $u$ only rewards success — a legitimate choice, but make it consciously.

### ⚠️ Detecting an effect of size $\delta$ is not establishing that the effect exceeds $\delta$

This is the most consequential distinction in design analysis. Compare two planning goals:

1. **Detect positivity**, evaluated when the true effect equals the smallest meaningful effect: rule $P(\tau > 0 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$, evaluated at truth $\tau^* = \delta$. More data steadily raises success toward $100\%$.
2. **Establish exceedance of the threshold**: rule $P(\tau > \delta \mid Y) \ge 0.95$. When the truth sits **exactly on** the boundary $\tau^* = \delta$, this rule does *not* become successful with growing sample size — in a regular Normal benchmark its success probability approaches $5\%$. It demands strong evidence that the effect lies beyond a boundary the truth sits on.

Both are legitimate scientific questions; they are simply different. Name the reference boundary and the generating effect **separately** in every worked design. And do not demand $90\%$ declaration success across a scenario grid that *includes* null or boundary scenarios — under the null the desired outcome is usually a correct equivalence or a withheld declaration, not a positive finding. Example C makes this concrete.

### Running Example A: One Study, Three Questions

*All numbers in Examples A–D come from one transparent model, recomputed independently; the derivations are in the dropdown.*

**Setup.** A balanced two-group contrast (exam scores; scale: points), total $N$ students across both arms, known residual SD $\sigma = 10$. At the contrast level the estimator satisfies $\hat\tau \mid \tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\tau, v_N)$ with $v_N = 400/N$. The **analysis prior** is $\tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, 10^2)$ — a deliberately weak prior of $\pm 10$ points. Take $N = 60$ and suppose the observed estimate is $\hat\tau = 5.0$ points. The posterior is $\tau \mid \hat\tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(4.69,\ 2.5^2)$, with 95% interval $[-0.21,\ 9.59]$ (width $9.80$).

Now ask the same posterior three different scientific questions:

| Question | Declaration rule | Result for this dataset |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Is the direction positive? | $P(\tau > 0 \mid y) \ge 0.95$ | $P(\tau>0 \mid y) \approx 0.970$ → **declared** |
| Is the effect above 2 points (our $\delta_{\min}$)? | $P(\tau > 2 \mid y) \ge 0.95$ | $P(\tau>2 \mid y) \approx 0.859$ → **not declared** |
| Is the estimate precise enough? | 95% interval width $\le 4$ points | width $\approx 9.80$ → **failed** |

The same data succeeds, fails, and fails — depending on the scientific question. Calling the dataset simply "successful" hides which question was asked.

**Now look forward, before the data arrive.** The rule $P(\tau > 2 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ translates into an observable threshold: the study declares "meaningful" precisely when $\hat\tau \ge 6.52$ points. If the true effect is $5$, future estimates scatter around 5 with SD $2.58$, so only about **27.8%** of future datasets will clear the bar. That is compatible with demanding 95% posterior probability *within* each successful dataset — the two percentages answer different questions, exactly as the $\gamma$ vs. $q$ box promised.

::::{dropdown} 🔢 Optional math: the conjugate update behind Example A
With prior $\operatorname{Normal}(m_0, s_0^2)$ and sampling variance $v_N = 400/N$ (known $\sigma = 10$):

$$
s_N^2 = \left(s_0^{-2} + v_N^{-1}\right)^{-1}, \qquad
\kappa_N = \frac{s_N^2}{v_N}, \qquad
m_N = (1 - \kappa_N)\, m_0 + \kappa_N\, \hat\tau .
$$

At $m_0 = 0$, $s_0 = 10$, $N = 60$: $v_N = 6.667$, $s_N^2 = 6.25$, $\kappa_N = 0.9375$, so the posterior is $\operatorname{Normal}(4.6875, 2.5^2)$ with 95% interval $[-0.21, 9.59]$. The directional probability is $1 - \Phi(-4.6875/2.5) = 0.970$; the exceedance probability is $1 - \Phi((2 - 4.6875)/2.5) = 0.859$.

The declaration rule $P(\tau > \delta \mid Y) \ge \gamma$ is equivalent to an observable cutoff on the estimate:

$$
\hat\tau \ \ge\ c_N = \frac{\delta + z_\gamma\, s_N - (1 - \kappa_N)\, m_0}{\kappa_N},
$$

with $z_{0.95} = 1.645$. At $\delta = 2$, $\gamma = 0.95$: $c_{60} = 6.520$. Future datasets have $\hat\tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(5, v_{60})$ under truth 5, giving success probability $1 - \Phi((6.520 - 5)/2.582) = 0.278$.
::::

::::{dropdown} 🔭 Advanced: directional probability is not model evidence (Example F)
An observed contrast $\hat\tau = 2$ with known SE $= 1$, testing $H_0: \tau = 0$ against $H_1: \tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, s_1^2)$:

$$
BF_{10} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{1 + s_1^2}} \exp\!\left[ \frac{\hat\tau^2}{2}\left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + s_1^2}\right) \right].
$$

| Alternative-prior SD $s_1$ | $P(\tau > 0 \mid y, H_1)$ | $BF_{10}$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $2$ | $0.963$ | $2.22$ |
| $10$ | $0.977$ | $0.72$ |

Strong directional probability **conditional on the continuous alternative** is compatible with weak evidence, or even evidence slightly favoring the point null: the broad alternative spreads predictive probability over many effects these data do not support. Within-model probability and between-model evidence answer different questions {cite}`schonbrodt2018bayes`.
::::

### Cookbook: choosing your declaration rule

| If your scientific question is… | Prespecify this rule | Report per scenario |
|:---|:---|:---|
| "Is the effect in the predicted direction?" | $P(\tau > 0 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ | success rate; wrong-direction rate among declared |
| "Is it big enough to matter?" (define $\delta_{\min}$ first, in outcome units) | $P(\tau > \delta_{\min} \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ | success at each truth incl. truth $= \delta_{\min}$ and truth $= 0$ |
| "Is it practically negligible?" | 95% interval entirely inside ROPE | equivalence rate; inconclusive rate |
| "How precise will the estimate be?" | width $\le w^*$ | expected width and its spread vs. $N$ |
| "Which mechanism/model?" | BF threshold both directions | strong-correct / strong-misleading / inconclusive counts |
| "What action should we take?" | maximize posterior expected utility | $\bar U(d)$ per candidate design |

**Before any threshold:** define the smallest scientifically meaningful effect or the practical-equivalence region **in the outcome's units** (points, ms, proportion). A high probability that an effect is positive does not establish that it is large enough to matter.

## 3. Fixed Scenarios and Bayesian Assurance

### The Scenario Grid

Rather than computing power at a single optimistic guess, evaluate candidate sample sizes across a grid of plausible scenarios:

* **The null / negligible scenario**: evaluates false-declaration rate and inconclusive decisions.
* **The smallest meaningful effect ($\delta_{\min}$)**: the boundary case — see the warning in Section 2.
* **The central empirical scenario**: best external estimate from meta-analyses or previous literature.
* **The adverse scenario**: high measurement error, low compliance, severe attrition.

Add uncertainty about **nuisance quantities** to the grid, not just the effect: residual variance, participant and item heterogeneity, dependence across trials, attrition, and missing responses. A design that survives plausible variation in these quantities is far more informative than a single sample-size answer conditional on favorable values. If retention is uncertain, simulate it — dividing required completers by expected retention controls an *expectation*, not a high-probability completion target.

### Where do the planning numbers come from?

**Priors need scientific interpretation, not defaults.** $\operatorname{Normal}(0, 1)$ is not automatically "weakly informative": on the raw exam-point scale of our example it concentrates most mass within about $\pm 2$ points — while the generating effect is 5 points, so this "default" is actually a skeptical prior that fights the data. Always state the units, the plausible magnitudes, and the reason for each planning prior.

**Pilot data: useful, but handle with care.**

* A noisy pilot *point estimate* is not automatically exaggerated — the dramatic Type M inflation arises from **selection**, e.g. when a significant-looking pilot result is the one that gets attention (Section 7). Even without selection, pilot uncertainty itself matters.
* Propagate that uncertainty through a **design distribution** rather than plugging in one number; a scenario grid over plausible values is an equally legitimate way to explore it.
* Ask whether historical evidence could be *optimistic* (publication selection, population differences) and whether the pilot is independent of the final data. If pilot data will also enter the final likelihood, do not count them a second time through the analysis prior — using them for planning is different from using them as evidence.

### Design prior vs. analysis prior

$$
A(d) = \int S(d;\theta)\, p_D(\theta)\, d\theta = \Pr_{\theta \sim p_D,\; Y \sim p(Y \mid \theta, d)}(\text{success}),
$$

**Assurance** {cite}`ohagan2005assurance` averages the success probability over a **design prior** $p_D(\theta)$ describing planning uncertainty. Two priors with distinct responsibilities:

```{important}
**Design prior vs. Analysis Prior:**
* The **design prior** $p_D(\theta)$ generates plausible true parameter values *during planning* (e.g. synthesizing historical pilot data).
* The **analysis prior** $p_A(\theta)$ is used *inside the Bayesian model* to compute the posterior for each synthetic or real dataset (e.g. a weakly informative regularizer).

They need not be identical, and usually should not be: planning may honestly believe "the effect is somewhere around 3 points, give or take 2" while the analysis still regularizes with a wide symmetric prior. The design prior may legitimately be optimistic or pessimistic — that is what assurance averages over — but the analysis prior must be defensible to a skeptic, because it shapes the conclusions.
```

### Example B: Conditional Success vs. Assurance — and an Unattainable Target

Keep Example A's analysis prior, and suppose planning uncertainty is described by $p_D(\tau) = \operatorname{Normal}(3, 2^2)$ points (an illustration, not a literature-derived estimate). "Meaningful" means declaring $\tau > 2$ with posterior probability $\ge 0.95$; "positive" means declaring $\tau > 0$:

| Total $N$ | Positive declaration, truth $= 5$ | Meaningful declaration, truth $= 5$ | **Assurance** of meaningful declaration (truth $\sim N(3,2^2)$) | 95% posterior width |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $60$ | $59.4\%$ | $27.8\%$ | $14.1\%$ | $9.80$ |
| $100$ | $79.5\%$ | $41.4\%$ | $19.5\%$ | $7.69$ |
| $250$ | $98.9\%$ | $75.4\%$ | $31.7\%$ | $4.92$ |
| $400$ | $99.96\%$ | $90.8\%$ | $38.2\%$ | $3.90$ |
| $1{,}000$ | $\approx 100\%$ | $99.9\%$ | $49.0\%$ | $2.47$ |

Two honest answers for the same design: at $N = 400$ the study meets a $90\%$ meaningful-declaration target **conditional on a five-point truth**, but its assurance under the less optimistic planning distribution is only $38.2\%$. Neither number is wrong — they average over different possibilities.

**Inspect the whole curve, including its limit.** Here assurance cannot exceed $P_D(\tau > 2) = \Phi(0.5) \approx 0.69$ no matter how large $N$ grows, and the finite-$N$ values stay well below that. If a funding requirement demands $90\%$ assurance **under this design prior, no sample size achieves it** — increasing $N$ cannot turn a genuinely small effect into a meaningful one. The right responses are to renegotiate the target, revise the planning distribution with better external evidence, or change the design (e.g. a more reliable outcome measure). This limit statement assumes the regular, correctly specified model used here; do not read it as a universal finite-$N$ ceiling for every prior and procedure — but always look before you search for a minimum $N$.

::::{dropdown} 🔢 Optional math: assurance for the Normal benchmark
Under truth $\theta^*$, declaration requires $\hat\tau \ge c_N$ (Example A), and $\hat\tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\theta^*, v_N)$, so

$$
S_N(\theta^*) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{c_N - \theta^*}{\sqrt{v_N}}\right), \qquad
A_N = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{c_N - 3}{\sqrt{4 + v_N}}\right),
$$

the latter because $\hat\tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(3, 4 + v_N)$ marginally under $p_D$. As $N \to \infty$, $A_N \to P_D(\tau > 2)$.
::::

### Example C: The Boundary Truth and Practical Equivalence

**Boundary truth.** Keep $\delta = 2$ but generate data at truth $\tau^* = 2$ — exactly the effect we would love to establish exceeds 2. The chance that the rule $P(\tau > 2 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ fires is about **4.0% at $N = 60$** and **4.7% at $N = 400$**, approaching $5\%$ as $N$ grows. If instead you want to *detect* an effect of that size, evaluate $P(\tau > 0 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ — at truth 2 that succeeds ever more often with growing $N$. Different scientific claims, different designs.

**Equivalence.** Now take a null truth $\tau^* = 0$ and a ROPE of $[-2, 2]$ points. A practical-equivalence declaration (95% interval entirely inside the ROPE) requires $|m_N| + 1.96\, s_N \le 2$:

| Total $N$ | Posterior width | P(declare equivalence $\mid$ truth $= 0$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $400$ | $3.90$ | $4.0\%$ |
| $1{,}000$ | $2.47$ | $77.4\%$ |
| $1{,}600$ | $1.96$ | $95.9\%$ |

An interval barely narrower than the ROPE rarely fits entirely inside it unless the estimate lands almost exactly at zero — so *"the interval is narrower than four points"* and *"the study will establish practical equivalence"* are **not interchangeable planning goals**. Four hypothetical intervals under this ROPE:

| Observed 95% interval | Decision under the interval rule |
|:---:|:---|
| $[3, 6]$ | Meaningful positive effect |
| $[-6, -3]$ | Meaningful negative effect |
| $[-1, 1]$ | Practical equivalence |
| $[-1, 4]$ | Inconclusive (overlaps both the ROPE and beyond) |

Merely *including* zero establishes nothing about equivalence — the $[-1, 4]$ interval is compatible both with a negligible and with a moderate positive effect. A prespecified rule based on $P(|\tau| \le 2 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ is also defensible, but it need not reach the same verdict as the interval rule on every dataset; pick one before data collection and state it {cite}`kruschke2015doing`.

### Example D (sensitivity box): A Narrow Posterior Can Be Driven by the Wrong Prior

At $N = 60$, replace the analysis prior with a confident, optimistic one: $\operatorname{Normal}(5, 1^2)$. The posterior width collapses to **$3.66$ points** — the four-point precision target is now met *by construction, for every dataset* under this known-variance model. But if the actual truth is zero, the rule $P(\tau > 2 \mid Y) \ge 0.95$ still declares a meaningful positive effect in about **99.2%** of hypothetical datasets.

This is not a computational failure. It is what happens when an analysis prior assigns very little plausibility to the generating scenario and dominates the small sample. Three lessons:

1. A precision target alone does not establish that the study *learns from new data*.
2. Compare a small set of scientifically justified priors across the same scenarios; report precision, false declarations, and prior sensitivity **together**.
3. Under fixed truths, posterior probability thresholds do not automatically deliver their nominal frequentist error rates — the declaration rates in your simulation are the honest summary.

One further observation that will save you confusion when reading simulation output: in all of Examples A–D the posterior variance depends only on $N$ and the prior, **not on the realized estimate** (known-variance Normal model). The probability of meeting a *width* target is therefore exactly 0 or 1 for each fixed $N$. Smooth precision-attainment curves in practice come from additional uncertainty — unknown residual variance, random allocation, missingness, hierarchy — which is exactly what a simulation can include and a one-line formula cannot.

## 4. What Does "Sufficient Sample Size" Mean?

There is no context-free universal sample size. A sample size is sufficient only with respect to a declared estimand, design constraints, and operating targets — and each constraint must state **which probability regime** it lives in:

$$
d^* = \arg\min_{d \in \mathcal{D}} \operatorname{Cost}(d) \quad \text{subject to} \quad
\begin{cases}
\Pr(\text{interval width} \le w^*) \ge q_w, \\[2pt]
\Pr(\text{wrong direction} \mid \text{declared}) \le q_S, \\[2pt]
\Pr(\text{success} \mid \theta_s, d) \ge q_s \quad \text{for required scenarios}, \\[2pt]
\Pr(\text{sampler failure}) \le q_c.
\end{cases}
$$

* The width constraint is a property of the analysis model and $N$ (exactly 0-or-1 per $N$ in the known-variance benchmark; more nuanced with estimated variance).
* The wrong-direction constraint should state whether it is **conditional on making a declaration** (a Type S rate, Section 7) or unconditional.
* The scenario constraint is conditional on named truths $\theta_s$; averaging it over a design prior turns it into an assurance target (Example B).
* At truth $\tau^* = 0$ the Type M ratio is undefined (no true direction exists) — report the **false-declaration rate** instead, and near zero prefer absolute error or decision loss over an unstable ratio.

If resources impose an absolute sample cap (e.g. $N \le 80$ clinical patients), reverse the question: **report the precision and effect sizes the feasible sample can reliably distinguish**, rather than claiming it is "powered."

## 5. The Design-Analysis Workflow (Cookbook Edition)

The workflow below is a record you can fill in for your own study — each step produces one line of the reporting template in Section 9:

1. **Scientific claim and scale.** Define the contrast, population, direction, and practical threshold. Distinguish raw points, standardized effects, milliseconds, and probabilities — a "$\delta = 2$" means nothing until its units are named.
2. **Generating scenarios.** Specify full effect *and nuisance* values, or a joint design distribution. Include null, boundary ($\tau = \delta$), plausible beneficial, and adverse scenarios. Distinguish effect heterogeneity from uncertainty about its population mean.
3. **Candidate designs.** State recruited vs. analyzed participants, trials, items, allocation, and cost. Simulate attrition if it is expected.
4. **Analysis and declarations.** Freeze the likelihood, priors, estimators, exclusion handling, and **all declaration categories**. If the real study will select among outcomes or stop early, simulate *that* process — fixed-$N$ operating characteristics do not describe a different stopping rule.
5. **Generate and analyze.** Use direct conjugate calculations where they suffice (this chapter's examples); switch to posterior computation only when needed. Analyze the same kind of data the real study will produce.
6. **Record all outcomes.** Keep scientific decisions, uncertainty summaries, *and numerical failures*. If $k$ replications succeed and $F$ fits remain unresolved among $M$ datasets, report the empirical bounds $[k/M,\ (k+F)/M]$ instead of silently dropping failures — or define the operational event "valid successful analysis" and count unresolved fits as failures.
7. **Quantify the two simulation errors.** *Outer* Monte Carlo error across future datasets, and *inner* numerical error in each posterior summary. A posterior probability estimated near the 0.95 boundary needs enough precision to classify it reliably — and ESS for a posterior mean is not automatically ESS for the tail indicator your rule actually uses.
8. **Compare and confirm.** Inspect whole performance curves and trade-offs; re-run finalists with larger $M$ and stricter diagnostics. If no feasible design meets the criteria, **say so** and revise the scope openly.

Two worked arithmetic checks to calibrate intuition:

* A simulated success estimate of $0.90$ from $1{,}000$ independent replications has MCSE $\approx 0.0095$ — an approximate 95% uncertainty range of $0.881$–$0.919$. A point estimate barely above $0.90$ is *not* strong evidence the design meets a $0.90$ target; confirm finalists with more replications and a prespecified decision rule.
* For an independent Bernoulli success proportion at $M = 1{,}000$, the largest possible MCSE is $\sqrt{0.25/1000} \approx 0.0158$ — so "MCSE $\le 0.016$" is the honest universal bound. That bound applies to a success *proportion*; conditional sign-error rates and mean Type M ratios have their own, usually much larger, Monte Carlo errors (Section 7).

**Include a misspecification scenario.** Same-model recovery evaluates internal performance only. Add at least one scenario where the generator and the analysis likelihood deliberately differ — heavier tails, unequal variance, unmodeled dependence — to see how the design behaves under plausible departures.

## 6. Worked Case: The Educational Intervention (*FakeMidtermFinal*)

Following Gelman, Hill, and Vehtari (*Regression and Other Stories*, Chapter 16), consider evaluating an educational coaching program. Students take a midterm exam ($x_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(60, 20^2)$), half are randomly assigned to coaching ($T_i \in \{0, 1\}$), and final exam scores follow:

$$
y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \tau T_i + \epsilon_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma^2 = 100).
$$

**Complete assumptions for what follows:** $N$ is the *total* number of students across both arms; $\tau$ is the average coaching effect for the student population being sampled; the residual SD is known ($\sigma = 10$) in the benchmark calculations; the analysis prior is $\tau \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, 10^2)$ where a prior is used, and the flat-prior results are labelled as such. The true effect in the scenario grid is $\tau = 5.0$ points ($0.5$ residual SD).

The standard error of $\hat\tau$ in the **ideal** balanced design with the covariate exactly orthogonal to treatment is the familiar benchmark:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\hat\tau) = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{N}}.
$$

Randomization makes treatment *approximately* orthogonal to the realized baseline covariate on average — it does not guarantee exact orthogonality in your one realized dataset, so treat $20/\sqrt{N}$ as a transparent benchmark, not a promise (the dropdown gives the exact conditional expression).

**Corrected benchmark numbers.** With known $\sigma = 10$ and a flat analysis prior, a 95% interval for $\tau$ centered at an estimate of 5 has:

| Total $N$ | SE (ideal) | 95% interval at $\hat\tau = 5$ | Width |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $60$ | $2.582$ | $[-0.06,\ 10.06]$ | $10.12$ |
| $386$ | $1.018$ | $[3.00,\ 7.00]$ | $3.99$ |
| $400$ | $1.000$ | $[3.04,\ 6.96]$ | $3.92$ |

* At $N = 60$ the 95% interval spans $[-0.06, 10.06]$ — the effect's *magnitude* is essentially unresolved even though the direction is nearly settled.
* Requiring width $\le 4$ points demands a continuous $N \ge 384.2$; the smallest **even** total for exact two-arm balance is $N = 386$, and $N = 400$ is a sensible rounded candidate — not "the" mathematical minimum.
* Under the Example A prior $\operatorname{Normal}(0, 10^2)$ instead of the flat prior, the $N = 60$ interval shifts to $[-0.21, 9.59]$ (width $9.80$) — prior choice moves small-sample intervals, which is precisely why Section 3 asks you to justify it.

::::{dropdown} 🔢 Optional math: the exact conditional standard error
For ordinary Gaussian regression conditional on the realized full-rank design matrix,

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\hat\tau \mid X) = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{N}\,\sqrt{1 - R^2_{T \sim x}}},

where $R^2_{T \sim x}$ is the realized linear association between treatment and the baseline covariate (including an intercept). The benchmark $20/\sqrt{N}$ assumes this association is exactly zero; randomization makes it small on average, so the benchmark is an excellent first calculation and a slightly optimistic one for any single realized dataset. A posterior interval additionally depends on the analysis prior and on whether $\sigma$ is known or estimated — state both before quoting interval numbers.

## 7. Beyond Power: Type S (Sign) and Type M (Magnitude) Selection Risks

When a study has low precision ($N$ small, residual variance large), filtering findings by statistical significance — here, the two-sided Normal rule $|\hat\tau| \ge 1.96 \times \operatorname{SE}$ — introduces selection distortions {cite}`gelman2014beyond`:

1. **Type S (sign) error**: among *selected* (significant) estimates, the fraction with the **wrong sign**:
   $$\text{Type S} \equiv \Pr\left(\operatorname{sgn}(\hat\tau) \neq \operatorname{sgn}(\tau) \;\middle|\; \text{selected}\right).$$
2. **Type M (magnitude) error**: among selected estimates, the expected factor by which $|\hat\tau|$ **overestimates** the true magnitude:
   $$\text{Type M} \equiv \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{|\hat\tau|}{|\tau|} \;\middle|\; \text{selected}\right].$$

**Corrected values for the chapter's benchmark** ($\tau = 5$, $\operatorname{SE} = 20/\sqrt{N}$, two-sided 1.96 selection):

| Total $N$ | Selection probability ("power") | Conditional Type S | Conditional Type M |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $40$ | $35.3\%$ | $0.057\%$ | $1.67\times$ |
| $250$ | $97.7\%$ | $\approx 2 \times 10^{-9}$ | $1.014\times$ |

The small-$N$ risk in this scenario is **magnitude exaggeration**, not sign errors: at $N = 40$ a selected estimate overstates a five-point effect by two-thirds on average, while wrong-sign findings are rare (about 6 in 10,000 selected results). Sign errors become substantial when the *true signal-to-noise ratio* is small — the next table shows how.

### Example E: One Design, Several Truths

Fix $N = 40$ ($\operatorname{SE} = 3.16$) and vary the truth, still under the two-sided significance filter:

| True effect $\tau$ | Selection probability | Wrong sign among selected | Expected magnitude ratio among selected |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $1$ point | $6.2\%$ | $18.6\%$ | $7.48\times$ |
| $2$ points | $9.7\%$ | $4.9\%$ | $3.82\times$ |
| $5$ points | $35.3\%$ | $0.06\%$ | $1.67\times$ |

**The mechanism:** significance requires a large estimate relative to the SE. When the truth is small, most estimates clearing that bar are unusually large *for that truth* — so the filter manufactures exaggeration, and at sufficiently low signal-to-noise ratios it also manufactures wrong signs. A "significant" finding from an underpowered study of a small effect is selected precisely for being extreme.

### Reading Type S/M numbers honestly

* **Rule-specific.** These are calculations under the stated two-sided Normal selection rule. A Bayesian declaration rule (Section 2) has its own operating characteristics — simulate *your* rule, don't import these numbers.
* **Estimator-specific.** The formulas describe an unregularized sampling estimator; a shrunk posterior mean behaves differently under the same filter.
* **Type M is an expected absolute-magnitude ratio** — not the share of selected estimates that overestimate. "Negligible" exaggeration needs a declared scientific tolerance, not an adjective.
* **The denominator is the number of selected replications.** At $\tau = 5$, $N = 40$, 1,000 replications produce only $\approx 353$ selected results and about $0.2$ wrong-sign selections *in expectation* — such a small count cannot estimate the tiny rate precisely. Zero observed errors do not establish zero risk.
* **At $\tau = 0$** the true sign does not exist: report the false-declaration rate instead.

::::{dropdown} 🔢 Optional math: analytic Type S/M and the Monte Carlo budget
With $\lambda = \tau/\operatorname{SE} > 0$, $z = 1.96$, $A = 1 - \Phi(z - \lambda)$, $B = \Phi(-z - \lambda)$:

$$
\text{Selection probability} = A + B, \qquad
\text{Type S} = \frac{B}{A + B}, \qquad
\text{Type M} = \frac{\lambda(A - B) + \phi(z - \lambda) + \phi(z + \lambda)}{\lambda (A + B)},
$$

with $\Phi, \phi$ the standard Normal CDF and density. The two density terms are why the magnitude ratio is not simply the mean of positive selected estimates.

**How many replications for a tiny rate?** At $\tau = 5$, $N = 40$: selection probability $r = 0.3526$, conditional wrong-sign rate $p_S = 0.000565$, so the expected wrong-sign selections are $M \cdot r \cdot p_S$:

| Replications $M$ | Expected wrong-sign selections | P(zero observed) | Relative MCSE of $\hat p_S$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $1{,}000$ | $0.20$ | $\approx 82\%$ | $224\%$ |
| $10{,}000$ | $1.99$ | $\approx 13.6\%$ | $71\%$ |
| $\approx 502{,}000$ | $\approx 100$ | — | $10\%$ |

$$
\frac{\operatorname{MCSE}(\hat p_S)}{p_S} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1 - p_S}{M\, r\, p_S}} .
$$

Choose $M$ from the precision you need for the *quantity you report*, not from a fixed default — and prefer analytical integration when the benchmark allows it. Distinguishing "estimating a tiny rate precisely" from "showing it lies below a looser risk tolerance" can save a 500-fold simulation budget: the latter often needs far fewer replications.
::::

## 8. Interactions Require Explicit Design Scenarios

A persistent fallacy in research design is assuming that a sample size powered for a main effect is sufficient to detect an interaction.

### The Balanced Gaussian $2 \times 2$ Factorial Design

Consider a $2 \times 2$ factorial experiment with cell means $\mu_{00}, \mu_{01}, \mu_{10}, \mu_{11}$, total sample size $N$ ($N/4$ observations per cell), independent homoscedastic errors with variance $\sigma^2$. Define the contrasts explicitly:

$$
\underbrace{\Delta_A = \frac{\mu_{10} + \mu_{11}}{2} - \frac{\mu_{00} + \mu_{01}}{2}}_{\text{marginal main effect of A}}, \qquad
\underbrace{\Delta_{AB} = (\mu_{11} - \mu_{10}) - (\mu_{01} - \mu_{00})}_{\text{interaction (difference of differences)}}.
$$

* The standard error of the marginal main contrast (comparing two margins of $N/2$ units):
  $$\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\Delta}_A) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{N/2} + \frac{\sigma^2}{N/2}} = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}.$$
* The standard error of the interaction contrast (summing variances across all four cells of $N/4$):
  $$\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\Delta}_{AB}) = \sqrt{4 \times \frac{\sigma^2}{N/4}} = \frac{4\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} = 2 \times \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\Delta}_A).$$

Because the standard error is **doubled**, matching the precision of a main effect requires **four times ($4\times$) the sample size**; and if the true interaction is half the main effect, matching its *signal-to-noise* ratio requires **sixteen times ($16\times$)** the observations.

:::{warning} Coding matters more than vocabulary
In $0/1$ treatment coding *with an interaction term*, the regression coefficient of A is the **simple effect of A at B = 0**, not the marginal contrast $\Delta_A$ above. Rescaling a coefficient changes its numerical magnitude without changing the underlying scientific contrast — the $4\times$ rule is about *named contrasts and their noise*, not about the word "interaction." Define your contrast, then compute its standard error.
:::

### Interactions in Generalized Linear Models: Declare Your Scale

For binary and count outcomes, interaction design depends critically on **which scale the scientific hypothesis lives on**. There is no universal sample-size multiplier — the honest answer is that the two scales can disagree about whether an interaction exists at all:

**Example G: zero interaction on the logit scale, real interaction on the probability scale.** Suppose independent binary responses satisfy

$$
\operatorname{logit}(p_{ab}) = -2 + a + b, \qquad a, b \in \{0, 1\},
$$

so the logit-scale interaction coefficient is exactly zero. The cell probabilities are nevertheless:

| Cell $(A, B)$ | Probability |
|:---:|:---:|
| $(0,0)$ | $0.119$ |
| $(0,1)$ | $0.269$ |
| $(1,0)$ | $0.269$ |
| $(1,1)$ | $0.500$ |

The effect of A on the probability scale is $0.150$ at $B = 0$ but $0.231$ at $B = 1$ — a probability-scale difference-in-differences of **$0.081$** despite zero logit interaction.

**Practical guidance.**

1. Ask whether the scientific hypothesis concerns additivity of *log odds* or additivity of *response probabilities*. Those are different hypotheses with different design requirements.
2. For a probability-scale target, compute the contrast for each joint posterior draw and apply your declaration rule to *that* distribution; with repeated participants or items, state whether the contrast is conditional on particular units or averaged over a target population.
3. Information about a probability is $I(p) = 1/[p(1-p)]$, while information about the logit is $I(\eta) = p(1-p)$ — rare outcomes make *log-odds* quantities hard to estimate, but that does not translate into a universal multiplier for absolute probability contrasts. Vary baseline probabilities and meaningful contrasts in a simulation while holding the declared goal fixed; boundary probabilities make Normal approximations poor, which is exactly why simulation-based design is preferred for GLMs.

```{admonition} Teaching Rule
:class: important
The $4\times$ and $16\times$ figures are worked consequences of the balanced, independent, homoscedastic Gaussian four-cell design and the *named contrasts above* — not immutable laws, and not transferable to GLMs as a single multiplier. Sample-size requirements depend on balance, predictor distributions, base rates, outcome scale, and hierarchy. Always simulate the specific scientific scenario forward.
```

### Same Number of Trials, Different Designs (bridge to sample allocation)

**Example H.** A balanced *within-person* experiment with $K$ trials per condition: suppose the population SD of the person-level condition effect is $10$ ms, trial residual SD is $20$ ms, the true benefit is $5$ ms, and the meaningful threshold is $2$ ms. The standard error of the mean within-person contrast is $\sqrt{100/J + 800/(JK)}$, and with a flat-prior directional benchmark:

| Participants $J$ | Trials per condition $K$ | Total trials $2JK$ | SE of contrast | P(declare $\tau > 2$ with $95\%$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $80$ | $20$ | $3{,}200$ | $1.32$ ms | $73.3\%$ |
| $40$ | $40$ | $3{,}200$ | $1.73$ ms | $53.5\%$ |

Identical total data volume, a $20$-percentage-point difference in design success — because precision depends on the **allocation**, not on the total. (These hypothetical numbers illustrate the allocation principle, not a recommended reaction-time effect.) The next subchapter develops this arithmetic and its assumptions:

## 9. Compact Reporting Template for Design Analyses

When presenting a Bayesian design analysis in a manuscript or grant proposal, fill in this structured template:

> **1. Estimand:** We target [contrast/parameter] on the [declared scale, with units] in [target population]. The smallest scientifically meaningful effect is [$\delta_{\min}$, justified].
> **2. Declaration rule:** Success for one dataset is defined as [exact posterior statement and threshold $\gamma$]; all other outcomes are recorded as [categories incl. inconclusive].
> **3. Candidate Designs:** We evaluated candidate sample sizes / allocations of [list], with [recruited vs. analyzed, retention] assumptions.
> **4. Generating Scenarios:** Scenarios were based on [external evidence] and varied over [effects, variances, nuisance quantities incl. attrition]; the design distribution for assurance was [distribution, source].
> **5. Analysis Procedure:** Each synthetic dataset was analyzed using [likelihood, priors, posterior summary]; deviations from the planned analysis (stopping rules, exclusions) were [simulated / not applicable].
> **6. Success Criteria & Risks:** Targets were [precision / declaration probability per scenario / assurance], guarded by [wrong-direction rate $\le q_S$, magnitude ratio $\le q_M$, false-declaration rate at null].
> **7. Simulation:** We ran $M = $ [value] replications per design–scenario combination (success-proportion MCSE $\le 0.016$ at $M = 1{,}000$; conditional-error rates reported with their own MCSE); unresolved fits were handled by [policy; empirical bounds reported].
> **8. Decision:** We selected [design] because it achieved [targets] across required scenarios; no feasible design met [criterion], which we address by [revision of scope/design].

## 10. Common Mistakes in Power & Design Planning

1. **Claiming Bayesians "don't need power calculations"**: Bayesian inference does not rescue an underpowered study from wide, uninformative posteriors or prior-dominated conclusions.
2. **Treating pilot point estimates as known truth**: propagate pilot uncertainty through a design prior or scenario grid. Expect strong exaggeration only when significant-looking pilot results have been *selected*; either way, don't reuse pilot data as both planning input and analysis prior.
3. **Leaving the declaration rule unspecified**: "detecting an effect of size $\delta$", "establishing the effect exceeds $\delta$", and "estimating to precision $w^*$" lead to different designs (Examples A–C). Write the rule down before choosing $N$.
4. **Powering for a main effect and assuming the interaction is covered**: factorial interaction contrasts require explicitly planned sample sizes on the declared scale (Section 8).
5. **Ignoring clustering or repeated measures**: treating repeated trials as independent observations inflates the apparent sample size and produces severely overconfident designs (next subchapter).
6. **Confusing MCMC effective sample size (ESS) with the empirical sample size ($N$)**: ESS measures sampler efficiency; $N$ determines the information about the population. And the ESS of a posterior *mean* is not automatically the ESS of the tail probability your declaration rule uses.
