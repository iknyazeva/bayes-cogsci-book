## 1. Why Straight Lines Break Down for Binary Choices

In Lessons 7–9, our outcome was continuous: test scores in KidIQ or reaction times in the sleep experiment. But in cognitive science, psychology, and public health, outcomes are often **discrete decisions**:
- Did a person remember a studied word or forget it? ($y \in \{0, 1\}$)
- Did a consumer accept or reject an offer? ($y \in \{0, 1\}$)
- Did a rural household in Bangladesh switch away from a contaminated drinking well? ($y \in \{0, 1\}$)

If we try to fit a standard linear regression line ($y_i = \alpha + \beta x_i + \epsilon_i$) to binary data, our model breaks down in two fatal ways:
1. **Impossible Probabilities**: A straight line continues forever in both directions. For large or small values of $x$, it predicts probabilities below $0$ (e.g., $-0.3$) or above $1$ (e.g., $1.4$), which are mathematically nonsensical.
2. **Invalid Variance Assumptions**: For binary events, the variance is inherently linked to the probability: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = p(1 - p)$. It is impossible for error variance to be a constant $\sigma^2$ across different values of $x$.

```
           Probability
             1.0 |                   •••••••••••• (y = 1)
                 |                 /
                 |               /   <-- Straight line predicts > 1!
             0.5 |             /
                 |           /       <-- Straight line predicts < 0!
             0.0 | ••••••••/                     (y = 0)
                 +------------------------------> Covariate x
```

To model binary choices, we need a mathematical curve that bends smoothly, keeping all predictions strictly bounded inside the valid probability interval $[0, 1]$.

---

## 2. The Logistic Link: S-Curves and Probabilities

Nelder and Wedderburn (1972) resolved this by introducing the **Generalized Linear Model (GLM)**.

Instead of predicting the binary outcome directly, we build a model in two steps:
1. **The Linear Predictor ($\eta_i$)**: We combine our predictors linearly on an unbounded scale:
   $$
   \eta_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki} \in (-\infty, \infty)
   $$
2. **The Inverse Link Function ($g^{-1}$)**: We pass $\eta_i$ through the **logistic sigmoid function** to compress it into a valid probability $p_i \in (0, 1)$:
   $$
   p_i = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta_i) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta_i}}
   $$
3. **The Likelihood**: Each observed choice is drawn from a **Bernoulli distribution**:
   $$
   y_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i)
   $$

```
   Predictors x ----> Linear Predictor η ----> Inverse Logit ----> Probability p ----> Outcome y
                       (α + β₁x₁ + ...)          1 / (1 + e⁻η)      p ∈ (0, 1)          y ∈ {0, 1}
```

## 3. Interpreting Logistic Coefficients on the Observable Scale

The coefficient $\beta$ lives on the **unbounded log-odds scale** ($\eta$). Telling a stakeholder that *"the slope is $\beta = 0.8$ log-odds"* is opaque. How do we translate this into real-world probability changes?

### The Divide-by-4 Rule (Instant Mental Math)
The logistic S-curve is steepest in the middle (at $p = 0.5$), where its slope is exactly $1/4$. Therefore:

$$
\Delta p \le \frac{\beta}{4} \quad \text{(Maximum possible change in probability)}
$$

If $\beta = 0.8$, a 1-unit increase in $x$ changes the probability by **at most** $\frac{0.8}{4} = 0.20$ ($20$ percentage points).

### The Golden Rule: Probabilities Depend on the Baseline
Because the S-curve flattens out near 0 and 1, **an identical coefficient produces different probability changes depending on where you start!**
- If baseline probability is $p = 0.50$, a shift of $\beta = 0.8$ moves probability to $\approx 69\%$ ($\Delta p \approx +19$ percentage points).
- If baseline probability is already $p = 0.95$, the exact same shift moves probability to $\approx 97.7\%$ ($\Delta p \approx +2.7$ percentage points).

**Always compute probability contrasts $\Delta p$ at explicit, meaningful baseline profiles!**

## 4. Empirical Case Study: Bangladesh Arsenic Wells

In Araihazar, Bangladesh (*ROS*, Ch. 13–14), researchers surveyed $N = 3{,}020$ rural households whose private drinking wells contained hazardous levels of arsenic ($> 0.5\, \mu\text{g/L}$).

- **Outcome ($y_i \in \{0, 1\}$)**: Did the household switch to a neighbor's safe well? ($1 = \text{Switched}, 0 = \text{Did not switch}$).
- **Predictors**:
  - $\text{dist100}_i$: Distance to the nearest known safe well (in hundreds of meters; mean $\approx 0.48$).
  - $\text{arsenic}_i$: Arsenic level in current well (in $\mu\text{g/L}$; mean $\approx 1.66$).

### Complete Separation and Bayesian Regularization
In classical logistic regression, if a covariate perfectly predicts success (e.g., every single household with $\text{arsenic} > 3.0$ switched), maximum likelihood optimization diverges to $\hat{\beta} = +\infty$ with standard error $\text{SE} = \infty$. 

In Bayesian GLMs, our scale-aware priors ($\operatorname{Normal}(0, 1.5)$) act as a natural regularizer, ensuring the posterior remains finite, realistic, and well-behaved.

## 5. The General GLM Recipe

Now that we understand binary logistic regression, we can see the grand design of Generalized Linear Models. Every GLM consists of three modular components:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                 THE TRIPARTITE GLM FRAMEWORK                                  |
+-------------------------------+-------------------------------+-------------------------------+
| 1. Linear Predictor (η)       | 2. Link Function g(·)         | 3. Likelihood Distribution    |
|                               |                               |                               |
| ηᵢ = α + β₁·x₁ᵢ + ... + βₖ·xₖᵢ| Connects mean μᵢ to ηᵢ:       | Describes the randomness      |
| Unconstrained line in ℝ       | ηᵢ = g(μᵢ) ⟺ μᵢ = g⁻¹(ηᵢ)     | of observed data yᵢ           |
+-------------------------------+-------------------------------+-------------------------------+
```

### The Taxonomy of Common GLM Families

| Outcome Domain ($\mathcal{Y}$) | Standard Family | Link $g(\mu)$ | Inverse Link $\mu = g^{-1}(\eta)$ | Substantive Application |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Binary $\{0, 1\}$** | **Bernoulli / Binomial** | Logit | $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta}}$ | Choices, accuracy, switching decisions |
| **Event Counts $\mathbb{N}_0$** | **Poisson / Neg-Binomial** | Log | $\exp(\eta)$ | Number of fixations, error counts, defects |
| **Ordered Ratings $\{1, \dots, K\}$** | **Ordered Logit** | Cumulative Logit | Threshold cutoffs | Survey Likert scales, confidence ratings |
| **Positive Durations $\mathbb{R}^+$** | **Exponential / Gamma** | Log | $\exp(\eta)$ | Response latencies, waiting times |

## 8. Concept Check

```{dropdown} Concept Check: The Odds Ratio vs. Absolute Risk
A medical study reports that a drug reduces the odds of an adverse event with an odds ratio of $\text{OR} = 0.50$ ($\beta = \log(0.50) \approx -0.69$). A journalist writes: "The drug cuts your risk of the disease in half!" Is this correct?

**Answer:** No. An odds ratio of 0.50 does not mean absolute risk is halved. If the baseline risk is $50\%$, the odds drop from $1.0$ to $0.5$, corresponding to a probability drop from $50\%$ to $33.3\%$ ($\Delta p = -16.7\%$). But if the baseline risk is already tiny ($1\%$), the probability drops from $1\%$ to approximately $0.5\%$ ($\Delta p = -0.5\%$). Odds ratios must always be converted to absolute probability differences at concrete baselines.
```

## Key Takeaways

- Binary outcomes require a **Bernoulli likelihood** and a **logistic sigmoid link** to keep predictions inside $[0, 1]$.
- Use the **divide-by-4 rule** ($\Delta p \le \beta / 4$) for rapid mental checks of maximum probability impact.
- Always translate findings into **absolute probability differences $\Delta p$** at explicit reference profiles.
- All GLMs share the same three-part structure: **Linear Predictor + Link Function + Exponential Likelihood**.
