> **Organizing Question:** When an outcome is a discrete choice (0 or 1), how do we build a regression that always produces valid probabilities, and how do we translate its coefficients into the familiar scale of percentages?

In Lessons 7–9 the outcome was continuous: test scores in KidIQ, reaction times in the sleep experiment. But in psychology and cognitive science the outcome is very often a discrete decision: remembered or forgot, accepted or rejected, pulled the left or the right lever. Throughout this lesson we work through one real example — switching away from arsenic-contaminated wells in Bangladesh — and follow the route from "why ordinary regression fails here" to a full Bayesian logistic model with interpretation on the probability scale.

## 1. The data: arsenic wells in Bangladesh

In the Araihazar district of Bangladesh (Gelman, Hill & Vehtari, *Regression and Other Stories*, Ch. 13–14) researchers surveyed $N = 3{,}020$ households whose wells contained hazardous arsenic levels. Each household was told its test result and shown the nearest safe well. Some time later, the researchers checked who had switched.

- **Outcome** $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$: whether the household switched wells (1) or kept using the old one (0). 57.5% switched.
- **Predictors**:
  - `dist100` — distance to the nearest safe well, in hundreds of meters (range 0 to 3.4, mean 0.48);
  - `arsenic` — arsenic level in the current well, in µg/L (range 0.51 to 9.65, mean 1.66).

Before fitting any model, look at the data — the share of switchers by decile of distance and of arsenic:

![The data: shares of switchers](figures/lesson10_logistic/wells_data.png)

Both relationships are visible at once: the farther the safe well, the fewer households switched; the more contaminated their own well, the more switched. Our task is to estimate these effects quantitatively, with uncertainty.

## 2. Why a straight line does not work

Try the familiar linear regression directly — predicting the binary outcome itself. This is called the linear probability model. Fitting it with both predictors gives

$$\widehat{P}(\text{switch}) = 0.515 - 0.20 \cdot \text{dist100} + 0.094 \cdot \text{arsenic}.$$

The model breaks literally: for 21 of the 3020 households it predicts "probabilities" above one (the maximum is 1.28, for households near a safe well with arsenic 9.65), and for one household — below zero. Probabilities outside $[0, 1]$ do not exist. A straight line extends forever in both directions while the valid range is finite — sooner or later the line must leave it.

![Linear model vs logistic](figures/lesson10_logistic/linear_vs_logistic.png)

There is also a second, less obvious problem: for a binary outcome the variance is tied to the probability, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = p(1-p)$ — largest at $p = 0.5$ and shrinking toward the edges. A linear regression with constant residual variance $\sigma^2$ is fundamentally wrong here.

What we need is a curve that stays inside $(0, 1)$ everywhere and approaches 0 and 1 smoothly rather than along a straight line.

## 3. The logistic model

The generalized linear model framework (Nelder & Wedderburn, 1972) builds the model in three steps.

**Step 1. The linear predictor** — exactly as in ordinary regression, unconstrained:

$$\eta_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki} \in (-\infty, \infty).$$

**Step 2. The inverse logistic link** squeezes $\eta_i$ into a probability:

$$p_i = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta_i) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta_i}} \in (0, 1).$$

**Step 3. The likelihood**: each observed response is Bernoulli with the resulting probability:

$$y_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i).$$

The interactive figure below shows, on the left, the S-curve itself: the same shift of $\eta$ by 0.8 produces a different probability gain depending on where we start (about 19 percentage points at $p = 0.5$, but only 2.7 pp at $p = 0.95$). On the right, why sensible priors matter: a "diffuse" $\operatorname{Normal}(0, 100)$ prior on the log-odds scale is actually extreme — it pushes 97.6% of prior mass toward probabilities near 0 or 1.

## 4. The Bayesian wells model in PyMC

We assemble the model with scale-aware priors. On the log-odds scale, $\operatorname{Normal}(0, 1.5)$ for the intercept says: before seeing the data we consider baseline probabilities between roughly 5% and 95% plausible, and $\operatorname{Normal}(0, 1)$ for the slopes says a predictor is unlikely to flip the probability from near 0 to near 1:

```python
import pymc as pm

with pm.Model() as wells_model:
    # 1. Priors on the log-odds scale
    alpha = pm.Normal("alpha", mu=0.0, sigma=1.5)
    beta_dist = pm.Normal("beta_dist", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0)
    beta_ars = pm.Normal("beta_ars", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0)

    # 2. Linear predictor
    eta = alpha + beta_dist * dist100 + beta_ars * arsenic

    # 3. Logistic link and Bernoulli likelihood
    p = pm.Deterministic("p", pm.math.invlogit(eta))
    pm.Bernoulli("switch_obs", p=p, observed=switch)

    idata = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, chains=4,
                      target_accept=0.95, random_seed=2026)
```

The posterior distributions of the coefficients (convergence is fine: `r_hat` = 1.00, ESS in the thousands):

| Parameter | Mean | 94% HDI | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\alpha$ | 0.00 | $[-0.15, 0.15]$ | log-odds of switching at dist = 0 and arsenic = 0 — a formal reference point at the edge of the data (arsenic below 0.5 never occurs) |
| $\beta_{\text{dist100}}$ | $-0.89$ | $[-1.08, -0.69]$ | each additional 100 m to the safe well lowers the log-odds of switching by 0.89 |
| $\beta_{\text{arsenic}}$ | $+0.46$ | $[0.38, 0.53]$ | each additional unit of arsenic raises the log-odds of switching by 0.46 |

![Posterior distributions of the coefficients](figures/lesson10_logistic/wells_posterior.png)

The signs match the picture in the data and the intervals are narrow — the effects are reliable. But so far the coefficients live on the log-odds scale, and nobody thinks on that scale. Let us translate.

## 5. Interpretation on the probability scale

### The divide-by-4 rule

The logistic curve is steepest in the middle, at $p = 0.5$, where its slope is exactly $1/4$. Hence an instant upper bound:

$$\Delta p \le \frac{\beta}{4}.$$

For $\beta_{\text{dist100}} = -0.89$: a hundred meters of travel means at most $0.89/4 \approx 22$ percentage points of decrease; for $\beta_{\text{arsenic}} = 0.46$ — at most $0.46/4 \approx 11$ pp of increase per unit of arsenic. The actual effect is almost always smaller, because the curve flattens toward the edges.

### The main rule: the effect depends on where you start

The same shift $\eta$ gives a different probability gain depending on the baseline. A shift of $\beta = 0.8$ raises a baseline of 0.50 to $\approx 69\%$ ($+19$ pp) but a baseline of 0.95 only to $\approx 97.7\%$ ($+2.7$ pp). That is why substantive conclusions are always computed at explicitly named conditions — as in the two figures below.

![Fitted probabilities](figures/lesson10_logistic/wells_curves.png)

On the left, the switching probability as a function of distance at three arsenic levels; on the right, as a function of arsenic at two distances. The bands are 94% posterior intervals: uncertainty grows toward the edges of the range, where data are sparser.

Now the quantitative answers to the substantive questions:

![Probability-scale contrasts](figures/lesson10_logistic/wells_contrasts.png)

- **Distance.** At the mean arsenic level (1.66), increasing the trip from 0 to 100 meters lowers the probability of switching by $0.21$ (94% HDI $[0.17, 0.26]$) — from 68% down to 47%.
- **Arsenic.** At the mean distance (48 m), raising contamination from 0.5 to 1.0 raises the switching probability by $0.06$ ($[0.05, 0.07]$) — a small effect, because baseline probabilities here are moderate, but an absolutely reliable one: the interval is far from zero.

Compare: "$\beta_{\text{dist100}} = -0.89$ log-odds" versus "+100 meters of travel means −21 percentage points of switching probability [17, 26]". Anyone can understand the second.

## 6. Finishing the analysis: interaction and model checking

### Does the distance effect depend on contamination?

So far we assumed the effects of distance and arsenic simply add up. Let us check that: add the product of the predictors to the model.

```python
with pm.Model() as wells_int_model:
    alpha = pm.Normal("alpha", mu=0.0, sigma=1.5)
    beta_dist = pm.Normal("beta_dist", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0)
    beta_ars = pm.Normal("beta_ars", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0)
    beta_int = pm.Normal("beta_int", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0)
    eta = alpha + beta_dist * dist100 + beta_ars * arsenic \
          + beta_int * dist100 * arsenic
    p = pm.Deterministic("p", pm.math.invlogit(eta))
    pm.Bernoulli("switch_obs", p=p, observed=switch)
    idata_int = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, chains=4,
                          target_accept=0.95, random_seed=2026)
```

The interaction coefficient: $\beta_{\text{int}} = -0.19$, 94% HDI $[-0.38, 0.00]$ — weak but substantively meaningful (ROS obtains the same value, −0.18). It is easiest to read through effects on the probability scale:

![Interaction](figures/lesson10_logistic/wells_interaction.png)

- at arsenic 0.5: +100 m of travel means −16 pp (from 53% to 37%);
- at the mean arsenic 1.66: −21 pp (from 68% to 48%);
- at arsenic 3.0: −22 pp (from 82% to 60%).

Put differently: heavy contamination motivates switching most when the safe well is nearby; if the trip is long, even strong contamination motivates less. The negative interaction says the two motives do not add up — they substitute for each other.

### Posterior predictive check

Generate new replicated datasets from the posterior and compare them with the real one:

![PPC](figures/lesson10_logistic/wells_ppc.png)

On the left, the total share of switchers: the observed value 0.575 sits exactly at the center of the predictive distribution $[0.553, 0.599]$. On the right, a check by distance decile: on average the model reproduces the decline, but at the very shortest distances it slightly overpredicts (0.63 versus the observed 0.56). This is an honest signal that the model does not capture everything: the full ROS analysis adds education and other predictors — adding them remains an exercise in the workbook.

## 7. Complete separation and Bayesian regularization

One typical trouble of logistic regression is complete separation: if some predictor separates the zeros and ones perfectly, maximum likelihood has no finite solution. On a small example where every point with $x > 0$ has $y = 1$ and every point with $x < 0$ has $y = 0$:

![Separation](figures/lesson10_logistic/separation.png)

The maximum likelihood estimate runs away (slope 35 with a giant standard error — a practical divergence): the data can be separated by any sufficiently steep line, and no "best" one exists. The Bayesian model with a $\operatorname{Normal}(0, 1)$ prior stays finite: slope $1.68$, 94% HDI $[0.59, 2.83]$. The prior does not forbid a strong effect — it just asks for data to support it. The wells data have no complete separation, but it does occur in sparse subgroups (few observations, extreme predictor values) — and sensible priors insure against it.

## 8. The general recipe: all GLMs are built the same way

Logistic regression is one instance of a general scheme. Every generalized linear model consists of three components: a linear predictor $\eta_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki}$; a link function $g$ connecting the mean response to the predictor ($\eta_i = g(\mu_i)$); and a likelihood distribution describing the randomness of the response. Change the support of the outcome — and the link and likelihood change:

| Outcome type | Family | Link | Inverse link | Application |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| binary $\{0, 1\}$ | Bernoulli / binomial | logit | $\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta}}$ | choices, accuracy, agreement |
| counts $\mathbb{N}_0$ | Poisson / negative binomial | log | $e^{\eta}$ | fixation counts, error counts |
| ordered $\{1, \dots, K\}$ | ordered logit | cumulative logit | thresholds | Likert scales |
| positive durations $\mathbb{R}^+$ | exponential / gamma | log | $e^{\eta}$ | response times, waiting times |

We will return to this table in the coming lessons; today its first row has been worked through completely.

## 10. Concept check

```{dropdown} Concept Check: The Odds Ratio vs. Absolute Risk
A medical study reports that a drug reduces the odds of an adverse event with an odds ratio of $\text{OR} = 0.50$ ($\beta = \log(0.50) \approx -0.69$). A journalist writes: "The drug cuts your risk of the disease in half!" Is this correct?

**Answer:** No. An odds ratio of 0.50 does not mean absolute risk is halved. If the baseline risk is 50%, the odds drop from 1.0 to 0.5, corresponding to a probability drop from 50% to 33.3% ($\Delta p = -16.7$ pp). But if the baseline risk is 1%, the probability drops from 1% to approximately 0.5% ($\Delta p = -0.5$ pp). Odds ratios must always be converted into absolute probability differences at a concrete baseline — exactly the rule we applied to the wells.
```

## Protocol milestone

1. **Outcome support and link**: state the nature of the response (binary, count, ordered) and justify the choice of link function.
2. **Prior check on the probability scale**: make sure the coefficient priors do not push probabilities toward 0 and 1 (figure above, right panel).
3. **Observable-scale reporting**: log-odds are never reported in isolation — only $\Delta p$ at named baseline conditions.
4. **Separation audit**: check sparse subgroups for complete separation.

## Key takeaways

- A binary outcome requires a Bernoulli likelihood and a logistic link that keeps predictions inside $(0, 1)$; the linear probability model produces impossible values already on the real wells data.
- The divide-by-4 rule ($\Delta p \le \beta/4$) is an instant upper bound on an effect; the actual effect is computed at explicitly named baselines: +100 m of travel is $-21$ pp $[17, 26]$, not "$-0.89$ log-odds".
- The analysis deserves to be finished: the interaction check showed the motives do not add up (the arsenic effect is stronger at short distances), and the posterior predictive check honestly showed where the model underexplains the data.
- Sensible priors insure against complete separation: the Bayesian estimate stays finite where the maximum likelihood estimate diverges.
- All GLMs are built the same way: linear predictor + link + likelihood; logistic regression is the first and central instance of the scheme.
