## Part A — Why is a posterior hard to compute?

### 1. Bayes' theorem gives a shape, not a size

Bayes' theorem says: multiply the prior by the likelihood, value by value. The result has the right **shape**, but it is not yet a probability distribution, because its total area is not 1. To make it a distribution we must divide by that total area. This area has several names: the **evidence**, the **marginal likelihood**, or the **normalising constant**.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_shape_not_size.html" width="100%" height="385px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice**

- The prior (grey) is wide; the likelihood (orange) is concentrated near 58%; their product (blue) has the posterior's shape.
- The blue bars add up to an area of about 0.014, not 1. We must divide by this number.

:::{dropdown} For the curious: the formula
$$
p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(y)}, \qquad p(y)=\int p(y\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta .
$$
The denominator $p(y)$ is the area under prior × likelihood. The integral sign $\int$ simply means "area under the curve".
:::

### 2. Every answer is an area

A distribution only becomes useful when we ask it questions. **Every** question is again an area under the posterior: the probability that support exceeds 50%, the interval that holds the middle 95%, even the average (the point where the area balances).

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_answers_are_areas.html" width="100%" height="455px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** what share of the area lies to the right of 50%?

**What to notice:** in Lesson 4 the Beta formula computed all of these areas for us. Without a formula we need another way to compute areas.

### 3. With one unknown, a grid is enough

With one unknown we can cut the horizontal axis into bars and add up their areas. A few dozen bars already give the exact answer.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_grid_area.html" width="100%" height="455px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

### 4. The grid explodes

Real models have many unknowns: group means, slopes, spreads, one parameter per participant. With 100 grid points per unknown, the number of cells is 100 × 100 × … : it is multiplied by 100 for every new unknown.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_grid_explosion.html" width="100%" height="795px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

| Unknowns | Grid cells | Time at one billion cells per second |
|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 100 | 0.1 microsecond |
| 2 | 10,000 | 10 microseconds |
| 5 | 10 billion | 10 seconds |
| 10 | 10²⁰ | about 3,200 years |
| 20 | 10⁴⁰ | about 10¹³ times the age of the universe |

This is called the **curse of dimensionality**.

### 5. Almost all of the box is empty

There is a second problem. The posterior occupies a small part of the space of possible values, and that part shrinks dramatically as the number of unknowns grows. Points placed blindly, on a grid or at random, almost never land where the posterior is.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_empty_box.html" width="100%" height="775px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** with 2 unknowns, 79% of random points are useful; with 10 unknowns, 0.25%; with 20 unknowns, 0.0000025%. A good method must **go where the probability is**.

## Part B — Monte Carlo: let randomness compute the area

### 6. Darts can compute an area

The idea that random experiments can compute a number is old. In **1777** Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon showed that the chance of a dropped needle crossing a line on the floor involves π {cite}`buffon1777essai`. Throw needles, or darts, many times, count, and you have an estimate of π.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_darts_pi.html" width="100%" height="475px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** with 10 darts, how close will the estimate be to 3.14? With 10,000?

**What to notice:** the estimate wobbles a lot at first and settles as darts accumulate. The share of darts inside the quarter circle *is* an area. This wobble, the **Monte Carlo error**, shrinks as more darts are thrown.

### 7. A pile of draws answers questions

If we could draw random values *from the posterior itself*, every question would become counting. You did exactly this in Lesson 4 with `rng.beta(60, 44, ...)`.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_pile_of_draws.html" width="100%" height="465px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** with 10 or 100 draws the pile is ragged and the counted share is off. With 10,000 draws the pile matches the curve and the share is 94.3%, the exact answer.

:::{important} The name "Monte Carlo"
In 1946 the mathematician **Stanislaw Ulam**, recovering from an illness, played solitaire and wondered about his chance of winning. Instead of calculating it, he thought: why not just play many games and count? With **John von Neumann** he turned the idea into a method for neutron physics at Los Alamos. The first computer runs were made on the **ENIAC** in 1948, programmed by **Klára Dán von Neumann** with **Nicholas Metropolis**. Metropolis suggested the code name *Monte Carlo*, after the casino. Metropolis and Ulam published *The Monte Carlo Method* in 1949 {cite}`metropolis1949monte,metropolis1987beginning,eckhardt1987stan`.

Earlier examples existed. In **1908** W. S. Gosset ("Student") wrote measurements of 3,000 prisoners on cards, shuffled them and drew small samples to check his new *t* distribution {cite}`student1908probable`. In the 1930s **Enrico Fermi** used random sampling by hand for neutron problems but did not publish it. What changed in the 1940s was the electronic computer.
:::

### 8. But how do we get draws from *our* posterior?

Here is the catch. We know only the *shape* (prior × likelihood), not the area that would turn it into a distribution. In **1951** von Neumann proposed **rejection sampling** {cite}`vonneumann1951various`: throw darts at a box around the curve and keep the ones that land under it. The kept darts' positions are exact draws from the posterior, and only the shape is needed.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_rejection.html" width="100%" height="625px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** even with one unknown, almost 9 darts out of 10 are wasted. With many unknowns the box is almost entirely empty (Section 5), so practically every dart would be rejected. Blind darts do not work for real models.

## Part C — Markov chain Monte Carlo: why and how it works

### 9. The idea of 1953: place the points where the probability is

In 1953 five physicists at Los Alamos faced our problem in another form. They wanted averages over all possible arrangements of molecules. Each arrangement's probability was known only up to an impossible normalising constant, the same "unknown area" as our denominator. **Nicholas Metropolis, Arianna Rosenbluth, Marshall Rosenbluth, Augusta Teller and Edward Teller** used the new **MANIAC** computer; Arianna Rosenbluth wrote the program. Their paper changed the question {cite}`metropolis1953equation`. Instead of choosing arrangements at random and *weighting* each by its probability, they *chose* arrangements with that probability, and then counted every one equally.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_weight_vs_place.html" width="100%" height="535px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

### 10. The walker only compares two heights

How can a computer place points "with the posterior's probability" when it does not know the posterior's area? With a **walker** that moves step by step:

1. From the current place, **propose** a nearby step.
2. If the proposed place is **higher** (more plausible), **move**.
3. If it is **lower**, move only **sometimes**: with probability equal to the ratio of the two heights (e.g. half as high → move half of the time).
4. If you do not move, **stay**, and count the current place once more.

Every decision uses only a **ratio** of two heights. Multiplying the whole curve by any number does not change any ratio. So the unknown area, the impossible denominator, is **never needed**.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_ratio_only.html" width="100%" height="465px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** if the curve is multiplied by one million, will the walker behave differently?

### 11. King Markov's islands: why the visits match the target

Richard McElreath tells this story in *Statistical Rethinking* (Chapter 9) {cite}`mcelreath2020statistical`. King Markov rules seven islands arranged in a ring; island 7 has seven times as many people as island 1. He wants to spend time on each island **in proportion to its population**, but he keeps no calendar and knows only the populations of the island he is on and the one he might visit. Each night he flips a coin to propose the left or the right neighbour. If that island is bigger, he goes. If it is smaller, he goes with probability = (its population) ÷ (current population).

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_king_markov.html" width="100%" height="645px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** after 20,000 nights, what share of nights will the king spend on island 7?

**What to notice:** at first the bars are jumpy. After thousands of nights the blue bars meet the orange marks: 25% of nights on island 7 (7 of 28 thousand people), 3.6% on island 1. The king never computed these shares; they emerge from the simple rule. The long-run distribution of the visits is called the **target** (or **stationary**) distribution.

The starting island does not matter in the long run: the chain **forgets where it started**.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_forget_start.html" width="100%" height="455px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

Why does the rule produce exactly the population shares? Look at the **traffic** between two islands. With the wrong shares, more travellers move one way than the other, which pushes the shares toward the populations. With the right shares, traffic is balanced in both directions and nothing changes any more.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_balanced_traffic.html" width="100%" height="495px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

:::{dropdown} For the curious: "detailed balance"
If $\pi(A)$ is the long-run share of time at A and the probability of moving from A to B is $P(A\to B)$, the Metropolis rule guarantees

$$
\pi(A)\,P(A\to B) = \pi(B)\,P(B\to A)
$$

for every pair of places. In words: the flow from A to B equals the flow back. Any distribution with this balance stays unchanged once reached. Because the rule only uses the ratio $\pi(B)/\pi(A)$, the normalising constant cancels.
:::

### 12. The walker on our survey hill

The same rule works on a continuous hill. Here the walker explores the survey posterior. It uses only prior × likelihood and never divides by the area.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_walker_hill.html" width="100%" height="845px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** the walker starts at 40%, where the hill is almost flat. What will its first steps look like?

**What to notice**

- Uphill proposals are always accepted (green); downhill proposals are sometimes rejected (red), and then the walker stays.
- The first steps climb toward the hill. Lesson 6 calls this the **warm-up**.
- After a few thousand steps the histogram of visits (right) matches the exact Beta(60, 44) curve (dashed). **This is Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).** "Markov chain" means that each step depends only on where the walker is now. "Monte Carlo" means that random numbers do the work.

## Part D — After 1953: the story to today

### 13. Hastings, Gibbs and the "MCMC revolution"

- **1970 — W. Keith Hastings** generalised the walker to lopsided proposals: the **Metropolis–Hastings algorithm** {cite}`hastings1970monte`. For almost twenty years statisticians hardly noticed.
- **1984 — Stuart and Donald Geman** restored noisy images with the **Gibbs sampler**: update one unknown at a time, drawing it from its exact distribution given all the others {cite}`geman1984stochastic`.
- **1990 — Alan Gelfand and Adrian Smith** showed that sampling solves everyday Bayesian problems {cite}`gelfand1990sampling`. Together with the **BUGS** software (from 1989), this started the "MCMC revolution": Bayesian analysis became practical for applied researchers {cite}`robert2011short`.
- **1992 — Andrew Gelman and Donald Rubin** proposed running several chains and checking whether they agree (**R-hat**) {cite}`gelman1992inference`. You will use its modern version in Lesson 6 {cite}`vehtari2021rank`.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_gibbs_staircase.html" width="100%" height="535px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

### 14. The skateboarder: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS

A random walker struggles when unknowns move together: the posterior becomes a long, narrow valley, and small random steps take forever to cross it.

- **1987 — Duane, Kennedy, Pendleton and Roweth**, working on particle physics, invented **Hybrid (Hamiltonian) Monte Carlo** {cite}`duane1987hybrid`. The walker becomes a skateboarder: it gets a random push and then *rolls* along the slope of the hill, travelling far in a single move.
- **1996–2011 — Radford Neal** brought Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) into statistics and machine learning {cite}`neal1996bayesian,neal2011mcmc`.
- **2014 — Matthew Hoffman and Andrew Gelman** created the **No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)**, which decides by itself how far to roll {cite}`hoffman2014nuts`.
- **Stan** (2012) and **PyMC** (from 2003; NUTS since PyMC3 in 2016) put NUTS into everyone's hands {cite}`carpenter2017stan,salvatier2016probabilistic,abril2023pymc`. When you call `pm.sample()` in Lesson 6, PyMC runs NUTS. The formulas are in [Going further: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS](5.HamiltonianMonteCarloNUTS.md).

<iframe src="../_static/lesson5_hmc_vs_walk.html" width="100%" height="455px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

### 15. The other road: approximation

Since Bayes, people have also tried to **replace the hill with a simpler shape** instead of sampling it. Bayes and Richard Price already struggled with the area under the posterior and could only bound it {cite}`bayes1763essay`. In **1774** Pierre-Simon Laplace approximated it with a bell curve at the peak, now called the **Laplace approximation** {cite}`laplace1774memoire`. Its modern descendants, including **variational inference** (VI), are fast but approximate. Lesson 6 shows when they work and when they mislead.

## Exit ticket

::::{dropdown} 1. Why can't we simply use a grid for a model with 20 unknowns?
With 100 points per unknown we would need 10⁴⁰ cells. Even at a billion cells per second this is far longer than the age of the universe. Moreover, almost all cells would lie where the posterior is practically zero.
::::

::::{dropdown} 2. The Metropolis walker never computes the denominator. Why is that allowed?
Every decision compares two heights through their **ratio**. The unknown constant multiplies both heights and cancels. The long-run share of visits still matches the posterior, as King Markov's islands show.
::::

::::{dropdown} 3. A walker is rejected and stays in place. Should that repeated value be deleted?
No. Staying is part of the rule. Repeated values make the walker spend more time in high places, which is exactly what makes the visits match the posterior. Deleting them would distort the result.
::::

::::{dropdown} 4. Monte Carlo error versus posterior uncertainty: which one shrinks with more draws?
Only the Monte Carlo error, the wobble of our estimate. The posterior's width reflects what the data tell us and does not shrink when we draw more values from it. Lesson 6 returns to this.
::::

## Terminology card

| Term | Plain meaning | RU |
|---|---|---|
| Normalising constant / evidence | Total area under prior × likelihood; the denominator of Bayes' theorem | нормировочная константа / обоснованность (evidence) |
| Integral | Area under a curve (or volume in many dimensions) | интеграл |
| Grid approximation | Computing prior × likelihood at many fixed points | сеточная аппроксимация |
| Curse of dimensionality | The number of grid cells explodes with the number of unknowns | проклятие размерности |
| Monte Carlo | Answering questions by simulating random draws and counting | метод Монте-Карло |
| Monte Carlo error | Wobble of a result caused by a finite number of draws | ошибка Монте-Карло |
| Rejection sampling | Keep random darts that land under the curve | выборка с отклонением |
| Markov chain | A sequence where each step depends only on the current position | марковская цепь |
| MCMC | A walker whose visits form the posterior | марковские цепи Монте-Карло (MCMC) |
| Proposal / acceptance | The suggested step / the decision to take it | предложение / принятие шага |
| Target (stationary) distribution | The distribution the visits settle into in the long run | целевое (стационарное) распределение |
| Detailed balance | Flow from A to B equals flow from B to A | условие детального баланса |
| Gibbs sampling | Update one unknown at a time from its exact conditional distribution | сэмплирование по Гиббсу |
| HMC / NUTS | The "skateboarder" sampler that uses the slope; PyMC's default | гамильтонов Монте-Карло / NUTS |
