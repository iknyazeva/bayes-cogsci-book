# Session 5: Simulation, Parameter Recovery and Research Design

Simulation is a controlled experiment on an entire statistical workflow. We specify a possible data-generating process, generate data for which the truth is known, fit the proposed model, and examine whether the resulting inferences are accurate enough for their scientific use. The goal is broader than choosing a sample size: simulation can expose coding errors, weak identification, inappropriate likelihoods, misleading decision rules, and inefficient allocation before real data are collected. {cite}`gelman2021regression`

## Learning objectives

After this session, you should be able to:

- translate a research design into a generative simulation;
- choose an outcome distribution from the measurement scale and generating process;
- distinguish recovery, calibration, and prospective design analysis;
- evaluate bias, interval width, empirical coverage, directional errors, and decision accuracy;
- compare recruitment of additional units with repeated measurement of existing units;
- document a simulation-based design analysis in the research protocol.

## 1. Where simulation enters the workflow

Session 4 examined what a prior and likelihood imply for parameters and observable data. Session 5 extends this reasoning to the **whole research procedure**. Session 6 will later examine whether an MCMC algorithm explores the posterior successfully.

The simulation should encode all planned features that can affect the estimand:

| Component | Questions to encode |
|---|---|
| Target population | Which people, organizations, places, items, or time points can appear? |
| Design | How are units sampled, assigned, clustered, or repeatedly measured? |
| Outcome process | Which likelihood represents the measurement and its conditional variation? |
| Measurement and missingness | Can values be censored, rounded, misclassified, missing, or measured with error? |
| Analysis | Which model, priors, contrasts, and predictive summaries will be reported? |
| Decision | What precision or posterior evidence would make the study useful? |

## 2. Three simulation questions

| Question | What is varied? | Main output | What it can reveal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Single-dataset recovery** | One chosen parameter vector and one simulated dataset | Posterior against known truth | Coding errors, sign mistakes, gross nonidentification |
| **Repeated recovery and calibration** | Many datasets at fixed or repeatedly drawn parameters | Bias, RMSE, coverage, rank behavior | Systematic distortion and uncertainty calibration |
| **Prospective design analysis** | Sample size, allocation, effect magnitude, missingness, or measurement quality | Precision and decision operating characteristics | Whether a feasible design answers the question |

Prior predictive checking asks whether a model generates plausible data. Posterior predictive checking asks whether a fitted model reproduces relevant features of observed data. Parameter recovery instead asks whether an analysis can learn known generating quantities under controlled conditions.

## 3. Parameter recovery

For a scalar parameter $\theta$, a repeated-recovery experiment is:

1. choose or draw $\theta_{\mathrm{true}}^{(m)}$;
2. simulate $y^{(m)}\sim p(y\mid\theta_{\mathrm{true}}^{(m)},d)$ under design $d$;
3. fit the planned model;
4. extract a posterior center $\widehat\theta^{(m)}$ and interval $[L^{(m)},U^{(m)}]$;
5. repeat for $m=1,\ldots,M$.

Plot the posterior center against the generating value and add the identity line $y=x$. Include interval bars for a manageable subset. A vertical shift suggests bias or a coding mismatch; a nearly horizontal cloud suggests weak identification; a funnel can reveal parameter-dependent uncertainty. Shrinkage toward a prior or hierarchical mean is expected, but its size and consequences must be evaluated.

```{important}
Good recovery under the fitted model does not show that the model describes the real world. It shows that the procedure works in the simulated world. Real-data adequacy still requires substantive validation and posterior predictive checking.
```

### Overfitting, Flexible Models and Bayesian Regularisation

Simulation is essential for understanding what happens when a model has the capacity to overfit empirical noise. Consider fitting a high-degree polynomial basis ($M = 9$) to $N = 12$ noisy observations:

1. **Unregularized MLE / Flat Prior**: The estimator chases idiosyncratic sample noise, oscillating wildly near boundaries (the Runge phenomenon) and producing catastrophic out-of-sample predictions.
2. **Bayesian Regularisation (Gaussian Prior $\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\lambda)$)**: Placing an explicit prior on the coefficients acts as a soft shrinkage mechanism, pulling high-order polynomial weights toward zero while retaining flexible curvature where evidence demands it.

## 4. Calibration and empirical coverage

For repeated simulations, interval coverage is

$$
\widehat C
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\mathbb I\!\left(
L^{(m)}\leq\theta_{\mathrm{true}}^{(m)}\leq U^{(m)}
\right).
$$

Two regimes answer different questions:

1. **Fixed-parameter coverage:** hold $\theta_{\mathrm{true}}$ fixed and repeatedly generate data. This is a frequentist operating characteristic of a Bayesian interval procedure at that parameter value.
2. **Joint model calibration:** draw $\theta_{\mathrm{true}}$ from the prior and then draw data from the likelihood. Posterior probabilities should be calibrated on average over this joint model when generation, fitting, and computation are coherent.

A nominal 95% interval will not yield exactly 95% in a finite Monte Carlo experiment. Conditional on coverage probability $C$,

$$
\operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat C)
=
\sqrt{\frac{C(1-C)}{M}}.
$$

With $M=1{,}000$ and $C=0.95$, the Monte Carlo standard error is about 0.7 percentage points. Report $M$ and Monte Carlo uncertainty rather than interpreting every deviation from 95% as model failure.

### Simulation-based calibration

Simulation-based calibration (SBC) repeatedly draws parameters from the prior, simulates data, fits the model, and records the rank of the generating parameter among posterior draws. Uniform ranks are expected when the generative and fitted model match and computation is correct. U-shaped, hump-shaped, or sloped rank histograms can indicate underdispersion, overdispersion, or bias. Full computational SBC is deferred until Session 6.

## 5. Design analysis: choose criteria before simulating

No single number defines an adequate design. Choose criteria that follow from the scientific use.

| Criterion | Definition or question |
|---|---|
| Bias | Average difference between posterior center and generating truth |
| RMSE | Typical squared distance between estimate and truth |
| Interval width | Is uncertainty narrow enough to separate substantively different values? |
| Coverage | Does the interval procedure represent uncertainty under the scenarios? |
| Directional accuracy | How often does the posterior favor the wrong direction? |
| Decision accuracy | How often does the prespecified action agree with the action under the truth? |
| Computational reliability | How often does fitting fail or produce poor diagnostics? |

At minimum, vary a negligible effect, the smallest substantively meaningful effect, a larger plausible effect, plausible variation and attrition, and one form of model misspecification.

## 6. Worked design case: an educational intervention

Gelman, Hill, and Vehtari illustrate design analysis by simulating a randomized intervention intended to improve exam scores. In their simplified scenario, the treatment effect is 5 points and the outcome standard deviation is 20 points. With 100 students split evenly between treatment and control, the standard-error scale for the difference is

$$
\sqrt{\frac{20^2}{50}+\frac{20^2}{50}}=4.
$$

A single simulation can produce a negative or implausibly large estimate even though the generating effect is 5. The design must therefore be judged from repeated simulations and expected uncertainty, not from whether one simulated estimate excludes zero. {cite}`gelman2021regression`

| Total students | Approximate SE | Approximate 95% interval width |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 4.00 | 15.7 |
| 400 | 2.00 | 7.8 |
| 900 | 1.33 | 5.2 |

## 7. Type S and Type M as selection risks

Type S and Type M errors are useful when estimates are selected or publicized only after passing a significance threshold:

$$
\operatorname{Type\ S}
=
P\!\left(
\operatorname{sign}(\widehat\theta)\neq
\operatorname{sign}(\theta_{\mathrm{true}})
\mid \text{selected}
\right),
$$

$$
\operatorname{Type\ M}
=
E\!\left(
\frac{|\widehat\theta|}{|\theta_{\mathrm{true}}|}
\mid \text{selected}
\right).
$$

They are not specifically Bayesian diagnostics and Type M is undefined when the generating effect is zero. Their purpose is to demonstrate selection-induced exaggeration. A Bayesian design analysis should additionally evaluate its actual posterior decision rule rather than organizing the entire simulation around $p<0.05$.

## 8. Interactions require explicit design scenarios

For a balanced $2\times2$ design with equal residual variance and predictors coded so that the main effect is an average contrast, a difference in means has standard-error scale $2\sigma/\sqrt N$, whereas a difference in differences has scale $4\sigma/\sqrt N$. An interaction of the same magnitude as the main effect therefore needs roughly four times the sample size for the same precision. If the interaction is half as large, equal relative precision can require roughly sixteen times the sample size. {cite}`gelman2021regression`

These are design intuitions, not universal constants. The result changes with imbalance, coding, unequal variances, repeated measures, missingness, multilevel structure, and the substantive contrast used as the estimand.


## 9. More units or more repeated observations?

For a balanced random-intercept design with $J$ units and $K$ repeated observations per unit,

$$
y_{jk}=\mu+u_j+\epsilon_{jk},
\qquad
u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_u^2),
\qquad
\epsilon_{jk}\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_e^2),
$$

the standard-error scale of the grand mean is

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\mu)
=
\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_u^2}{J}+\frac{\sigma_e^2}{JK}}.
$$

Increasing $K$ reduces measurement noise but cannot replace sampling more units from the target population:

$$
\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\mu)
=\frac{\sigma_u}{\sqrt J}.
$$

This conclusion is conditional on the random-intercept model, equal $K$, and independent residuals. Time trends, learning, item effects, and autocorrelation must be simulated when they belong to the design.

```{admonition} Illustration: design-allocation heatmap
:class: tip
Plot $J$ on one axis and $K$ on the other. Color each cell by expected interval width or RMSE, and overlay contours of equal total observations $J\times K$. Diminishing returns from increasing $K$ should be visible when between-unit variation is substantial.
```

## 10. Reproducible simulation structure

Separate generation, fitting, and evaluation so each component can be tested.

```python
def generate_data(rng, truth, design):
    """Generate one dataset from the assumed population and design."""
    ...

def fit_and_summarize(data, model_spec):
    """Return estimand summaries and computational diagnostics."""
    ...

def evaluate(summary, truth, decision_rule):
    """Return recovery, interval, and decision metrics."""
    ...

results = []
for simulation_id in range(1_000):
    truth = draw_truth_or_select_scenario(simulation_id)
    data = generate_data(rng, truth, design)
    summary = fit_and_summarize(data, model_spec)
    results.append(evaluate(summary, truth, decision_rule))
```

Use fixed seeds for reproducibility, but rerun the complete analysis with several seeds before treating a surprising result as substantive.

## 11. Guided practice

````{dropdown} Question 1: recovery or model fit?
A model recovers its generating coefficient in simulated data but fails to reproduce the skewness of the real outcome. What has been learned?

**Answer.** The implementation can recover the coefficient under the assumed model, but the assumed outcome model may not represent the real data. Recovery cannot substitute for posterior predictive checking.
````

````{dropdown} Question 2: what does 92% coverage mean?
A nominal 95% interval covers the fixed generating value in 920 of 1,000 simulations. Is the procedure necessarily miscalibrated?

**Answer.** The estimate is below the target, but it must be assessed with Monte Carlo uncertainty, across parameter values, and across repeated runs. Investigate whether undercoverage is concentrated near a boundary or under particular missingness or variance scenarios.
````

````{dropdown} Question 3: choose an allocation
One design samples 20 schools with 100 pupils per school; another samples 100 schools with 20 pupils per school. What determines which is more informative for a population-average estimand?

**Answer.** Between-school and within-school variation, the sampling target, costs, and the fitted multilevel model determine the answer. With substantial between-school variation, additional schools commonly contribute more population information.
````

## 12. Research protocol milestone 5

1. **Estimand:** identify the parameter, contrast, or prediction the design must recover.
2. **Generative assumptions:** specify the population, likelihood, clustering, measurement, missingness, and plausible parameter scenarios.
3. **Analysis procedure:** state the model, priors, posterior summary, diagnostics, and decision rule applied unchanged to every dataset.
4. **Recovery study:** report shrinkage or bias, RMSE, interval width, and coverage across at least 500 successful simulations.
5. **Design comparison:** compare at least two feasible sample-size or allocation strategies.
6. **Stress test:** repeat the evaluation under at least one plausible form of misspecification.
7. **Decision:** justify the selected design and state which conclusions it cannot support.
