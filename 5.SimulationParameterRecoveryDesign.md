Simulation is a controlled experiment on an entire statistical workflow. Before recruiting a single participant or collecting empirical observations, we specify an explicit data-generating process, simulate synthetic datasets for which the ground truth is known, fit the planned Bayesian model, and evaluate whether the resulting inferences satisfy our scientific requirements {cite}`gelman2021regression`.

The goal of simulation is far broader than calculating a sample size: it exposes software bugs, unidentifiable parameters, misspecified likelihoods, and inefficient sample allocations before investing time and resources in data collection.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this session and its deepening modules, you will be able to:
1. Translate a substantive research design into an executable forward generative simulation.
2. Structure a reusable simulation pipeline that evaluates parameter recovery, calibration, and design success.
3. Distinguish single-dataset recovery, repeated calibration, and prospective design analysis.
4. Diagnose estimator pathologies (bias, non-identification, shrinkage) using $45^\circ$ recovery grids and coverage audits.
5. Define Bayesian design adequacy using posterior precision, directional confidence, and Type S/M error risks.
6. Optimize sample allocation across participants ($J$), repeated trials ($K$), and clusters ($m$).
7. Document a simulation-based design analysis for Protocol Milestone 5.

## 1. Where Simulation Enters the Research Workflow

Session 4 examined what priors and likelihoods imply for parameters and observable data. Session 5 extends this generative perspective to the **entire research procedure**:

```{mermaid}
flowchart TB
    Q["1. Research Question & Estimand"] --> M["2. Measurement & Sampling Design"]
    M --> G["3. Forward Generative Model"]
    G --> S["4. Simulate True Parameters & Data"]
    S --> F["5. Fit Proposed Bayesian Model"]
    F --> E["6. Evaluate Recovery, Coverage & Decisions"]
    E --> R{"Adequate for Intended Scientific Use?"}
    R -- "No: Redesign / Reparameterize" --> M
    R -- "Yes: Freeze Protocol" --> P["7. Collect Real Empirical Data"]
```

The simulation model must encode every feature of the planned study that can influence the target estimand:

| Design Component | Questions to Encode in the Generative Simulator |
|:---|:---|
| **Population & Units** | Who is being sampled? Are observations independent individuals, repeated trials, or clusters? |
| **Data-Generating Likelihood** | What is the natural outcome scale? Continuous, binary choices, response times, or counts? |
| **Systematic Predictors** | Are experimental treatments randomized between- or within-subjects? Are covariates centered? |
| **Heterogeneity & Hierarchy** | Do intercepts or treatment slopes vary across participants, stimuli, or testing sites? |
| **Observation Artifacts** | What are the anticipated rates of missing data, trial exclusions, or measurement attrition? |

## 2. Three Questions Simulation Answers

Simulation serves three distinct functions across the model development lifecycle:

| Simulation Mode | Input Setup | Primary Diagnostic Output | Scientific Problem Solved |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **1. Single-Dataset Recovery** | One fixed true parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$; generate one synthetic dataset $y_{\text{sim}}$. | Posterior distribution compared to known $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$. | Catches coding bugs, inverted signs, and gross non-identifiability. |
| **2. Repeated Recovery & Calibration** | Draw $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(m)} \sim p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ or evaluate a parameter grid across $M = 1{,}000$ runs. | Bias, RMSE, $45^\circ$ recovery plot, empirical interval coverage $\widehat{C}$. | Verifies estimator calibration and quantifies systematic shrinkage. |
| **3. Prospective Design Analysis** | Vary candidate sample sizes $N$, allocations ($J, K$), and effect scenarios. | Operating curves: precision width, success rate, Type S/M risks. | Determines the smallest feasible sample size meeting scientific goals. |

## 3. The Core Simulation Engine: A Reusable Loop

Every simulation-based design analysis follows a unified, reproducible nine-step structure. Rather than writing ad-hoc scripts for each experiment, implement the workflow as modular, reusable functions:

```python
def run_simulation_experiment(scenario, candidate_design, M=1000, rng=None):
    """Reusable Simulation-Based Research Design (SBRD) Pipeline."""
    records = []
    for rep in range(M):
        # 1. Sample true parameter from design prior or grid
        theta_true = scenario.draw_truth(rng)
        # 2. Simulate synthetic dataset forward from generative model
        dataset = candidate_design.generate_data(theta_true, rng)
        # 3. Fit the planned Bayesian model
        posterior = candidate_design.fit_model(dataset)
        # 4. Evaluate inferential criteria (precision, direction, coverage)
        metrics = candidate_design.evaluate_criteria(posterior, theta_true)
        records.append(metrics)
    
    # 5. Summarize operating characteristics with Monte Carlo standard errors
    return summarize_operating_characteristics(records)
```

## 4. Case Study: An Educational Intervention

To see simulation-based research design in action, consider evaluating an educational coaching program adapted from Gelman, Hill, and Vehtari (*Regression and Other Stories*, Chapter 16).

Students take a baseline midterm exam ($x_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(60, 20^2)$), half are randomly assigned to coaching ($T_i \in \{0, 1\}$), and final exam scores follow:

$$
y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \tau T_i + \epsilon_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma^2 = 100),
$$

with true treatment effect $\tau = 5.0$ points ($0.5$ residual SD). The theoretical standard error of $\hat{\tau}$ in a balanced randomized trial with pre-treatment covariate $x$ is:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\tau}) = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{2 \times 10}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{N}}.
$$

### Interactive Simulation: Operating Characteristics vs. Sample Size

The interactive widget below visualizes the transition from a single noisy trial to systematic operating characteristics across candidate sample sizes from $N = 40$ to $N = 600$:

<iframe src="../_static/fig5_educational_intervention.html" width="100%" height="520px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

* **The Single-Study Fallacy**: A single small experiment ($N = 60$) can yield an estimated treatment effect of $\hat{\tau} = 1.8$ points (statistically inconclusive) or $\hat{\tau} = 8.5$ points (dramatically exaggerated). A single dataset provides no information about estimator reliability.
* **Posterior Interval Width**: Reaching an expected 95% interval width $\le 4.0$ points ($w^* = 4.0$) requires $N \ge 400$ students.
* **Monte Carlo Uncertainty**: When evaluating $M = 1{,}000$ synthetic datasets, an estimated success rate of $80\%$ carries a Monte Carlo standard error of $\operatorname{MCSE} \approx \sqrt{0.80 \times 0.20 / 1{,}000} = 1.3\%$.

## 5. Subchapters Review

The subsequent subchapters examine each technical domain of simulation-based research design in detail:

### 1. {doc}`5.ParameterRecoveryCalibration`
Covers procedures for verifying estimator identifiability and numerical stability:
* **$45^\circ$ Recovery Grid**: Plotting posterior point estimates against known true parameters to detect bias, non-identification, and variance funnels.
* **Bayesian Regularization**: Comparing unconstrained maximum likelihood overfitting (Runge boundary oscillations) with Gaussian prior shrinkage.
* **Interval Calibration & SBC**: Auditing empirical 95% coverage bounds and diagnosing posterior geometry via Simulation-Based Calibration rank histograms.

### 2. {doc}`5.PowerAnalysisBayesianDesignSimulation`
Treats sample-size planning through explicit inferential criteria rather than default $80\%$ null-rejection rules:
* **Bayesian Design Success**: Defining criteria based on posterior precision, directional certainty, ROPE boundaries, and Bayes factors.
* **Bayesian Assurance**: Pre-posterior design analysis averaging conditional power over a design prior $p_D(\theta)$.
* **Type S and Type M Selection Risks**: Formulations and curves showing sign error and magnitude exaggeration in low-precision studies.
* **Interaction Scenarios**: Explaining the $4\times$ to $16\times$ sample size requirements for balanced factorial interactions.

### 3. {doc}`5.SampleAllocationDesigns`
Addresses experimental resource allocation in multilevel and repeated-measurement designs:
* **Participants ($J$) vs. Repeated Trials ($K$)**: The asymptotic precision floor $\lim_{K \to \infty} \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\mu}) = \sigma_{\text{person}} / \sqrt{J}$ and diminishing returns from adding trials.
* **Crossed Stimulus Designs**: Generalizing across both participants ($J$) and sampled items/stimuli ($I$).
* **Clustered Field Trials**: Intra-class correlation (ICC) and design effects in multi-site interventions.
* **Budget Optimization**: Analytical trade-offs between participant recruitment costs and per-trial costs.

## 6. Guided Practice & Concept Checks

````{dropdown} Concept Check 1: What does good parameter recovery prove?
A researcher generates 500 synthetic datasets from a Gaussian likelihood, fits their Bayesian model, and finds that the true parameters fall cleanly along the $45^\circ$ diagonal with $95.2\%$ coverage. Does this prove the model is correct for real empirical data?

**Answer.** No. Parameter recovery proves that the model is **internally identifiable** and that the software estimation code is free of bugs. It does not prove that the data-generating assumptions (e.g. Gaussianity, linearity, absence of confounding) hold in the real world. Real-world validation requires posterior predictive checking, sensitivity analysis, and substantive domain theory.
````

````{dropdown} Concept Check 2: The fallacy of post-hoc / observed power
After completing an experiment with $N = 50$ participants and finding $p = 0.12$ (or a 95% credible interval spanning zero), a reviewer asks for "post-hoc power." Why should you refuse?

**Answer.** Observed/post-hoc power computed from the sample estimate is mathematically redundant with the p-value and interval width. It creates a false illusion of retrospective design justification. Design analysis is strictly **prospective**; once empirical data are collected, researchers must directly interpret the posterior credible interval and parameter uncertainty.
````

## 7. Research Protocol Milestone 5

As part of your course research project, complete the simulation-based design plan in your research protocol:

1. **Declared Estimand**: State the primary target parameter or contrast on its natural outcome scale.
2. **Forward Generative Model**: Write down the mathematical likelihood, hierarchical variance components, and design priors.
3. **Plausible Scenarios**: Define a grid of scenarios (null, minimum meaningful effect, optimistic effect, adverse noise/attrition).
4. **Candidate Designs**: Compare at least three sample sizes or allocation ratios ($J$ participants vs. $K$ trials).
5. **Operating Targets**: Pre-register your success criterion (e.g. 95% interval width $\le w^*$, Type S error $\le 1\%$).
6. **Simulation Audit**: Run $M = 1{,}000$ synthetic replications in Google Colab and report the operating characteristic curves with MCSE.

## Core Takeaways

* **Simulation before collection**: Generative simulation tests the complete statistical workflow before gathering real data.
* **Three simulation functions**: Single-dataset recovery checks software bugs; repeated recovery verifies calibration; design analysis justifies sample size.
* **Beyond $80\%$ power**: Evaluate expected interval width, directional certainty, and Type S/M exaggeration risks under realistic effect sizes.
* **Allocation matters**: In hierarchical designs, increasing repeated measurements ($K$) cannot eliminate uncertainty between units ($J$).
* **Reproducible simulation pipeline**: Build modular, vectorized simulation loops and quantify Monte Carlo uncertainty.
