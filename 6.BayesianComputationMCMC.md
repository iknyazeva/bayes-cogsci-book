Bayesian inference usually leaves us with a distribution rather than a single estimate. The practical question is therefore computational: how can we calculate expectations, probabilities, intervals, predictions, and contrasts under a posterior distribution that is not available in closed form?

This session develops one continuous argument. We begin with independent Monte Carlo draws, introduce Markov chains when direct simulation is unavailable, study Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs sampling as transparent constructions, and then explain why contemporary software usually relies on Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) for continuous differentiable models. The final section establishes the computational checks that must precede substantive interpretation. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian` {cite}`kruschke2015doing`

8. propose a remedy that addresses the diagnosed problem rather than merely increasing the number of draws.

## 1. What computation is required?

For data $y$ and parameters $\theta$,

$$
p(\theta\mid y)=\frac{p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)},
\qquad
p(y)=\int p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$

The marginal likelihood $p(y)$ is often difficult to evaluate. This does **not** mean that it must be computed before posterior simulation. Metropolis–Hastings and HMC can work with the unnormalised target

$$
\widetilde p(\theta\mid y)=p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta),
$$

because the unknown normalising constant cancels in the relevant ratios. The main objective is usually a posterior expectation,

$$
\mathbb E[g(\theta)\mid y]
=\int g(\theta)p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta,
$$

where $g$ may represent a coefficient, a probability, a predicted outcome, a group contrast, or a policy-relevant threshold event.

### A computational ladder

Use the simplest reliable method available:

1. **analytic calculation**, when conjugacy or another closed form exists;
2. **grid approximation or numerical quadrature**, for very low-dimensional problems;
3. **direct simulation**, when independent draws are available;
4. **importance or rejection sampling**, when a tractable auxiliary distribution is adequate;
5. **MCMC**, when the posterior can be evaluated up to proportionality but cannot be sampled directly.

MCMC is therefore an important general method, not a requirement for every Bayesian model.

## 2. Monte Carlo: integration by simulation

If $\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(S)}$ are independent draws from the target distribution, then

$$
\widehat{\mathbb E}[g(\theta)]
=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}g(\theta^{(s)}).
$$

The approximation has Monte Carlo standard error

$$
\operatorname{MCSE}(\bar g)
\approx \frac{\operatorname{SD}\{g(\theta)\}}{\sqrt S}.
$$

Increasing $S$ reduces simulation noise but does not make the posterior itself narrower. Four times as many independent draws are needed to halve MCSE. The detailed treatment is in {doc}`6.MonteCarloEstimation`.

> **Check before continuing.** A 95% credible interval describes uncertainty about an unknown quantity under the model. MCSE describes uncertainty in our numerical approximation to that interval or another posterior summary. They are not interchangeable.

## 3. From independent draws to Markov chains

When direct draws are unavailable, MCMC constructs a sequence

$$
\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)},\ldots
$$

whose transition rule depends on the current state and whose invariant distribution is the posterior. Under appropriate regularity conditions, averages along the chain converge to posterior expectations even though neighbouring draws are dependent.

Dependence affects precision. For a scalar estimand $g(\theta)$, we summarize it by an effective sample size (ESS): the number of independent draws that would provide comparable information. Modern software reports rank-based bulk ESS and tail ESS rather than relying only on the elementary autocorrelation formula. ESS is specific to a quantity: a model can estimate a posterior mean adequately while estimating a tail probability poorly.

See {doc}`6.MarkovChains` for invariant distributions, autocorrelation, and mixing.

## 4. Two transparent MCMC constructions

### 4.1 Metropolis–Hastings

Starting from $\theta$, propose $\theta^*\sim q(\theta^*\mid\theta)$ and accept it with probability

$$
a(\theta,\theta^*)=
\min\left[
1,
\frac{\widetilde p(\theta^*\mid y)q(\theta\mid\theta^*)}
     {\widetilde p(\theta\mid y)q(\theta^*\mid\theta)}
\right].
$$

If the proposal is symmetric, the $q$ terms cancel. Rejected proposals remain in the chain as repeated states; deleting them changes the target distribution. Proposal scale creates a trade-off: small steps are accepted frequently but move slowly, while large steps may be rejected frequently. There is no universal acceptance rate that certifies a good chain. See {doc}`6.MetropolisHastings`.

### 4.2 Gibbs sampling

Gibbs sampling updates blocks from their **full conditional distributions**:

$$
\theta_1^{(s)}\sim p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2^{(s-1)},y),
\qquad
\theta_2^{(s)}\sim p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1^{(s)},y).
$$

These are exact conditional draws, so pure Gibbs updates do not require a separate accept–reject decision. Gibbs sampling remains useful for conjugate and discrete latent-variable models, but strongly correlated parameters can make component-wise movement slow. See {doc}`6.GibbsSampling`.

## 5. Why HMC and NUTS?

Random-walk proposals ignore the local geometry of a continuous posterior. When parameters are numerous or strongly correlated, useful proposals become difficult to construct. HMC augments the parameter vector with an auxiliary momentum and uses gradients of the log joint density to simulate a trajectory through parameter space.

Define potential and kinetic energy as

$$
U(\theta)=-\log p(y,\theta),
\qquad
K(r)=\frac{1}{2}r^\top M^{-1}r.
$$

The leapfrog integrator approximates Hamiltonian motion under $H(\theta,r)=U(\theta)+K(r)$. A Metropolis correction removes the remaining discretisation bias. Because the trajectory can travel far while remaining in a region of plausible energy, HMC often produces less autocorrelated draws than a random walk.

NUTS adapts the trajectory length during warmup and stops extending a trajectory when it begins to double back. Contemporary implementations also adapt a step size and mass matrix. These mechanisms make NUTS a strong default for many continuous differentiable models, but they do not repair an unidentified or scientifically misspecified model. {cite}`mcelreath2020statistical` {cite}`gelman2021regression`

## 6. Warmup is not retained sampling

During **warmup** (often called tuning), NUTS adapts numerical settings and moves away from potentially poor initial values. Warmup draws are not treated as posterior draws. After adaptation ends, the sampler generates the **retained draws** used for inference.

This distinction is more informative than the older idea of choosing a fixed fraction as “burn-in.” A longer warmup may improve adaptation; a larger retained sample may reduce MCSE. These changes solve different problems.

## 7. Running a model in PyMC: the Beta–Binomial benchmark

To validate the computational sampler against known ground truth, we implement the conjugate Beta–Binomial benchmark:

$$
\theta \sim \operatorname{Beta}(2, 2), \qquad y \mid \theta \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(n=80, \theta), \qquad y = 48,
$$

which has the exact closed-form analytic posterior:

$$
\theta \mid y \sim \operatorname{Beta}(2 + 48, 2 + 32) = \operatorname{Beta}(50, 34).
$$

The exact posterior mean is $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = 50 / 84 \approx 0.5952$, and the exact 95% equal-tailed credible interval is $[0.489, 0.697]$.

```python
import arviz as az
import pymc as pm
import scipy.stats as stats

# Exact analytic benchmark values
exact_mean = 50 / (50 + 34)  # 0.5952
exact_ci = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], 50, 34)  # [0.489, 0.697]

with pm.Model() as beta_binom_model:
    # Prior
    theta = pm.Beta("theta", alpha=2, beta=2)
    # Likelihood with observed data (48 successes out of 80 trials)
    y = pm.Binomial("y", n=80, p=theta, observed=48)

    # Sample using NUTS
    idata = pm.sample(
        draws=1_000,
        tune=1_000,
        chains=4,
        random_seed=2026,
        return_inferencedata=True,
    )

# Compute MCMC diagnostics and posterior summary
summary = az.summary(idata, var_names=["theta"])
print(summary[["mean", "sd", "hdi_3%", "hdi_97%", "mcse_mean", "ess_bulk", "r_hat"]])
az.plot_rank(idata, var_names=["theta"])
```

Comparing the sampled posterior mean ($\approx 0.595$) and interval with the exact values validates that the HMC sampler and implementation are functioning correctly within Monte Carlo error ($\operatorname{MCSE} \approx 0.001$). Estimators must always be validated against simple, known benchmarks before applying them to complex models.

## 8. The computational diagnostic gate

No single number proves convergence. Before interpretation, inspect several complementary signals for every primary estimand and important transformed quantity.

| Question | Evidence | Course screening rule | What failure may indicate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do chains explore the same marginal distribution? | rank plots and rank-normalized split $\widehat R$ | prefer $\widehat R\leq1.01$ | non-stationarity, multimodality, poor mixing |
| Are central summaries precise enough? | bulk ESS and MCSE | justify precision for the estimand; 400 is a useful minimum screen, not a universal guarantee | autocorrelation or too few retained draws |
| Are intervals and tail probabilities precise enough? | tail ESS and MCSE for quantiles/probabilities | assess the reported tail quantity directly | poorly explored tails |
| Did HMC encounter problematic geometry? | divergences, energy/BFMI, maximum tree depth | require zero unresolved divergences for course reports | curvature, boundaries, weak identification, poor scaling |
| Do traces and ranks tell a coherent story? | trace and rank plots across chains | no persistent drift, separation, or sticking | chains may not represent the same target |

The course uses four chains as a transparent default, $\widehat R\leq1.01$ as a screening threshold, and zero unresolved divergences as a reporting requirement. These rules are safeguards, not a proof that the likelihood, priors, measurement model, or causal interpretation is adequate.

### A remedy should follow the diagnosis

1. verify data coding, parameter support, likelihood, and deterministic transformations;
2. inspect prior predictive implications and parameter scaling;
3. identify weakly identified parameters and posterior correlations;
4. rescale predictors or reparameterise the model, comparing centred and non-centred forms where appropriate;
5. adjust sampler settings such as `target_accept` when the geometry is otherwise defensible;
6. add warmup or retained draws only when adaptation or Monte Carlo precision is the actual limitation.

Running longer cannot by itself fix structural non-identification or persistent divergent geometry. Thinning is generally inefficient because it discards draws; it is defensible mainly when storage or downstream processing is the binding constraint.

The later chapter on assessing convergence and MCMC diagnostics ({doc}`6.AssessingConvergence`) develops these checks in greater depth.

````{dropdown} Question 1: Matching means but differing ranks
Two chains have nearly identical posterior means, but their rank histograms occupy different parts of the rank range. What claim about computation is not yet justified?

**Answer.** Agreement of two means does not establish that the chains explored the same distribution. They may differ in spread, skewness, tails, or modes. The posterior should not be interpreted until the discrepancy is explained.
````

````{dropdown} Question 2: Divergences alongside good R-hat
A model has $\widehat R=1.00$ and large ESS for every displayed coefficient, but it reports 17 divergences. What should happen next?

**Answer.** Do not interpret the model yet. Locate the divergent draws, examine scaling, constraints, identification, and hierarchical parameterisation, and refit. A higher `target_accept` may help with discretisation error, but it should not substitute for investigating the geometry.
````

````{dropdown} Question 3: Acceptance rate vs. sampling efficiency
Why can a chain with a 95% acceptance rate estimate a posterior mean less efficiently than a chain with a lower acceptance rate?

**Answer.** Very small proposals can be accepted almost every time while producing highly autocorrelated draws. Acceptance must be evaluated together with movement, ESS, and MCSE.
````
