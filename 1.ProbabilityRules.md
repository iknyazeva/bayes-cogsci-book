# Probability Background

Before diving into Bayesian analysis, we need a solid foundation in probability. This section reviews the core rules — using both classic textbook examples and cognitive science applications.

## Rules of Probability

Probabilities are defined for **events**. An event is some outcome that we could potentially observe, such as the result of rolling a fair six-sided die.
```{image} CatsDice.jpeg
:alt: Cats and Dice
:width: 320px
:align: center
```

- Example: Let **A** be the event "rolling a 4."
  - Then $P(A) = \tfrac{1}{6}$.
  - If we represent the die roll as the random variable $X$, then we can also write $P(X = 4) = \tfrac{1}{6}$.

### Basic rules

- Probabilities are always between zero and one:

  $$
  0 \leq P(A) \leq 1
  $$

- The sum of probabilities over all possible events is one:

  $$
  \sum_{i=1}^{6} P(X = i) = 1
  $$

- Complement: the probability of "not A" (denoted $A^c$) is:

  $$
  P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)
  $$

- Union of two events:

  $$
  P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
  $$

- If $A_i$ for $i=1,\ldots,m$ are **mutually exclusive** (only one can happen), then:

  $$
  P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(A_i)
  $$

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example
:class: tip
In a visual search experiment, a participant must find a target letter among distractors on a screen. Let $A$ = "target is detected" and $B$ = "target appears in the left visual field."

Suppose $P(A) = 0.85$, $P(B) = 0.50$, and $P(A \cap B) = 0.45$. Using the union rule:

$$
P(A \cup B) = 0.85 + 0.50 - 0.45 = 0.90
$$

So there is a 90% probability that either the target is detected or it appeared on the left side (or both).
```


##  Odds

Probabilities can also be expressed in terms of **odds**.

For example, for event A = "rolling a 4":

$$
O(A) = \frac{P(A)}{P(A^c)} = \frac{P(A)}{1 - P(A)}
$$

Substituting $P(A) = \tfrac{1}{6}$:

$$
O(A) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{6}}{\tfrac{5}{6}} = \tfrac{1}{5}
$$

This can be read as **1:5 odds** (or "5 to 1 against").

- Event with probability $\tfrac{3}{10}$ → odds = 3:7.
- Event with probability $\tfrac{4}{5}$ → odds = 4:1.

### From odds back to probability

If an event has odds $a:b$, then:

$$
\frac{P(B)}{1 - P(B)} = \frac{a}{b} \implies P(B) = \frac{a}{a+b}
$$

Example: odds 2:5 → probability = $\tfrac{2}{7}$.

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example
:class: tip
In clinical neuropsychology, **odds ratios** are commonly reported. For instance, if the probability that a patient with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) progresses to dementia within 5 years is $P = 0.3$, then:

$$
O(\text{progression}) = \frac{0.3}{0.7} \approx 0.43 \quad \text{(about 3:7 against)}
$$

Odds ratios are especially useful in logistic regression models of cognitive outcomes, where the effect of a predictor (e.g., years of education) is expressed as a multiplicative change in odds.
```


## Expectation

The **expected value** of a random variable $X$ is a weighted average of its possible values, weighted by their probabilities.

If $X$ can take values $x_1, \ldots, x_n$:

$$
E(X) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \cdot P(X = x_i)
$$

**Example:** fair six-sided die:

$$
E(X) = \sum_{i=1}^{6} i \cdot \tfrac{1}{6}
= \tfrac{1}{6}(1+2+3+4+5+6)
= 3.5
$$

Note: the die will never land on 3.5, but across many rolls the average outcome tends toward this number.

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example
:class: tip
A memory recall experiment presents word lists at three difficulty levels. Performance is scored on a 0–10 scale:

| Condition | Score | Probability |
|-----------|-------|-------------|
| Easy      | 9     | 0.40        |
| Medium    | 6     | 0.35        |
| Hard      | 3     | 0.25        |

The expected score across randomly assigned conditions:

$$
E(X) = 9 \times 0.40 + 6 \times 0.35 + 3 \times 0.25 = 3.6 + 2.1 + 0.75 = 6.45
$$

This tells us the average performance we would expect if conditions are assigned with these probabilities.
```


## Variance

Expectation tells us the **average** value of a random variable, but it does not tell us how much values vary around that average. This is where **variance** comes in.

Variance of $X$:

$$
Var(X) = E\big[(X - E(X))^2\big]
$$

This can also be written as:

$$
Var(X) = E(X^2) - (E(X))^2
$$

### Example: variance of a fair six-sided die

1. First compute $E(X^2)$:

$$
E(X^2) = \sum_{i=1}^{6} i^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6}
= \tfrac{1}{6}(1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2 + 6^2)
= \tfrac{91}{6}
$$

2. Recall $E(X) = 3.5$, so $(E(X))^2 = 12.25$.

3. Thus:

$$
Var(X) = \tfrac{91}{6} - 12.25 = \tfrac{35}{12} \approx 2.92
$$

The **standard deviation** is the square root of variance:

$$
SD(X) = \sqrt{Var(X)} \approx 1.71
$$

So, while the expected value of the die is 3.5, the outcomes typically vary about ±1.7 around this mean.

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example
:class: tip
Reaction times (RTs) in cognitive experiments often exhibit high variance. If a participant's mean RT in a Stroop task is 450 ms with $SD = 80$ ms, the variance is $80^2 = 6400 \text{ ms}^2$.

High RT variance can indicate **attentional lapses**, strategy shifts between trials, or a mixture of fast automatic responses and slow controlled responses — a pattern that is itself informative about the underlying cognitive processes.
```

```{important}
**Key idea:**
- **Expectation** = average outcome.
- **Variance** = how spread out the outcomes are around the average.

These two quantities — together with the full probability distribution — are the building blocks we need for Bayesian modeling.
```
