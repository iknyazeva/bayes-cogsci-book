# When Bayesian and Frequentist Results Become Numerically Similar

Bayesian and frequentist procedures can sometimes produce the same interval endpoints or very similar point estimates. This can happen exactly in a few reference-prior models and approximately in large, regular samples. Numerical similarity does not make the underlying questions identical.

## Learning goals

After this subchapter, you should be able to:

- demonstrate an exact Bayesian–frequentist correspondence in a Normal model;
- explain why a fixed regular prior often has diminishing local influence as sample size grows;
- state the conditions behind a large-sample Normal approximation to the posterior;
- recognize settings in which that approximation is unreliable;
- interpret a credible interval and a confidence interval without conflating them.

## Similar endpoints, different probability statements

A 95% Bayesian credible interval is calculated from the posterior distribution and, conditional on the model and observed data, contains 95% posterior probability for the parameter.

A 95% frequentist confidence procedure is calibrated over hypothetical repetitions of the sampling process: before observing the data, 95% of intervals constructed by that procedure cover the fixed parameter under the assumed model.

```{important}
Even when the two intervals have identical numerical endpoints, their probability statements are different. A numerical correspondence is a property of a model and procedure, not a license to exchange interpretations.
```

## Exact correspondence 1: a Normal mean with known variance

Let

$$
y_i\mid\mu\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2),
$$

where $\sigma$ is known, and use the improper flat prior $p(\mu)\propto1$. The posterior is

$$
\mu\mid y\sim\operatorname{Normal}\!\left(\bar y,\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right).
$$

Its central 95% credible interval is

$$
\bar y\pm1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N},
$$

the same endpoints as the classical $z$ confidence interval. This is an exact algebraic correspondence under this model and prior.

```{admonition} Demonstration: same interval, two readings
:class: tip
Suppose a standardized social-attitude score has $\bar y=52$, known $\sigma=10$, and $N=100$. Both calculations give approximately $[50.04,53.96]$.

- Bayesian reading: conditional on the model, prior, and observed data, the posterior probability that $\mu$ lies in this interval is 0.95.
- Frequentist reading: the interval-producing procedure covers the fixed $\mu$ in 95% of repeated samples under the model.

Ask students to identify which parts of each sentence are random before and after data are observed.
```

## Exact correspondence 2: unknown Normal variance

For Normal observations with both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ unknown, the common reference prior

$$
p(\mu,\sigma^2)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
$$

produces a Student-$t$ marginal posterior for $\mu$. Its central interval has the same endpoints as the classical one-sample $t$ confidence interval. This result is useful for understanding the connection, but it should not be generalized to arbitrary models, priors, or interval definitions. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

```{note}
The reference prior above is often used for this Normal model. In a multiparameter problem, “the Jeffreys prior” is not a single unambiguous label: the determinant rule and an independent/reference construction can differ. See {doc}`4.JeffreysPrior`.
```

## A Beta–Binomial comparison: convergence rather than identity

With a fixed $\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$ prior and $k$ successes in $N$ trials,

$$
p\mid k,N\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a+k,b+N-k),
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E[p\mid k,N]
=\frac{a+k}{a+b+N}.
$$

Writing $\hat p=k/N$ shows the posterior mean as a weighted compromise between the prior mean and the sample proportion. If $a$ and $b$ remain fixed while $N$ grows, the prior contribution to this average becomes small relative to $N$. The posterior also becomes increasingly concentrated under regular conditions.

This does not imply exact equality with a Wald, Wilson, or likelihood-based confidence interval. Different classical intervals differ from one another, especially in small samples or near 0 and 1, and Bayesian intervals still depend on the chosen prior.

```{admonition} Interactive experiment
:class: tip
Use the prior explorer in {doc}`4.PriorsPosteriorPredictiveChecks`.

1. Set $N=10$ and choose data that conflict with the contextual prior. Compare the four posteriors.
2. Hold $k/N$ approximately fixed and increase $N$ to 50 and 200.
3. Track the posterior mean, interval width, and $P(p>0.5)$.
4. Decide which features converge first and whether practically relevant differences remain.

Do not change the prior after seeing each result: the purpose is to study sensitivity, not to search for a preferred conclusion.
```

## Why large samples can produce similar answers

In a regular, finite-dimensional model, if the parameter is identifiable and lies in the interior of the parameter space, the likelihood is sufficiently smooth, and the prior gives positive continuous density near the data-generating value, the posterior is often approximately Normal:

$$
p(\theta\mid y)
\approx
\operatorname{Normal}\!\left(
\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}},
J_N(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}})^{-1}
\right).
$$

This is the intuition behind the Bernstein–von Mises connection: locally, the likelihood contributes information of order $N$, whereas a fixed regular prior contributes order-one curvature. Bayesian credible regions and likelihood-based confidence regions may therefore become close. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

```{warning}
“With enough data, the prior does not matter” is too strong. The approximation is conditional on a model, an estimand, regularity, identification, and a prior that does not exclude relevant parameter values. Large datasets can make a misspecified model confidently wrong.
```

## When the approximation can fail

Numerical convergence may be slow, incomplete, or absent when:

- a parameter is near a boundary, as with a rare event probability close to zero;
- the likelihood is weakly identified, flat, separated, or multimodal;
- mixture components or latent classes create label ambiguity;
- the number of parameters grows with the sample size;
- many groups have sparse observations in a hierarchical model;
- the prior is strongly informative or puts negligible mass near data-supported values;
- the sampling or measurement model is misspecified;
- the target is a discontinuous decision or a tail probability rather than a posterior center.

### Sociological example: rare events and separation

Suppose a logistic regression predicts participation in a rare protest event, and no event is observed in one subgroup. A maximum-likelihood coefficient may diverge under complete separation. A weakly informative prior can keep the posterior finite and encode that implausibly enormous log-odds effects require evidence. Here, approximation to an unregularized frequentist estimate is neither rapid nor necessarily desirable.

### Sociological example: sparse group comparisons

In a multilevel comparison of schools, neighborhoods, or organizations, some groups may remain weakly informed even when the total dataset is large. Partial pooling continues to matter for those groups because the relevant information is the within-group and between-group structure, not merely the grand total $N$.

## A practical comparison protocol

When a Bayesian result resembles a classical result, ask:

1. Are the estimand and likelihood the same?
2. Is the correspondence exact, simulation-based, or asymptotic?
3. Which prior was used, and is it proper?
4. Are parameters near boundaries or weakly identified?
5. Are the intervals constructed by the same rule: central, highest-density, Wald, score, or likelihood-ratio?
6. Do both analyses address the same decision or merely report similar parameter summaries?

```{admonition} Guided question 1
:class: question

```

```{admonition} Guided question 2
:class: question

```

```{admonition} Guided question 3
:class: question
Construct a case in which the posterior mean is close to the maximum-likelihood estimate but a posterior tail probability still changes enough to alter a decision.
```

## What to retain

- Exact numerical correspondences occur in particular models with particular priors.
- Large-sample approximation requires regularity; it is not a universal law that data erase priors.
- Similar interval endpoints do not imply identical probability statements.
- Weak priors can remain scientifically important near boundaries, under weak identification, and for sparse hierarchical groups.
- The goal of Bayesian analysis is not to reproduce a $p$-value, but to build and criticize a generative model that answers the research question.
