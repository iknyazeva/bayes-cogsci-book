## 1. Bernoulli and Binomial formulas

A binary response with success probability $\theta$ has probability mass function

$$
p(y\mid\theta)=\theta^y(1-\theta)^{1-y},\qquad y\in\{0,1\}.
$$

For $N$ conditionally independent responses with the same $\theta$, their total $k$ is Binomial:

$$
p(k\mid N,\theta)=\binom{N}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{N-k},
\qquad k\in\{0,\ldots,N\}.
$$

Its mean and variance are

$$
\mathbb E[k\mid N,\theta]=N\theta,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(k\mid N,\theta)=N\theta(1-\theta).
$$

For the proportion $k/N$, the variance is $\theta(1-\theta)/N$. This explains why the proportions in the lesson's simulation become less variable when $N$ increases.

### Worked likelihood calculation

For $k=13$ and $N=20$:

$$
\mathcal L(0.65)=\binom{20}{13}(0.65)^{13}(0.35)^7\approx0.184401,
$$

$$
\mathcal L(0.50)=\binom{20}{13}(0.50)^{20}\approx0.073929.
$$

Their ratio is approximately $2.4943$. The Binomial coefficient cancels in this ratio because the same observed count and sample size are used in both terms.

The likelihood is a function of $\theta$ for fixed data. It is not required to integrate to one over $\theta$. For this Binomial likelihood,

$$
\int_0^1\mathcal L(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{1}{N+1}=\frac{1}{21}.
$$

Normalizing it on this scale gives the posterior associated with a uniform prior on $\theta$. A different prior generally gives a different posterior.

## 2. Poisson counts and variation between observations

A Poisson model can describe event counts over an exposure interval:

$$
p(y\mid\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^y e^{-\lambda}}{y!},
\qquad y\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.
$$

Here $\lambda$ is the **expected count for the stated exposure**. If an event rate is $r$ per minute and observation lasts $t$ minutes, then $\lambda=rt$ under a constant-rate model.

The conditional mean and variance both equal $\lambda$. Sample mean and sample variance need not be identical, even if the model is appropriate. Compare counts with the same exposure and account for relevant groups or predictors before interpreting excess variation.

### Practice: errors across participants

Participants complete 100 trials, with at most one error per trial. Their mean error count is 4 and the sample variance is 12.

1. What is the range of possible counts?
2. Why might a common error probability fail to describe these participants?
3. What would you check before changing the distribution?

:::{dropdown} Answer
The count ranges from 0 to 100, so a Binomial model is a natural starting point. At a common error probability of 0.04, its population mean is 4 and variance is $100(0.04)(0.96)=3.84$. A Poisson model is only a rare-event approximation here.

A sample variance of 12 warrants examining variation between participants, experimental conditions, trial dependence, missing trials, and coding. The number of independent participants matters when judging how surprising this discrepancy is. Differing participant probabilities may motivate a Beta-Binomial model; the mean and variance alone cannot identify the cause.
:::

For counts without a fixed trial limit, varying event rates can produce a negative binomial distribution. For example,

$$
\lambda_i\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\text{rate}=\alpha/\mu),
\qquad y_i\mid\lambda_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_i)
$$

implies marginal mean $\mu$ and variance $\mu+\mu^2/\alpha$. This is one possible account of extra variation. Unequal exposures or omitted predictors can produce similar patterns. See the [distribution reference](3.DistributionsInBayes.md) for other choices.

## 3. Parameters and predictions

A parameter is a quantity defined by a model, such as a support probability or average response time. It need not be a directly observable property or a causal effect. Observed data are the recorded values used for inference; future or replicated data are new values generated or predicted under specified conditions.

| Distribution | What it describes |
|---|---|
| Prior $p(\theta)$ | Parameter uncertainty before using the current data |
| Prior predictive $p(y_{\mathrm{sim}})$ | Possible observations generated using the prior and observation model |
| Posterior $p(\theta\mid y)$ | Parameter uncertainty after using the current data |
| Posterior predictive $p(\tilde y\mid y)$ | New observations generated using the posterior and observation model |

For a continuous parameter,

$$
p(y_{\mathrm{sim}})=\int p(y_{\mathrm{sim}}\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta,
$$

$$
p(\theta\mid y)=\frac{p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)},
$$

$$
p(\tilde y\mid y)=\int p(\tilde y\mid\theta)p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta.
$$

The last expression assumes that the new observations are conditionally independent of the observed data given $\theta$ and the specified design. For discrete parameters, use sums in place of integrals.

To simulate a predictive dataset, draw one parameter value from the prior or posterior, then generate a dataset conditional on that draw. Repeat both steps. Keep the parameter shared within each dataset when the model assumes a common parameter (in the PyMC workflow, this is automated via `pm.sample_prior_predictive` and `pm.sample_posterior_predictive`).
 
Simulation at a **fixed chosen parameter** is different from prior predictive simulation, which also draws parameters from a prior. Prior predictive checks assess whether those combined assumptions generate plausible observations. Posterior predictive checks compare replicated and observed data on relevant features (commonly visualized using ArviZ routines such as `az.plot_ppc`). Neither establishes that a model is true.

## 4. Two sources of predictive uncertainty

Parameter uncertainty is sometimes called *epistemic uncertainty*. Variation in observations conditional on the parameters is sometimes called *aleatory uncertainty*. These distinctions refer to the specified model: adding useful predictors may explain some variation previously left unexplained.

When the relevant variances are finite, the law of total variance gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\tilde Y\mid D)
=
\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\tilde Y\mid\theta,D)\mid D]
+
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[\tilde Y\mid\theta,D]\mid D).
$$

The first term describes average variation at fixed parameter values. The second describes variation in the conditional mean due to parameter uncertainty. Both terms are on the scale of the predicted outcome; comparing the variance of a count directly with the variance of a probability is not meaningful.

### More observed data and larger future samples

These are different changes. Suppose a future sample contains $m$ conditionally independent Bernoulli responses with a shared $\theta$. For its proportion $\tilde p$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\tilde p\mid D)
=\frac{\mathbb E[\theta(1-\theta)\mid D]}{m}
+\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid D).
$$

Increasing the future sample size $m$ reduces the first term, but leaves current parameter uncertainty unchanged. More informative observed data can reduce the second term. Concentration around the true parameter requires suitable model, prior, and sampling conditions; it is not guaranteed merely by increasing the number of recorded rows.

Even if $\theta$ were known exactly, an individual future answer would remain uncertain when $0<\theta<1$.

## 5. From simulation to a research model

A useful sequence is to describe the observation and study design, choose a probability model, and simulate under several plausible parameter values. A dependency diagram can help document a more complicated model, but its arrows do not automatically have a causal interpretation.

Check simulated ranges, variation, and patterns relevant to the intended analysis. Substantial negative reaction times, for example, would prompt reconsideration of the distribution or chosen parameter values. A plausible histogram does not check every assumption, establish software correctness, or show that the model can identify the parameter of interest.

**Parameter recovery** adds another step: fit the model to simulated datasets and examine estimates and uncertainty intervals across repeated simulations. This is developed in [Lesson 5](5.SimulationParameterRecoveryDesign.md).
