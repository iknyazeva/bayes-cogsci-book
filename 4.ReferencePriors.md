# Appendix: Reference, Flat, and Jeffreys Priors

The phrase **non-informative prior** suggests that a probability distribution can represent complete ignorance. In most applied problems, this is too strong. Every prior privileges a scale, assigns relative plausibility to parameter regions, and combines with a specific likelihood. It is therefore more precise to distinguish weakly informative priors, flat or diffuse priors, formal reference priors, and improper priors.

| Flat or diffuse | Has nearly constant density over a chosen region or is very broad on a chosen scale | Sometimes |
| Reference prior | Constructed by a formal rule intended to limit prior influence or obtain an invariance property | Sometimes |
| Improper prior | Has infinite total mass and is used only as a limiting calculation | No |

These labels are not ordered from “most Bayesian” to “least Bayesian.” They answer different design questions.

:::{important}
An improper prior is not a probability distribution. It cannot generate prior predictive data, cannot be assigned a literal prior probability interpretation, and must not be used unless the resulting posterior is shown to be proper.
:::

## 2. Why a uniform prior is not assumption-free

For a Binomial probability theta, a common default is

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)=\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).
$$

It is flat on the probability scale. If eta=logit(theta), however, the induced prior on eta is not flat:

$$
p(\eta)
=
p(\theta)\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta)
\left[1-\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta)\right].
$$

Thus “uniform” is incomplete unless the parameterization is named.

*Figure 4.16: The Transformation Trap. A flat uniform prior $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ on probability $\theta$ induces a heavy-tailed standard logistic density on the log-odds scale $\beta = \text{logit}(\theta)$, placing nearly 20% of its prior probability on extreme odds $(|\beta| > 2.2)$ and over 40% on $(|\beta| > 1.39)$:*

<iframe src="../_static/interactive_prior_weight_seesaw.html" width="100%" height="400px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>
*(Open the "Transformation Trap" tab in the interactive widget above to explore probability vs. log-odds densities).*

The prior predictive implication is also informative. If

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1),
\qquad
y\mid\theta,N\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(N,\theta),
$$

then the Beta-Binomial prior predictive distribution is uniform over the N+1 possible counts:

$$
p(y=k)=\frac{1}{N+1},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,N.
$$

Before observing any data, 0 supporters, N/2 supporters and N supporters receive the same probability as count outcomes. That may be reasonable for a teaching example, but it is not automatically weak in an applied survey.

````{dropdown} Intuition check: flat density versus flat predictions
A flat density over theta does not make individual binary outcomes equally surprising in every sense; it implies a particular mixture over Binomial datasets. Always inspect the implied distribution of the observable quantity.
````

## 3. Smaller Beta parameters are not simply “less information”

The limiting prior

$$
p(\theta)\propto\theta^{-1}(1-\theta)^{-1},
$$

is sometimes written Beta(0,0) and called the **Haldane prior**. It is improper and concentrates its limiting mass near 0 and 1. Beta(epsilon,epsilon) with small positive epsilon is proper but strongly U-shaped.

This prior can reproduce the sample proportion as a posterior mean when 0<k<N:

$$
\theta\mid k,N\sim\operatorname{Beta}(k,N-k),
\qquad
\mathbb{E}(\theta\mid k,N)=\frac{k}{N}.
$$

That algebraic match does not make the prior neutral. If k=0 or k=N, the posterior remains improper. For model comparison, near-Haldane alternatives can also behave pathologically because they assign almost no prior mass to moderate parameter values supported by ordinary data. Kruschke uses this case to show why a mathematically diffuse default need not represent a credible scientific alternative. {cite}`kruschke2015doing`

:::{admonition} Illustrative Scenario: The “Zero Errors” Usability Testing Dilemma
:class: tip
A cognitive engineering team tests a safety-critical cockpit alert system on $N = 5$ pilots during flight simulation. All 5 pilots complete the emergency maneuver without making an error ($k = 0$ errors).

What is the estimated pilot error probability $\theta$?

1. **Maximum Likelihood (Frequentist MLE)**:
   $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{0}{5} = 0.0$ (0.0%).
   *The Point-Estimate Trap*: The MLE is merely a point estimate, not a declaration that error is physically impossible. In fact, for zero events in 5 trials, the exact one-sided 95% Clopper-Pearson upper confidence bound is $1 - 0.05^{1/5} \approx 0.451$ (a true error rate as high as 45% remains entirely compatible with the data!). The error lies in treating the unregularized plug-in estimate $\hat{\theta} = 0$ as absolute certainty.

2. **Haldane Prior $\operatorname{Beta}(0, 0)$**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(0 + 0, 0 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(0, 5)$ (Non-integrable / Improper).
   *The Breakdown*: Fails completely when no events are observed in either direction ($k = 0$ or $k = N$).

3. **Jeffreys Reference Prior $\operatorname{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)$**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(0.5 + 0, 0.5 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(0.5, 5.5) \implies \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{0.5}{6.0} \approx 0.083$ (8.3%).

4. **Uniform Prior $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$ (Laplace's Rule of Succession)**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(1 + 0, 1 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(1, 6) \implies \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{1}{7.0} \approx 0.143$ (14.3%).

5. **Weakly Informative Baseline $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 9)$ ($\mu_0 = 0.10$ historical error rate, $n_0 = 10$)**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(1 + 0, 9 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(1, 14) \implies \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{1}{15.0} \approx 0.067$ (6.7%).

*Key Takeaway*: In small samples, weakly informative priors and reference rules protect empirical researchers from asserting absolute certainty (0% or 100%) based solely on small-sample stochastic absences, smoothly integrating prior plausibility with the wide uncertainty inherent in sparse data.
:::

## 4. Improper reference calculations for Normal data

### Known variance, unknown mean

If


$$
y_i\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2)
$$

with known sigma, the flat prior

$$
p(\mu)\propto 1
$$

gives the proper posterior

$$
\mu\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}\left(\bar y,\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right).
$$

This is an exact case in which the posterior mean equals the maximum-likelihood estimate and the equal-tailed credible interval has the same endpoints as the familiar z confidence interval. Their interpretations remain different.

### Unknown mean and variance

A widely used reference specification is

$$
p(\mu,\sigma^2)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^2}.
$$

It yields

$$
\frac{\mu-\bar y}{s/\sqrt{N}}
\mid y
\sim t_{N-1},
$$

so the usual posterior interval for mu has the same numerical endpoints as the classical t interval. Jackman presents this as a direct example of a Bayesian calculation reproducing a standard frequentist result. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

:::{warning}
Do not call p(mu,sigma^2) proportional to 1/sigma^2 “the joint Jeffreys prior” without qualification. In a multiparameter Normal model, the full determinant-based Jeffreys rule and commonly used independent/reference constructions are not identical. This distinction is developed in {doc}`4.JeffreysPrior`.
:::

## 5. Weakly informative priors

A weakly informative prior is proper and deliberately broader than the analyst's best substantive knowledge while still excluding unreasonable values. Gelman and colleagues recommend this strategy in many applied problems because it provides stability without pretending to encode complete knowledge. {cite}`mcelreath2020statistical` {cite}`gelman2021regression`

### The scale must be stated

The statement

$$
\beta\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)
$$

cannot be judged until beta is interpreted. It can be weak for a standardized mean difference, strong for an annual change measured in percentage points, or extremely broad for a probability expressed on the raw scale.

| Parameter | Possible weakly informative prior | Necessary qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Standardized continuous effect | Normal(0, 1) | Outcome and predictor scaling must be documented |
| Logistic coefficient | Normal(0, 1.5) | Interpret implied changes in probability for realistic predictor contrasts |
| Standard deviation | Half-Normal(0, s) | Choose s on the observed outcome scale |
| Common population proportion | Beta(2, 2) or a context-specific Beta prior | Inspect prior predictive counts for the planned N |

These are templates, not universal defaults.

### Two construction routes

1. Begin with a reference or very broad prior and add enough information to prevent implausible predictions.
2. Begin with the strongest plausible substantive prior and widen it to acknowledge uncertainty and limited transportability.

Both routes should end with a prior predictive audit.

:::{admonition} Sociological demonstration: rare event prevalence
:class: tip
Suppose a severe administrative event is known to occur in fewer than 1 in 1,000 cases. A Uniform(0,1) prior is not weak in the relevant scientific sense: after observing 0 events in 100 cases, it gives Beta(1,101), whose mean is about 0.98%. That is almost ten times the upper prevalence believed plausible before the study.

A contextually weak prior should operate on the rare-event scale and should be checked by simulating event counts for the planned sample size.
:::

## 6. Nonregular Models and Boundary Estimation: The German Tank Problem

A fundamental challenge to the notion that "flat priors are always safe" arises in **nonregular models**, where the support of the observed data depends on the unknown parameter itself.

A classic example is estimating the total population size $N \in \{1, 2, \dots\}$ from a sample of $k$ serial numbers $x = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k\}$ drawn uniformly without replacement.

### The Likelihood

Let $M = \max(x_1, \dots, x_k)$ be the sample maximum. The probability of observing this specific set of $k$ distinct numbers is:

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_k \mid N) = \frac{1}{\binom{N}{k}} = \frac{k!(N-k)!}{N!} \qquad \text{for } N \ge M,
$$

and $0$ for $N < M$.

Because the likelihood is strictly decreasing for $N \ge M$, the Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) is $\widehat{N}_{\text{MLE}} = M$. However, the sample maximum $M$ is downward-biased on average ($\mathbb{E}[M] = \frac{k}{k+1}(N+1) - 1 < N$), even though it equals the exact population truth $N$ with non-zero probability $P(M = N) = k/N$.

### Frequentist Unbiased Estimator (MVUE)

The minimum-variance unbiased estimator corrects for the downward bias of the maximum:

$$
\widehat{N}_{\text{MVUE}} = M + \frac{M}{k} - 1 = M\left(1 + \frac{1}{k}\right) - 1.
$$

### Bayesian Posterior Analysis

1. **Under an Improper Flat Prior $p(N) \propto 1$ ($N \ge 1$):**

$$
p(N \mid x) = \frac{\frac{(N-k)!}{N!}}{\sum_{N'=M}^\infty \frac{(N'-k)!}{N'!}} = \frac{k-1}{k} \frac{\binom{M-1}{k-1}}{\binom{N}{k}} = (k-1) \frac{(M-1)!}{(M-k)!} \frac{(N-k)!}{N!} \qquad (N \ge M).
$$

For $k \ge 2$, the posterior sum is finite and proper. The posterior expectation is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[N \mid x] = (M - 1)\frac{k - 1}{k - 2} \qquad (\text{defined for } k > 2).
$$

*(Note: For $k = 1$, the flat prior produces an improper posterior; for $k = 2$, the posterior is proper but has an infinite expectation).*

2. **Under an Invariant Scale Prior $p(N) \propto 1/N$ (Jeffreys-like discrete prior):**

$$
p(N \mid x) \propto \frac{(N-k)!}{N \cdot N!} \qquad (N \ge M).
$$

For $k \ge 2$, the posterior expectation is finite. For the worked example ($M = 60, k = 4$), the exact numeric expectation is **$78.9$ tanks** (with large-$M$ asymptotic approximation $\mathbb{E}[N \mid x] \approx M \frac{k}{k - 1} = 80.0$).

*Core insights from this nonregular problem:*
- **Parameter-dependent support**: The hard lower boundary at $N = M$ violates standard Cramér–Rao regularity, causing standard asymptotic normality to fail.
- **Sensitivity of improper priors**: A flat prior on $N$ requires $k \ge 3$ observations just to obtain a finite posterior mean, whereas the scale prior $1/N$ provides sensible finite regularisation with only $k \ge 2$ observations.

## 8. When flat-prior Bayesian results resemble frequentist results

Numerical agreement arises in some exact conjugate models and, under regularity conditions, approximately in large samples. It does not arise because the Bayesian analysis has ceased to be Bayesian. The two frameworks continue to condition on different objects and assign different meanings to intervals and probabilities.

The full treatment is in {doc}`4.BayesianFrequentistConnections`.

## Key takeaways

- “Uninformative” is a goal or historical label, not a property that can be established by visual flatness.
- Uniform priors depend on parameterization.
- Small Beta shape parameters can increase commitment to boundary values rather than remove information.
- Improper priors require a proof or reliable check that the posterior is proper.
- Weakly informative priors should be calibrated on the outcome scale and checked through prior predictive simulation.
- Similar Bayesian and frequentist numerical answers do not have identical interpretations.

## Jeffreys Prior and Reparameterization

A prior that is uniform in one parameterization is generally not uniform in another. Jeffreys' rule addresses this problem for regular models by constructing a prior measure from the likelihood's Fisher information. Its main attraction is **invariance**, not the impossible promise of representing complete ignorance. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

## 1. Why uniformity depends on the scale

Suppose $\theta$ is a probability and

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).
$$

If $\eta = \operatorname{logit}(\theta)$, then

$$
\theta=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta),
\qquad
\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|=\theta(1-\theta).
$$

The induced density is

$$
p(\eta)=p(\theta)\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|
\propto\theta(1-\theta),
$$

which is not uniform on the log-odds scale.

:::{admonition} Intuition
:class: tip
A density is measured per unit of its coordinate system. Equal-width intervals in probability do not correspond to equal-width intervals in log-odds. “Flat” therefore has no meaning until the coordinate system is named.
:::

## 2. Scalar Jeffreys rule

For a regular model with one parameter $\theta$, the expected Fisher information for one observation is

$$
I(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{y\mid\theta}
\left[
\left(
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
\log p(y\mid\theta)
\right)^2
\right].
$$

Under standard regularity conditions it is also

$$
I(\theta)
=
-\mathbb{E}_{y\mid\theta}
\left[
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}
\log p(y\mid\theta)
\right].
$$

Jeffreys prior is

$$
\pi_J(\theta)\propto\sqrt{I(\theta)}.
$$

The square root of information defines a volume element that transforms correctly under a smooth one-to-one reparameterization. If $\phi = g(\theta)$, deriving the rule in $\phi$ gives the same prior measure as transforming the rule derived in $\theta$.

:::{warning}
Jeffreys prior does **not** generally put more weight where the likelihood is less informative. It is proportional to the square root of Fisher information. Its rationale is invariant measurement of parameter-space volume, not compensation for low information.
:::

## 3. Bernoulli and Binomial probability

For $y \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\theta)$,

$$
\log p(y\mid\theta)
=
y\log\theta+(1-y)\log(1-\theta).
$$

The Fisher information is

$$
I(\theta)=\frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)}.
$$

For $N$ independent observations the information is multiplied by $N$, which does not change the prior after normalization. Therefore,

$$
\pi_J(\theta)
\propto
\theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2},
$$

or

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}\left(\frac12,\frac12\right).
$$

This Jeffreys prior is proper and U-shaped. It assigns more density near 0 and 1 than the Uniform $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ prior. That shape is a consequence of the information geometry; it should not be described as a neutral belief that moderate and extreme probabilities are equally plausible.

*Figure 4.21: Bernoulli Fisher Information & Jeffreys Prior. The U-shaped density of $\text{Beta}(1/2, 1/2)$ arises from the square root of expected Fisher information near 0 and 1, ensuring volume invariance under reparameterization:*

<iframe src="../_static/fig4_21_jeffreys_bernoulli.html" width="100%" height="360px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

### Demonstration: probability versus log-odds

Transforming the Jeffreys density to $\eta = \operatorname{logit}(\theta)$ gives

$$
\pi_J(\eta)
=
\pi_J(\theta)
\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|
\propto
\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}.
$$

Deriving the Fisher information directly in $\eta$ gives

$$
I(\eta)=\theta(1-\theta),
$$

and therefore the same result. The densities have different shapes because they use different coordinates, but they represent the same prior measure.

````{dropdown} Question: Does invariance mean the density curve looks identical after transformation?
No. The curve changes because density is measured per unit of the new coordinate. Invariance means that deriving the Jeffreys rule directly in the new parameterization agrees with transforming the original prior using the Jacobian.
````

## 4. Normal-model examples

Jeffreys constructions must state which parameters are known and which are estimated.

### Unknown mean with known variance

For

$$
y\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2)
$$

with known $\sigma$,

$$
I(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2},
\qquad
\pi_J(\mu)\propto1.
$$

The prior is improper and flat over the location parameter.

### Unknown variance with known mean

Writing $v = \sigma^2$,

$$
I(v)=\frac{1}{2v^2},
\qquad
\pi_J(v)\propto\frac{1}{v}.
$$

Equivalently, the prior is flat in $\log(v)$.

### Unknown mean and variance together

For the two-parameter model, terminology matters:

- the full determinant-based Jeffreys rule gives

$$
\pi_J(\mu,\sigma)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^2},
$$

  equivalently $\pi_J(\mu, v) \propto v^{-3/2}$;

- a commonly used independent/reference prior gives

$$
\pi_R(\mu,\sigma)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma},
$$

  equivalently $\pi_R(\mu, v) \propto 1/v$.

The second specification produces the standard posterior $t$ interval for the Normal mean. It is often informally called “Jeffreys” in applied texts, but it is not identical to the full multiparameter Jeffreys rule. Multiparameter reference priors can depend on which parameter is treated as the primary inferential target. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

## 5. Sociological example: response probability

Suppose a pilot survey obtains 7 completed interviews from 10 sampled people. With Jeffreys prior,

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(0.5,0.5),
$$

the posterior is

$$
\theta\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{Beta}(7.5,3.5).
$$

This calculation estimates the response probability only under the model that the ten outcomes are exchangeable Bernoulli trials. It does not correct nonresponse bias: respondents and nonrespondents may differ systematically in the survey variables of interest.

:::{admonition} Demonstration block
:class: tip
Compare Jeffreys $\operatorname{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)$, Uniform $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$ and weakly informative $\operatorname{Beta}(2, 2)$ priors for the same $7/10$ responses. Then repeat with $70/100$ responses. The comparison shows how prior differences become smaller relative to the likelihood as information accumulates, while the interpretation of the response mechanism remains unchanged.
:::

## 6. Limitations and safe use

Jeffreys priors require care because:

1. they are often improper;
2. posterior propriety must be established;
3. multiparameter extensions are not unique in all practical senses;
4. improper priors cannot be used directly for prior predictive simulation;
5. ordinary Bayes factors are undefined when arbitrary normalizing constants from improper priors do not cancel;
6. invariance does not make a prior substantively appropriate for a particular population or decision.

For applied empirical work, a weakly informative proper prior is often easier to justify, diagnose and communicate. Jeffreys prior remains valuable as a reference analysis and as a demonstration of why parameterization matters.

- Uniformity is parameterization-dependent.
- In regular one-parameter models, Jeffreys prior is proportional to the square root of Fisher information.
- Invariance means equivalence after the correct change of variables, not identical density shapes.
- For a Binomial probability, Jeffreys prior is Beta(1/2,1/2).
- One-parameter and multiparameter Normal-model results must not be conflated.
- Jeffreys priors are reference constructions, not substitutes for substantive prior predictive reasoning.

## Fisher Information: Curvature and the Jeffreys Construction

Fisher information connects three ideas used throughout this session: the shape of the likelihood, the attainable precision of estimation, and the construction of the Jeffreys prior. It is best understood as a **local property of a statistical model for a specified parameter**, not as a universal score attached to a dataset.

### Learning goals

After this subchapter, you should be able to:

- distinguish the score, observed curvature, and expected Fisher information;
- explain why information adds across independent observations;
- compute Fisher information in simple Normal and Bernoulli models;
- use Fisher information to derive a scalar Jeffreys prior;
- explain why the amount of information depends on the parameterization and the estimand.

## From a broad likelihood to a sharp likelihood

Suppose that one observation has density $p(y\mid\theta)$ and log-likelihood

$$
\ell(\theta;y)=\log p(y\mid\theta).
$$

The **score** measures the local slope of the log-likelihood:

$$
s(\theta;y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ell(\theta;y).
$$

The expected Fisher information in one observation is

$$
I_1(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{y\mid\theta}\!\left[s(\theta;y)^2\right].
$$

Under the usual regularity conditions, this is also

$$
I_1(\theta)
=-\mathbb E_{y\mid\theta}\!\left[
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ell(\theta;y)
\right].
$$

For $N$ conditionally independent observations, Fisher information is additive: $I_N(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N I_i(\theta)$. When observations are additionally identically distributed (i.i.d.), each observation contributes equal information:

$$
I_N(\theta)=N I_1(\theta).
$$

```{note}
The two expressions for Fisher information are equivalent only under regularity conditions that permit differentiation under the integral sign and make the expected score equal to zero. Boundary parameters, changing support, mixtures, and other nonregular models require additional care.
```

### Expected versus observed information

The expected information averages over hypothetical datasets generated at $\theta$. The **observed information** uses the curvature of the log-likelihood for the dataset actually obtained:

$$
J_N(\theta;y)=-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ell_N(\theta;y).
$$

Near a well-identified maximum, a strongly curved log-likelihood changes rapidly as $\theta$ moves away from its best-fitting value; a shallow log-likelihood leaves a broader range of values compatible with the data. This geometric intuition is local: the full likelihood may still be asymmetric, multimodal, or bounded.

## Example 1: a Normal mean

Let

$$
y_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2 \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2),
$$

with known $\sigma$. For one observation,

$$
I_1(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2},
$$

and for $N$ independent observations,

$$
I_N(\mu)=\frac{N}{\sigma^2}.
$$

Thus, information about the mean grows linearly with sample size and decreases when individual observations are noisier. The corresponding standard-error scale decreases as $1/\sqrt{N}$, not as $1/N$.

```{admonition} Demonstration: sample size and likelihood curvature
:class: tip
Hold the sample mean fixed. Plot the Normal likelihood for $N=10$, $40$, and $160$ observations with the same known standard deviation. The peak remains at the same value, but its width shrinks by a factor of two whenever $N$ is multiplied by four.

Before plotting, predict which quantity changes linearly with $N$: the information, the variance scale, or the standard-error scale.
```

## Example 2: a binary social indicator

Suppose $y_i=1$ means that respondent $i$ supports a policy and

$$
y_i\mid p\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p).
$$

For the probability $p$ itself,

$$
I_1(p)=\frac{1}{p(1-p)}.
$$

This expression becomes large near 0 and 1. That statement is easy to misread. It does **not** mean that estimating a rare proportion is automatically easy in every scientifically relevant sense. It describes information in the coordinate $p$. If instead the parameter is the log-odds

$$
\eta=\operatorname{logit}(p),
$$

then

$$
I_1(\eta)=p(1-p),
$$

which is largest near $p=0.5$.

Fisher information is tied to the parameter being estimated. Statements such as “the data are most informative near 0.5” are incomplete unless they identify whether the target is a probability, a log-odds parameter, a regression coefficient, a threshold, or another transformation.

### A sociological reading

Imagine estimating support for a new policy. On the probability scale, changing support from 0.01 to 0.02 doubles the probability, although the absolute change is only one percentage point. On the log-odds scale, distances are represented differently. Neither scale is intrinsically correct: the estimand must follow the research question. This is why uncertainty should eventually be translated back to the substantively meaningful scale.

## From Fisher information to the Jeffreys prior

For a scalar parameter, the Jeffreys rule is

$$
\pi_J(\theta)\propto\sqrt{I_1(\theta)}.
$$

For the Bernoulli probability,

$$
\pi_J(p)\propto[p(1-p)]^{-1/2},
$$

which is the $\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$ density. For a Normal mean with known variance, $I_1(\mu)$ is constant in $\mu$, so the Jeffreys rule gives $\pi_J(\mu)\propto1$.

The rationale is **invariant volume under a smooth one-to-one reparameterization**. It is incorrect to say that the Jeffreys prior simply “compensates” for low information by putting more weight where a likelihood is flat: the formula is proportional to the square root of information, and its density changes with the coordinate system. The invariant object is the induced measure, not the numerical height of a density on one chosen scale. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

```{admonition} Check the invariance numerically
:class: exercise
1. Draw a grid of probabilities $p$ and evaluate the $\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$ density.
2. Transform each point to $\eta=\operatorname{logit}(p)$.
3. Include the Jacobian $dp/d\eta=p(1-p)$.
4. Verify that deriving the Jeffreys measure directly for $\eta$ gives the same probability assignments after transformation.

Repeat the calculation for a uniform density on $p$. Why is the transformed density not uniform on $\eta$?
```

## Fisher information is not a complete measure of evidence

Fisher information is useful but local. It does not by itself reveal:

- multiple separated likelihood modes;
- weak identification along combinations of parameters;
- model misspecification or measurement error;
- dependence among observations;
- whether an estimand answers the substantive research question;
- whether a posterior predictive distribution reproduces important features of the data.

The Cramér–Rao inequality also has a narrower scope than is sometimes suggested: under regularity conditions, it bounds the variance of unbiased estimators by the inverse information. It is not a guarantee that every estimator attains that bound, nor is it a general criterion for choosing a Bayesian model.

## What to retain

- Information is expected local curvature, expressed for a particular parameter.
- Independent contributions add; standard-error scales therefore usually decrease as $1/\sqrt{N}$.
- Observed and expected information are related but not identical.
- The scalar Jeffreys rule uses $\sqrt{I(\theta)}$ to construct a reparameterization-invariant measure.
- Information geometry does not replace prior predictive checking, posterior predictive checking, or substantive model criticism.
