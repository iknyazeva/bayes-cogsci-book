# Distribution Zoo: Choosing a Likelihood from the Data-Generating Process

A distribution is not selected because its histogram vaguely resembles the data. A likelihood represents how observations could have been generated **conditional on parameters and predictors**. Its support, mean-variance relationship, tail behavior, and treatment of zeros or boundaries must agree with the measurement process and the estimand.

This subchapter is a map, not an encyclopedia. It supplies the likelihood vocabulary needed to construct and simulate the data-generating process in Session 5. Detailed model fitting follows in later sessions.

## Learning objectives

After this subchapter, you should be able to:

- distinguish an outcome likelihood from a parameter prior;
- narrow the likelihood family using the unit of observation and measurement support;
- choose between Poisson and negative-binomial count models using their variance assumptions;
- distinguish ordinary zeros, structural-zero mixtures, and hurdle processes;
- identify when Normal, Student-$t$, lognormal, or Gamma models are plausible for continuous outcomes;
- use prior predictive simulation and posterior predictive checking to criticize distribution choice.

## 1. Begin with the observation, not the distribution name

Ask three questions in order.

1. **What is one observation?** A person-level response, household count, school mean, event duration, trial-level reaction time, or organization-year record?
2. **What values are possible?** Binary, nonnegative integer, bounded count, ordered category, real-valued, positive, or censored?
3. **What process creates variation?** Repeated trials, arrivals, multiplicative growth, latent thresholds, occasional contamination, selection, or structural absence?


Support is necessary but not sufficient. Several distributions can share the same support while representing different generative assumptions.

## 2. Likelihood map by measurement type

| Observation | Candidate likelihood | Defining assumption | Empirical example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binary response | Bernoulli | One conditionally exchangeable event with probability $p$ | Policy support; completion; correct response |
| Successes among $N$ trials | Binomial | Fixed $N$ and common conditional $p$ | Number supporting a proposal in a sampled group |
| Unbounded count | Poisson | Conditional mean equals conditional variance | Complaints per organization-month |
| Overdispersed count | Negative binomial | Variance exceeds mean through unobserved heterogeneity | Protest events across districts |
| Count with a separate zero process | Hurdle or zero-inflated count | Some zeros arise by a process different from positive counts | Organizations structurally unable to record an event |
| Ordered category | Ordered categorical/logit or probit | Categories reflect thresholds on an ordered latent scale | Likert response or interviewer rating |
| Nominal category | Categorical or multinomial | Mutually exclusive outcomes without natural order | Party choice among several parties |
| Approximately symmetric continuous value | Normal | Additive conditional variation with light symmetric tails | Standardized assessment score within a comparable group |
| Continuous value with occasional extremes | Student-$t$ | Symmetric but heavier-tailed conditional variation | Organization performance with occasional extreme deviations |
| Positive, multiplicative value | Lognormal | Log outcome has approximately additive variation | Income, duration, or expenditure |
| Positive, right-skewed value | Gamma | Positive outcome with variance increasing with its conditional mean | Waiting duration or monetary amount under a Gamma process |
| Event time with censoring | Exponential, Weibull, or survival model | Hazard process and censoring are modeled explicitly | Time to job exit or program dropout |
| Reaction time | Shifted lognormal, ex-Gaussian, or process model | Positive latency with a lower bound and right tail | Trial-level response latency |

The table identifies starting points. Dependence, clustering, predictors, measurement error, and missingness must be represented separately rather than expected to disappear through choice of a marginal distribution.

## 3. Counts: Poisson, negative binomial, and zero mechanisms

### Poisson

For a count $y_i$ observed over a comparable exposure,

$$
y_i\mid\lambda_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_i),
\qquad
E(y_i\mid\lambda_i)=\operatorname{Var}(y_i\mid\lambda_i)=\lambda_i.
$$

The equality concerns **conditional** mean and variance. Predictors or varying exposure can create large marginal variation even when the conditional Poisson model is reasonable.

### Negative binomial

A common mean-dispersion parameterization is

$$
E(y_i\mid\mu_i,\alpha)=\mu_i,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(y_i\mid\mu_i,\alpha)
=\mu_i+\frac{\mu_i^2}{\alpha}.
$$

The negative binomial can represent persistent unobserved heterogeneity or clustering that produces more variation than a Poisson likelihood permits. Parameterizations differ across software, so document whether the second parameter is a size, dispersion, or inverse-dispersion quantity.

### Zero-inflated and hurdle models

A zero-inflated model mixes a structural-zero state with an ordinary count distribution:

$$
P(y_i=0)=\pi_i+(1-\pi_i)f(0\mid\theta_i),
$$

$$
P(y_i=k>0)=(1-\pi_i)f(k\mid\theta_i).
$$

A hurdle model instead separates the probability of any event from the positive count distribution. These models should be motivated by a plausible process, not merely by observing many zeros.

```{admonition} Sociological example: complaints across organizations
:class: tip
Suppose many organizations record no formal complaints in a year. Three explanations imply different models:

1. complaints arise from one common Poisson process, and some organizations happen to observe zero;
2. rates vary between organizations, producing negative-binomial overdispersion;
3. some organizations have no functioning reporting mechanism, creating a distinct structural-zero process.

Plotting zeros is insufficient to choose among these explanations. Collection procedures and organizational context are part of the model.
```

## 4. Continuous outcomes: location, tails, positivity, and bounds

### Normal and Student-$t$

A Normal likelihood is a useful baseline for conditionally symmetric outcomes generated by many small additive contributions. A Student-$t$ likelihood adds a degrees-of-freedom parameter $\nu$ and heavier tails. It can reduce the influence of occasional extreme observations on location and scale estimates. {cite}`kruschke2015doing`

Heavy tails do not repair miscoding, dependence, skewness, censoring, or a mixture of substantively different populations. Extreme observations should first be checked as possible data errors and then modeled as part of a credible process.

### Lognormal and Gamma

If $y>0$ and multiplicative changes are scientifically meaningful, a lognormal model states

$$
\log y\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2).
$$

The exponentiated location parameter is related to the median, while the arithmetic mean also depends on $\sigma$. Coefficients on the log scale must therefore be translated carefully to the original scale.

A Gamma likelihood is also positive and right-skewed, but it imposes a different mean-variance relationship. Comparing prior and posterior predictive distributions on the original scale is more informative than choosing between Gamma and lognormal from density shapes alone.

### Bounded and censored outcomes

A bounded score can show floor or ceiling effects even when its middle appears roughly Normal. A continuous proportion can sometimes use a Beta likelihood, but exact zeros and ones require a transformation, a zero/one-inflated model, or a model for the underlying numerator and denominator. Censoring must be included in the likelihood rather than handled by deleting boundary cases.

## 5. Specialized example: ex-Gaussian reaction time

An ex-Gaussian random variable is the sum of independent Normal and Exponential components:

$$
X=Z+W,
\qquad
Z\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2),
\qquad
W\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda).
$$

Its mean and variance are

$$
E(X)=\mu+\frac{1}{\lambda},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^2+\frac{1}{\lambda^2}.
$$

This is a useful descriptive model for right-skewed reaction times, but the components should not automatically be interpreted as distinct cognitive mechanisms. A shifted lognormal, Gamma, ex-Gaussian, or evidence-accumulation model may answer different questions. Reaction-time models are therefore an optional cognitive-science extension rather than the organizing example for general empirical analysis.

## 6. A likelihood is not a prior

The same distribution name can play different roles. A Normal distribution may describe an outcome likelihood, a prior for a coefficient, or a population distribution of varying effects. These uses are not interchangeable.

| Parameter domain | Common proper prior family | Required check |
|---|---|---|
| Real-valued location or coefficient | Normal or Student-$t$ | Implied outcome changes for realistic predictor contrasts |
| Probability | Beta or logistic-Normal | Prior predictive success counts |
| Positive scale | Half-Normal, half-Student-$t$, or contextually scaled Exponential | Implied outcome variation and mass near zero |
| Positive rate | Gamma or lognormal | Implied event counts or waiting times |
| Dispersion | Distribution matched to software parameterization | Implied mean-variance relationship |

Inverse-Gamma priors remain important for understanding conjugate Normal models, but they should not be presented as universal default priors for variance. Uniformity is also parameterization-dependent and should not be labeled automatically non-informative. These issues are developed in {doc}`4.NonInformativePriors` and {doc}`5.NormalData`.

## 7. Distribution choice through simulation

Use the same workflow for every candidate likelihood:

1. define the unit of observation and its support;
2. state the proposed generating mechanism;
3. choose priors for its parameters;
4. simulate datasets at the planned sample size and design;
5. compare ranges, zeros, tails, group variation, and other meaningful features with substantive expectations;
6. fit plausible alternatives to real data;
7. use posterior predictive checks targeted at the differences between those alternatives;
8. report whether the substantive estimand changes.

```{warning}
A histogram of the pooled outcome is not a likelihood-selection algorithm. Conditional distributions can differ from marginal distributions because of predictors and grouping, and several generative processes can produce similar-looking histograms.
```

## 9. Guided practice

````{dropdown} Question 1: counts with many zeros
A dataset contains many organization-months with zero protest events. What evidence is needed before fitting a zero-inflated model?

**Answer.** A credible mechanism must distinguish structural zeros from sampling zeros. First assess exposure, organization heterogeneity, collection practices, and whether a negative-binomial model can reproduce the zeros without a separate latent state.
````

````{dropdown} Question 2: a positive skewed outcome
Household income is positive and strongly right-skewed. Why is “use a lognormal model” not yet a complete answer?

**Answer.** The analyst must specify whether the scientific target is a median, arithmetic mean, inequality contrast, tail probability, or prediction. Top-coding, zero income, sampling weights, and population mixtures can be more important than the choice between two smooth positive distributions.
````

````{dropdown} Question 3: an outlier
One school has an unusually high outcome. When is a Student-$t$ likelihood preferable to deleting the observation?

**Answer.** When the value is valid and extreme observations are a plausible part of the generating process. If the value is a coding or measurement error, the data problem should be corrected or modeled explicitly rather than hidden by a heavy-tailed likelihood.
````

## Key takeaways

- Choose a likelihood from the observation unit, support, and generating process.
- Support rules out impossible models but rarely identifies one unique model.
- Poisson, negative-binomial, hurdle, and zero-inflated counts represent different mechanisms.
- Normal, Student-$t$, lognormal, and Gamma likelihoods make different predictions about tails, scales, and extreme values.
- Likelihoods and priors are different components even when they use the same distribution family.
- Simulation and targeted predictive checks are the final judges of whether distributional implications are adequate for the research purpose.
