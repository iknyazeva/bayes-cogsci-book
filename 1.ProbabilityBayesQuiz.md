## Quiz

### 1. Interpretations of probability

Match each statement to the most relevant interpretation: **classical/symmetry**, **long-run frequency**, or **epistemic/Bayesian uncertainty**. Briefly justify each answer.

1. A fair four-option question gives a random guess a probability of $1/4$ of being correct.
2. In 100 sampled dental patients, 43 received a filling in the preceding year; the observed proportion is used to estimate the annual rate in a specified patient population.
3. After considering past elections, demographic change, and local knowledge, you assign a candidate a $3/4$ probability of winning the next election.
4. You favour one answer on a three-option question because it contains terminology repeatedly emphasized by the lecturer, so your probability is greater than $1/3$.

### 2. A discrete random variable

Two fair dice are rolled. Let $X$ be the number of dice showing six.

1. Find $P(X=0)$, $P(X=1)$, and $P(X=2)$.
2. Calculate $\mathbb E[X]$.
3. Is $X$ discrete or continuous?

### 3. Expected return and a fair bet

Your friend proposes a chess bet. If she wins, you pay her \$3. If you win, she pays you \$5. Let $p$ be her personal probability of winning.

1. Write her expected monetary return.
2. Find the value of $p$ that makes the bet fair to her.

### 4. Coherence and a Dutch book

Rain/overcast and sunny are treated as complementary events.

- Bet A: if it rains or is overcast, you pay your friend \$4; if it is sunny, your friend pays you \$6.
- Bet B: if it is sunny, you pay your friend \$5; otherwise, your friend pays you \$5.

1. What is your net return in either weather outcome if you accept both bets?
2. Bet A is fair to your friend only if they assign $P(\text{rain or overcast})=0.60$. Bet B is fair only if they assign $P(\text{sunny})=0.50$. Why are these beliefs incoherent?

### 5. Conditional probability: Titanic table

Use this historical aggregate table of passengers and crew:

| Survived | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | Crew |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Yes | 203 | 118 | 178 | 212 |
| No | 122 | 167 | 528 | 673 |

For a uniformly selected person in the table, calculate:

1. $P(\text{first class})$;
2. $P(\text{survived})$;
3. $P(\text{survived}\mid\text{first class})$;
4. whether first-class status and survival are independent.

### 6. Bayes' theorem: bags and marbles

One bag is selected with equal probability, then one marble is sampled from that bag.

- Bag A: 2 red and 3 blue.
- Bag B: 5 red and 1 blue.
- Bag C: 3 red and no blue.

Calculate:

1. $P(\text{blue})$;
2. $P(A\mid\text{blue})$;
3. $P(C\mid\text{blue})$;
4. $P(C\mid\text{red})$.

### 7. Cognitive screening and base rates

A cognitive screening tool has sensitivity $0.85$ and specificity $0.90$. In a memory-clinic population, impairment prevalence is $0.20$.

1. Calculate $P(\text{impairment}\mid +)$.
2. Calculate $P(\text{healthy}\mid -)$.
3. Explain why sensitivity is not the same as the probability of impairment after a positive result.

### 8. Changing prevalence

Keep sensitivity at $0.85$ and specificity at $0.90$, but reduce impairment prevalence to $0.05$. Calculate $P(\text{impairment}\mid +)$ and explain the change.

### 9. Prior information must include strength

A researcher observes 140 detections in 200 signal-detection trials. Use the explicitly stated prior

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(60,40).
$$

1. What is the sample proportion?
2. What is the posterior distribution under a Binomial likelihood?
3. What is the posterior mean, and why is it between the prior mean and sample proportion?

### 10. Central Limit Theorem

Suppose $X_i$ are independent Bernoulli outcomes with $P(X_i=1)=0.20$, and $\bar X$ is the event proportion across $n=100$ trials.

1. Give the CLT approximation to the sampling distribution of $\bar X$.
2. Does the CLT claim that each Bernoulli observation becomes Normal?
3. Would the same calculation automatically be valid for 100 strongly dependent trials from one participant?

## Answers and reasoning

:::{dropdown} 1. Interpretations of probability
1. **Classical/symmetry:** the four alternatives are treated as exchangeable under random guessing.
2. **Long-run frequency:** the observed rate is used to estimate a population frequency. The target population and sampling process still need to be stated.
3. **Epistemic/Bayesian uncertainty:** the probability represents uncertainty about one future event after combining several sources of information.
4. **Epistemic/Bayesian uncertainty:** the keyword changes the information state, so the options are no longer treated as exchangeable.

These interpretations are not competing answers to every problem. The important issue is what the probability refers to and how it is justified.
:::

:::{dropdown} 2. A discrete random variable
There are 36 ordered dice outcomes:

$$
P(X=0)=\frac{25}{36},\qquad
P(X=1)=\frac{10}{36},\qquad
P(X=2)=\frac{1}{36}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathbb E[X]
=0\frac{25}{36}+1\frac{10}{36}+2\frac{1}{36}
=\frac13.
$$

$X$ is discrete because its possible values are the countable set $\{0,1,2\}$.
:::

:::{dropdown} 3. Expected return and a fair bet
From the friend's perspective, the return is $+3$ if she wins and $-5$ if she loses:

$$
\mathbb E[R]=3p-5(1-p)=8p-5.
$$

The fair-bet condition is $8p-5=0$, so $p=5/8=0.625$.
:::

:::{dropdown} 4. Coherence and a Dutch book
If it rains or is overcast, you lose \$4 on Bet A and gain \$5 on Bet B: a net gain of \$1. If it is sunny, you gain \$6 on Bet A and lose \$5 on Bet B: again a net gain of \$1.

Because the two weather events are complementary, coherent probabilities must sum to 1. The friend's values sum to $0.60+0.50=1.10$, allowing the guaranteed loss.
:::

:::{dropdown} 5. Conditional probability: Titanic table
The total is $2201$, with $325$ first-class persons and $711$ survivors:

$$
P(\text{first})=\frac{325}{2201}\approx0.15,
$$

$$
P(\text{survived})=\frac{711}{2201}\approx0.32,
$$

$$
P(\text{survived}\mid\text{first})
=\frac{203}{325}\approx0.62.
$$

The events are not independent because $0.62\ne0.32$.
:::

:::{dropdown} 6. Bayes' theorem: bags and marbles
Using the law of total probability,

$$
P(\text{blue})
=\frac13\left(\frac35+\frac16+0\right)
=\frac{23}{90}\approx0.26.
$$

Then

$$
P(A\mid\text{blue})
=\frac{P(\text{blue}\mid A)P(A)}{P(\text{blue})}
=\frac{18}{23}\approx0.78.
$$

Because Bag C contains no blue marbles, $P(C\mid\text{blue})=0$. For a red marble,

$$
P(C\mid\text{red})
=\frac{(1)(1/3)}{(1/3)(2/5+5/6+1)}
=\frac{30}{67}\approx0.45.
$$
:::

:::{dropdown} 7. Cognitive screening and base rates
The true-positive probability is $0.85(0.20)=0.17$, while the false-positive probability is $(1-0.90)(0.80)=0.08$. Hence

$$
P(\text{impairment}\mid +)
=\frac{0.17}{0.17+0.08}
=0.68.
$$

For a negative result,

$$
P(\text{healthy}\mid -)
=\frac{0.90(0.80)}{0.90(0.80)+(1-0.85)(0.20)}
=\frac{0.72}{0.75}
=0.96.
$$

Sensitivity is $P(+\mid\text{impairment})$; the requested positive predictive value reverses the conditioning and also depends on prevalence.
:::

:::{dropdown} 8. Changing prevalence
With prevalence $0.05$,

$$
P(\text{impairment}\mid +)
=\frac{0.85(0.05)}{0.85(0.05)+0.10(0.95)}
=\frac{0.0425}{0.1375}
\approx0.31.
$$

Positive results contain more false positives relative to true positives when the condition is rare. Test properties alone do not determine the probability after observing a result.
:::

:::{dropdown} 9. Prior information must include strength
The sample proportion is $140/200=0.70$. Beta–Binomial updating gives

$$
\theta\mid y
\sim\operatorname{Beta}(60+140,40+60)
=\operatorname{Beta}(200,100).
$$

The posterior mean is

$$
\mathbb E[\theta\mid y]
=\frac{200}{300}
=\frac23
\approx0.667.
$$

It lies between the prior mean $0.60$ and sample proportion $0.70$ because the posterior combines the prior's stated concentration with 200 new trials. Merely saying that earlier studies were “around 0.60” would not determine this compromise; prior strength is essential.
:::

:::{dropdown} 10. Central Limit Theorem
For Bernoulli outcomes, $\mu=p=0.20$ and $\sigma^2=p(1-p)=0.16$. Thus

$$
\bar X\approx
\operatorname{Normal}\!\left(0.20,\frac{0.16}{100}\right),
$$

with standard error $\sqrt{0.16/100}=0.04$.

The individual observations remain binary; the approximation concerns the distribution of the sample proportion across repetitions. Strong within-participant dependence violates the simple independence assumption and changes the effective information, so the same calculation is not automatic.
:::
