# Part 6.2: Markov chains

MCMC replaces independent simulation with a dependent sequence whose long-run distribution is the desired target. To understand what can go right or wrong, we need to distinguish a transition rule, an invariant distribution, and the realised chain.

## 1. The Markov property

A sequence $X_0,X_1,\ldots$ is a first-order Markov chain when

$$
P(X_{t+1}\in A\mid X_t,X_{t-1},\ldots,X_0)
=P(X_{t+1}\in A\mid X_t).
$$

If the transition rule is the same at every iteration, the chain is **time-homogeneous**. This is not the same as stationarity.

- **Time-homogeneous:** the transition kernel does not change with $t$.
- **Stationary:** the marginal distribution of $X_t$ is unchanged by a transition.

If $X_t\sim\pi$ and a transition leaves $X_{t+1}\sim\pi$, then $\pi$ is an **invariant distribution** of the transition kernel. A chain may be time-homogeneous without being started in stationarity.

## 2. A finite-state example

Consider movement among three residential states: central city, inner suburb, and outer suburb. The transition matrix is

$$
Q=
\begin{pmatrix}
0.7 & 0.3 & 0.0\\
0.2 & 0.6 & 0.2\\
0.0 & 0.4 & 0.6
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Rows describe the current state and columns the next state. The invariant distribution satisfies

$$
\pi=\pi Q,
\qquad
\sum_j\pi_j=1,
$$

which gives

$$
\pi=\left(\frac{4}{13},\frac{6}{13},\frac{3}{13}\right).
$$

Starting from the central city does not make the first few distributions stationary. Repeated multiplication by $Q$ moves the distribution toward $\pi$. In this example, self-transitions make the chain aperiodic and all states communicate, so long-run convergence is straightforward.

```python
import numpy as np

Q = np.array([
    [0.7, 0.3, 0.0],
    [0.2, 0.6, 0.2],
    [0.0, 0.4, 0.6],
])
p0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
p20 = p0 @ np.linalg.matrix_power(Q, 20)
```

The migration story is only an analogy. In MCMC, states are parameter values and the transition rule is deliberately designed so that the posterior is invariant.

## 3. Why an invariant distribution is not enough

An invariant distribution alone does not guarantee useful simulation. Intuitively, the chain must also be able to reach all relevant regions and must not be trapped in a deterministic cycle. For finite-state chains, irreducibility and aperiodicity provide familiar sufficient conditions. Continuous-state chains require corresponding recurrence and ergodicity conditions.

Detailed balance,

$$
\pi(x)K(x,x')=\pi(x')K(x',x),
$$

is a convenient sufficient condition for invariance and motivates Metropolis–Hastings. It is not necessary for every valid MCMC algorithm.

## 4. Autocorrelation and effective information

Consider the stationary autoregressive chain

$$
X_{t+1}=\phi X_t+\varepsilon_t,
\qquad
\varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2),
\qquad |\phi|<1.
$$

Its invariant distribution is

$$
X_t\sim\mathcal N\left(0,\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}\right),
$$

and its lag-$k$ autocorrelation is $\rho_k=\phi^k$. With $\phi=0.20$, the chain forgets its current value quickly. With $\phi=0.95$, it moves slowly and many iterations contain comparatively little new information.

For the mean of a stationary scalar chain, an elementary approximation is

$$
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
\approx
\frac{S}{1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_k}.
$$

This formula provides intuition, not a complete modern diagnostic procedure. Rank-based bulk and tail ESS are more robust, and ESS can differ across parameters, transformations, and estimands. Negative autocorrelation can even produce an ESS larger than the nominal draw count for some summaries.

## 5. Mixing, modes, and misleading traces

A visually stable trace can still be wrong. If a posterior has two separated modes, one chain may remain in the left mode and another in the right. Each trace can look stationary within its own region while neither represents the full posterior.

This is why MCMC assessment uses multiple chains initialized from different states, rank comparisons, and estimand-specific ESS rather than relying on a single “fuzzy caterpillar” trace.

## 7. Guided practice

````{dropdown} Question 1: Time-homogeneity vs. stationarity
A transition matrix is constant across time. What additional fact would be needed before calling a realised chain stationary?

**Answer.** Its current marginal distribution must already be invariant under the transition kernel ($p_t = \pi$). A constant transition matrix establishes time homogeneity, not stationarity of the realised chain.
````

````{dropdown} Question 2: Identical histograms, differing precision
Two simulations have the same number of draws and the same marginal histogram. Why might their Monte Carlo errors differ substantially?

**Answer.** Their serial dependence may differ. Strong positive autocorrelation lowers effective sample size ($\text{ESS} \ll S$) and increases MCSE even when the marginal histograms look similar.
````
