In [Lesson 5](5.MonteCarloToMCMC.md) a walker produced draws from the survey posterior without ever computing the hard denominator. A walker can also fail: it can be too slow, start in the wrong place, get stuck on one hill, or produce draws that are too similar to each other. Today we learn to **run** a sampler in PyMC, to **check** whether its draws can be trusted, and to recognise two fast **shortcuts**, the Laplace approximation and variational inference.

## Part A — How a walker can fail, and how we notice

### 1. Step size: not too small, not too large

The walker proposes steps of some typical size. Too small: almost every step is accepted, but the walker crawls. Too large: almost every step is rejected, and the walker stands still for long stretches.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_step_size.html" width="100%" height="735px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** which walker accepts the most proposals? Which one gives the best histogram?

**What to notice:** the tiny-step walker accepts 98% of its proposals, yet 1,000 steps are worth only about **3** independent draws. A high acceptance rate is **not** a sign of a good sampler. PyMC tunes the step size automatically during warm-up.

### 2. Warm-up: first find the hill

A walker that starts far away needs some steps to reach the hill. These first steps do not represent the posterior.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_warmup.html" width="100%" height="655px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** including the warm-up steps adds a false bump at low support (red). PyMC calls these steps `tune`; it uses them to adapt the step size and then **discards** them. Only the steps after warm-up (`draws`) are reported.

### 3. Several walkers: do they tell the same story? (R-hat)

One walker cannot tell us that it has missed something. So we run **four chains** from different starting points. If they explore the same distribution, their traces overlap like "hairy caterpillars" and their histograms lie on top of each other. **R-hat** compares the variation *between* chains with the variation *within* chains {cite}`gelman1992inference,vehtari2021rank`.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_four_chains.html" width="100%" height="655px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** in "Not converged", the four walkers take tiny steps from 20%, 40%, 70% and 90%. Will R-hat be close to 1?

**Course rule:** R-hat ≤ **1.01** for every reported quantity.

### 4. Sticky draws: the effective sample size (ESS)

Each step of a walker starts from the previous one, so neighbouring draws are similar. 2,000 similar draws carry less information than 2,000 independent ones. The **effective sample size** (ESS) says how many independent draws they are worth.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_sticky_ess.html" width="100%" height="635px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** with very sticky steps, 2,000 draws are worth about 2 independent draws, and the similarity bars (right) stay high for many lags. With lively steps the bars drop to zero quickly and ESS is large.

**Course rule:** ESS ≥ **400** for every reported quantity, both in the middle of the distribution ("bulk ESS") and in its tails ("tail ESS").

### 5. Width versus wobble: Monte Carlo error

Recall Lesson 5: more draws make our *estimate* more precise, but they do not make the *posterior* narrower. The **Monte Carlo standard error** (MCSE) measures the wobble of an estimate between runs. Tail quantities, such as the ends of a 95% interval, wobble more than the average because few draws land in the tails.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_mcse_wobble.html" width="100%" height="695px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** the posterior's width (dashed line) stays at about 4.8 points no matter how many draws we take. The wobble of the average falls steadily, and the tail (red) always wobbles more. Report numbers only to a precision the MCSE supports. If the MCSE of a mean is 0.001, writing 57.73% is pretending.

### 6. Coverage: does the sample cover the whole posterior?

Some posteriors have **more than one hill**. Suppose the data tell us only how *large* an effect is, not its *sign*: then +2 and −2 are equally plausible. A single walker finds one hill and stays there. Its trace looks perfectly healthy, but it covers only half of the posterior.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_coverage_two_hills.html" width="100%" height="615px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** with one chain nothing looks wrong. Four chains started in different places land on different hills, and R-hat = 1.74 raises the alarm. This is the main reason for running several chains from dispersed starting points. Good coverage means every region with substantial probability has been visited, in the right proportion.

## Part B — PyMC does the walking

### 7. Fitting the survey in PyMC

PyMC builds the walker for us. We write down the **model**, which is the same generative story as in Lesson 3:

```python
import pymc as pm
import arviz as az

with pm.Model() as survey_model:
    support = pm.Beta("support", alpha=2, beta=2, shape=2)          # prior for both groups
    pm.Binomial("supporters", n=[100, 100], p=support,
                observed=[58, 40])                                    # likelihood
    idata = pm.sample(draws=1000, tune=1000, chains=4, random_seed=2026)

az.summary(idata)
```

`pm.sample()` runs **NUTS**, the "skateboarder" from Lesson 5, Section 14. `tune=1000` is the warm-up, `draws=1000` are the kept steps per chain, and `chains=4` gives four walkers. The result `idata` holds 4 × 1,000 draws of both support values.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_pymc_vs_exact.html" width="100%" height="615px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** PyMC's draws (bars) match the exact Beta posteriors (dashed). The probability that the gap exceeds 5 points is the same as in Lesson 4, about 0.96. From here on, **you use PyMC's draws exactly like the draws in Lessons 4–5**: compute the quantity for each draw, then count or average.

```python
draws = idata.posterior["support"].values.reshape(-1, 2)   # 4,000 rows, 2 columns
gap = draws[:, 0] - draws[:, 1]
print("P(gap > 5 points):", (gap > 0.05).mean())
```

### 8. The trust-check card

Check the card **before** you interpret any number. Do this every time, for every model, in every later lesson.

| Question | Where to look | 🟢 may interpret | 🔴 do not interpret |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do the chains agree? | `r_hat` column; trace plot of four chains | R-hat ≤ 1.01 and overlapping traces | R-hat > 1.01 or separated traces |
| Is there enough information? | `ess_bulk`, `ess_tail` columns; `mcse_mean` | ESS ≥ 400; MCSE small compared with the precision you report | ESS < 400 |
| Did the sampler warn? | divergence count, `idata.sample_stats["diverging"].sum()` | 0 divergences | any divergence |

```python
def trust_check(idata, var):
    r = float(az.rhat(idata, var_names=[var])[var].max())
    e = float(az.ess(idata, var_names=[var])[var].min())
    d = int(idata.sample_stats["diverging"].sum())
    ok = (r <= 1.01) and (e >= 400) and (d == 0)
    print(f"R-hat {r:.3f} | ESS {e:.0f} | divergences {d} ->", "may interpret" if ok else "DO NOT interpret")
```

:::{important} Green lights are necessary, not sufficient
Passing the card means the **computation** can be trusted. It does not mean that the **model** is right: that requires predictive checks (Lessons 3–4 and later). The thresholds are course screening rules, not proofs of convergence.
:::

### 9. Gallery: what failed fits look like

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_broken_fits.html" width="100%" height="655px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

| Case | What happened | Light | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Healthy fit | survey model, 4 × 1,000 draws | 🟢 R-hat 1.00, ESS ≈ 4,400, 0 divergences | interpret |
| Too few draws | only 25 kept draws per chain | 🔴 ESS ≈ 95 | draw more |
| Chains disagree | two hills (only the size of the effect is identified) | 🔴 R-hat ≈ 1.7 | rethink the model: what can the data actually tell? |
| Divergences | a hierarchical model with a "funnel" (the classic eight-schools example {cite}`rubin1981estimation`) | 🔴 205 divergences in the figure (the number changes with the random seed) | do not report; the model needs to be reparameterised (later lessons) or ask for help |

**What to notice:** in the divergence case the red dots cluster at the narrow neck of a funnel-shaped posterior. The skateboarder cannot follow such sharp curvature. More draws do **not** fix divergences or disagreeing chains; they fix only low ESS caused by too few draws.

## Part C — Shortcuts: approximate the hill instead of walking it

Sampling is general and becomes exact with enough draws, but it can be slow for very large models. Since Laplace, people have also tried the second road from Lesson 5: replace the posterior with a **simple shape**. Two such methods appear in PyMC and in many papers, so you should recognise their names. The formulas are in [Going further: Laplace approximation and variational inference](6.VariationalInference.md).

### 10. Laplace approximation: a bell at the top of the hill

Laplace's 1774 idea {cite}`laplace1774memoire,tierney1986accurate`:

1. Find the highest point of the posterior, the **posterior mode** or **MAP** estimate (*maximum a posteriori*). In PyMC: `pm.find_MAP()`.
2. Measure how sharply the hill bends at the top (its **curvature**).
3. Draw a Normal bell with that centre and that bend.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_laplace.html" width="100%" height="515px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**Predict first:** will a symmetric bell fit the tiny pilot (1 supporter of 10 households) as well as the full survey?

**What to notice:** with 100 households the bell is almost exact (95% interval 48.3–67.4% versus the exact 48.1–67.0%). With 10 households the posterior is skewed and pressed against 0%. The bell puts **6%** of its probability on impossible negative support and misses the long right tail. Laplace is fast and works well with plenty of data and symmetric hills. It fails with small samples, skewed posteriors and boundaries. Extensions of the idea power fast methods such as INLA {cite}`rue2009approximate` and the frequentist mixed-model software you may already know.

### 11. Variational inference: the bell that adjusts itself

**Variational inference** (VI) also uses a simple shape, but it finds the best one by **optimisation** {cite}`jordan1999introduction,blei2017variational`:

1. Choose a **family** of simple shapes, e.g. all bells. PyMC first transforms bounded unknowns to an unbounded scale, so its bells respect 0% and 100%.
2. Measure how far a bell is from the posterior with the **Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence** {cite}`kullback1951information`.
3. Move and stretch the bell until the distance is as small as possible.

We cannot compute the KL distance directly: it contains the same unknown denominator. Instead VI **maximises** the **ELBO** (*evidence lower bound*). Raising the ELBO is exactly the same as shrinking the KL distance. In PyMC: `pm.fit()` runs **ADVI**, automatic differentiation variational inference {cite}`kucukelbir2017automatic`.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_vi_fit.html" width="100%" height="715px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** the bell starts in the wrong place (20%) and slides toward the posterior while the ELBO climbs and levels off. The remaining KL distance falls from about 27 to below 0.1.

### 12. When the bell is too simple: mean-field VI

The fastest kind of VI, **mean-field** VI, gives each unknown its own independent bell, so it ignores links between unknowns. When two unknowns move together, the approximation becomes **too narrow**: the model is more confident than the data allow.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_meanfield.html" width="100%" height="575px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

**What to notice:** with a correlation of 0.9 between the two unknowns, mean-field VI reports less than half of the true spread (0.44 instead of 1.00). **Full-rank** VI lets the bell tilt and fixes this example, at a higher computational cost. It is still only a bell.

### 13. One question, five ways

<iframe src="../_static/lesson6_method_map.html" width="100%" height="555px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

| Method | 95% interval, full survey (58/100) | 95% interval, tiny pilot (1/10) | Speed | Exact with enough effort? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exact formula (Lessons 2–4) | 48.1–67.0% | 5.0–45.4% | instant | yes, but only for special models |
| Grid (Lesson 5) | matches exact | matches exact | fast for 1–2 unknowns | yes, but explodes with more unknowns |
| MCMC, `pm.sample()` | 48.4–66.9% | 4.7–45.9% | slower | **yes**, with more draws |
| Laplace, `pm.find_MAP()` + bell | 48.3–67.4% | **−4.4–37.8%** | very fast | no |
| VI, `pm.fit()` | 47.6–67.5% | 5.6–**50.5%** | fast | no |

*(Values from the figure above, generated with PyMC; MCMC and VI vary slightly from run to run.)*

**Course default:** use MCMC (`pm.sample()`) and the trust-check card. Treat Laplace and VI as fast shortcuts that must be checked against MCMC before you rely on them. When you read "MAP estimate", "Laplace approximation", "variational Bayes" or "ELBO" in a paper, you now know what was done and what can go wrong.

Every result you report from now on starts with one sentence about computation:

> *Posterior draws were obtained with NUTS in PyMC (4 chains, 1,000 warm-up and 1,000 retained draws each). All reported quantities had R-hat ≤ 1.01 and bulk and tail ESS ≥ 400, and there were no divergent transitions.*

If a check fails, say so and do not interpret the affected quantities.

## Exit ticket

::::{dropdown} 1. A sampler accepted 97% of its proposals. Good news?
Not necessarily. Very small steps are almost always accepted but move slowly, so the draws are sticky and ESS is low. Look at ESS and the traces, not at the acceptance rate.
::::

::::{dropdown} 2. R-hat = 1.00 and ESS = 3,000, but PyMC reports 12 divergences. Can you report the result?
No. Divergences mean the sampler could not follow part of the posterior, so the draws may miss a region. The card needs all three lights green.
::::

::::{dropdown} 3. Would running 10 times more draws fix the "two hills" case?
No. Each chain stays on its own hill no matter how long it runs. The problem lies in what the model and data can identify, and more draws do not change that.
::::

::::{dropdown} 4. A paper reports a Laplace approximation from a sample of 12 people and an outcome near 0%. What would you worry about?
The bell can put probability on impossible values and miss skewness when samples are small and the posterior is close to a boundary. Ask whether the result was checked with MCMC.
::::
