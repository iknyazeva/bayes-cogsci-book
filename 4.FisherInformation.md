# Fisher Information and Its Role in Jeffreys Prior

## What is Fisher Information?

**Fisher Information** is a fundamental concept in statistics that measures the amount of information that an observable random variable $X$ carries about an unknown parameter $\theta$ of a statistical model. It provides insight into how sensitive the likelihood function is to changes in the parameter.

```{important}
**Fisher Information.** Fisher Information $I(\theta)$ is defined as:

$$
I(\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log f(X | \theta) \right)^2 \right]
$$

where $f(X | \theta)$ is the likelihood function, and the expectation is taken over the distribution of $X$ given $\theta$. Equivalently, under regularity conditions:

$$
I(\theta) = -\mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \log f(X | \theta) \right]
$$

High Fisher information means the data is highly informative about $\theta$; low Fisher information means the data tells us little.
```

### Interpretation of Fisher Information

1. **Sensitivity**: A higher Fisher Information indicates that small changes in $\theta$ will cause larger changes in the likelihood function, suggesting that the data provides more information about $\theta$.

2. **Precision**: Fisher Information can be seen as a measure of the precision of an estimator. More information about the parameter typically results in smaller variance for the estimator.

3. **Inverse Relation**: The Fisher Information is inversely related to the variance of an estimator. In general, if the Fisher Information is high, the variance of the estimator for $\theta$ will be low, which means we can estimate $\theta$ more precisely.

### Curvature of the Likelihood Function

The **curvature of the likelihood function** is closely related to Fisher Information. Specifically, the Fisher Information can be viewed as a measure of the curvature of the log-likelihood function around the true parameter value:

- A **steep curvature** in the log-likelihood function (high Fisher Information) indicates that the likelihood changes rapidly with respect to changes in $\theta$. This means the data provides a lot of information about the parameter.
- A **flat curvature** (low Fisher Information) suggests that the likelihood function does not change much with small changes in $\theta$, indicating less information about the parameter.

```{note}
**Curvature intuition.** Think of the log-likelihood as a landscape. High Fisher information corresponds to a sharp, well-defined peak — you can pinpoint the parameter value precisely. Low Fisher information corresponds to a flat, broad hilltop — many parameter values are nearly equally compatible with the data. The Jeffreys prior places more prior weight where the likelihood is less informative (flat), compensating for the difficulty of learning the parameter in that region.
```

## Jeffreys Prior: Basis and Significance

The **Jeffreys Prior** is a non-informative prior that aims to provide a way of assigning prior probabilities that is invariant under reparameterization of the model. It is defined as:

$$
\pi(\theta) \propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}
$$

where $I(\theta)$ is the Fisher Information.

### Why Use Fisher Information for Jeffreys Prior?

1. **Invariance**: Since Fisher Information is derived from the likelihood function, using it as the basis for the Jeffreys prior ensures that the prior is invariant under transformations of the parameter space. This means that regardless of how we parameterize the model, the prior information remains consistent.

2. **Information Content**: The use of the square root of the Fisher Information provides a prior that reflects the amount of information available about the parameter. If a parameter is more sensitive to changes (higher Fisher Information), the Jeffreys prior will assign it more weight.

3. **Improper Priors**: Jeffreys Prior often results in improper priors (those that do not integrate to one), but they can still yield proper posterior distributions when combined with sufficient data. This property allows researchers to conduct Bayesian inference even when the prior itself is not proper.

4. **Objective Bayesian Approach**: The Jeffreys prior is a cornerstone of **objective Bayesian** statistics, where the goal is to minimize the amount of subjective information entered through the prior. By relying on Fisher Information, Jeffreys Prior allows the data to dominate the inference process.

### Examples

- For the normal distribution, the Fisher Information for the mean $\mu$ (with known variance $\sigma^2$) is given by $I(\mu) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}$. Thus, the Jeffreys prior for $\mu$ is uniform:

  $$
  \pi(\mu) \propto 1
  $$

- For the variance $\sigma^2$, the Fisher Information is  $I(\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}$. The Jeffreys prior for $\sigma^2$ is:

  $$
  \pi(\sigma^2) \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^2}
  $$

- For a Bernoulli distribution with parameter $\theta$, the Fisher Information is $I(\theta) = \frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)}$, leading to the Jeffreys prior:

  $$
  \pi(\theta) \sim \text{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)
  $$

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example: Fisher Information in Psychophysics
:class: tip
In a psychophysical detection task, a participant's responses at a given stimulus level follow a Bernoulli distribution with detection probability $\theta$. The Fisher information $I(\theta) = 1/[\theta(1-\theta)]$ is highest near $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = 1$ and lowest at $\theta = 0.5$ (chance performance).

However, the goal in psychophysics is usually to estimate a **threshold** parameter (e.g., the stimulus intensity at which the participant achieves 75% correct), not $\theta$ itself. The information about the threshold depends on the **slope of the psychometric function** — and this slope is steepest near the inflection point (typically around 75% correct for a 2AFC task). This is why adaptive staircase methods target that performance level: they maximize information about the *threshold*, not about the Bernoulli success probability at a fixed stimulus.

This distinction illustrates an important general point: Fisher information depends on *which parameter* you are trying to estimate, and reparameterizing from $\theta$ to threshold changes where the information is concentrated.
```

## Key Takeaways

- **Fisher Information** $I(\theta)$ quantifies how much a dataset can tell us about a parameter — it is the expected curvature of the log-likelihood.
- Higher Fisher information means the data is more informative and the parameter can be estimated more precisely.
- The **Jeffreys prior** $\pi(\theta) \propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}$ uses Fisher information to construct a prior that is invariant to reparameterization.
- Fisher information is connected to the **Cramér-Rao bound**: no unbiased estimator can have variance less than $1/I(\theta)$.

# Invariance Under Reparameterization


When we say that a prior is **invariant under reparameterization**, we mean that the resulting inferences remain consistent regardless of how the parameters are expressed. If we transform a parameter $\theta$ to a new parameterization $\phi = g(\theta)$, the Jeffreys prior automatically adjusts via the change-of-variables formula, producing the same posterior inferences in either parameterization.

This property is crucial in Bayesian statistics because it ensures that the choice of prior is not an artifact of the particular way we happen to write down the model.

## Example: Variance vs. Precision

Consider a Normal distribution with unknown variance $\sigma^2$. We can equivalently parameterize the model in terms of:

- **Variance**: $\sigma^2$, or
- **Precision**: $\tau = 1/\sigma^2$

### Jeffreys Prior for Variance

The Fisher information for the variance parameter is $I(\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}$, giving the Jeffreys prior:

$$
\pi(\sigma^2) \propto \sqrt{I(\sigma^2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma^2} \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^2}
$$

### Transforming to Precision

If we reparameterize to precision $\tau = 1/\sigma^2$, we apply the change-of-variables formula. Since $\sigma^2 = 1/\tau$ and $|d\sigma^2/d\tau| = 1/\tau^2$:

$$
\pi(\tau) = \pi(\sigma^2) \cdot \left|\frac{d\sigma^2}{d\tau}\right| = \frac{1}{1/\tau} \cdot \frac{1}{\tau^2} = \frac{1}{\tau}
$$

### Deriving Jeffreys Prior for Precision Directly

Alternatively, the Fisher information for the precision parameter is $I(\tau) = \frac{1}{2\tau^2}$, giving:

$$
\pi(\tau) \propto \sqrt{I(\tau)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\tau} \propto \frac{1}{\tau}
$$

The two approaches give the **same** result: $\pi(\tau) \propto 1/\tau$. This is the invariance property in action — whether we derive the Jeffreys prior in the $\sigma^2$ parameterization and transform, or derive it directly in the $\tau$ parameterization, we get the same prior.

```{note}
**Contrast with uniform priors.** A uniform prior on $\sigma^2$ ($\pi(\sigma^2) \propto 1$) transforms to $\pi(\tau) \propto 1/\tau^2$ — which is *not* uniform in $\tau$. Conversely, a uniform prior on $\tau$ transforms to $\pi(\sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^4$ — not uniform in $\sigma^2$. Uniform priors are inherently tied to a specific parameterization, while Jeffreys priors are not.
```

## Importance of Invariance

The invariance under reparameterization ensures that our statistical analyses are robust and not dependent on arbitrary modeling choices. It allows us to focus on the underlying relationships in the data rather than getting caught up in the specifics of how we choose to express the model.
