# Fisher Information: Curvature and the Jeffreys Construction

Fisher information connects three ideas used throughout this session: the shape of the likelihood, the attainable precision of estimation, and the construction of the Jeffreys prior. It is best understood as a **local property of a statistical model for a specified parameter**, not as a universal score attached to a dataset.

## Learning goals

After this subchapter, you should be able to:

- distinguish the score, observed curvature, and expected Fisher information;
- explain why information adds across independent observations;
- compute Fisher information in simple Normal and Bernoulli models;
- use Fisher information to derive a scalar Jeffreys prior;
- explain why the amount of information depends on the parameterization and the estimand.

## From a broad likelihood to a sharp likelihood

Suppose that one observation has density $p(y\mid\theta)$ and log-likelihood

$$
\ell(\theta;y)=\log p(y\mid\theta).
$$

The **score** measures the local slope of the log-likelihood:

$$
s(\theta;y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ell(\theta;y).
$$

The expected Fisher information in one observation is

$$
I_1(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{y\mid\theta}\!\left[s(\theta;y)^2\right].
$$

Under the usual regularity conditions, this is also

$$
I_1(\theta)
=-\mathbb E_{y\mid\theta}\!\left[
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ell(\theta;y)
\right].
$$

For $N$ conditionally independent observations,

$$
I_N(\theta)=N I_1(\theta).
$$

```{note}
The two expressions for Fisher information are equivalent only under regularity conditions that permit differentiation under the integral sign and make the expected score equal to zero. Boundary parameters, changing support, mixtures, and other nonregular models require additional care.
```

### Expected versus observed information

The expected information averages over hypothetical datasets generated at $\theta$. The **observed information** uses the curvature of the log-likelihood for the dataset actually obtained:

$$
J_N(\theta;y)=-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ell_N(\theta;y).
$$

Near a well-identified maximum, a strongly curved log-likelihood changes rapidly as $\theta$ moves away from its best-fitting value; a shallow log-likelihood leaves a broader range of values compatible with the data. This geometric intuition is local: the full likelihood may still be asymmetric, multimodal, or bounded.

## Example 1: a Normal mean

Let

$$
y_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2 \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2),
$$

with known $\sigma$. For one observation,

$$
I_1(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2},
$$

and for $N$ independent observations,

$$
I_N(\mu)=\frac{N}{\sigma^2}.
$$

Thus, information about the mean grows linearly with sample size and decreases when individual observations are noisier. The corresponding standard-error scale decreases as $1/\sqrt{N}$, not as $1/N$.

```{admonition} Demonstration: sample size and likelihood curvature
:class: tip
Hold the sample mean fixed. Plot the Normal likelihood for $N=10$, $40$, and $160$ observations with the same known standard deviation. The peak remains at the same value, but its width shrinks by a factor of two whenever $N$ is multiplied by four.

Before plotting, predict which quantity changes linearly with $N$: the information, the variance scale, or the standard-error scale.
```

## Example 2: a binary social indicator

Suppose $y_i=1$ means that respondent $i$ supports a policy and

$$
y_i\mid p\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p).
$$

For the probability $p$ itself,

$$
I_1(p)=\frac{1}{p(1-p)}.
$$

This expression becomes large near 0 and 1. That statement is easy to misread. It does **not** mean that estimating a rare proportion is automatically easy in every scientifically relevant sense. It describes information in the coordinate $p$. If instead the parameter is the log-odds

$$
\eta=\operatorname{logit}(p),
$$

then

$$
I_1(\eta)=p(1-p),
$$

which is largest near $p=0.5$.

```{important}
Fisher information is tied to the parameter being estimated. Statements such as “the data are most informative near 0.5” are incomplete unless they identify whether the target is a probability, a log-odds parameter, a regression coefficient, a threshold, or another transformation.
```

### A sociological reading

Imagine estimating support for a new policy. On the probability scale, changing support from 0.01 to 0.02 doubles the probability, although the absolute change is only one percentage point. On the log-odds scale, distances are represented differently. Neither scale is intrinsically correct: the estimand must follow the research question. This is why uncertainty should eventually be translated back to the substantively meaningful scale.

## From Fisher information to the Jeffreys prior

For a scalar parameter, the Jeffreys rule is

$$
\pi_J(\theta)\propto\sqrt{I_1(\theta)}.
$$

For the Bernoulli probability,

$$
\pi_J(p)\propto[p(1-p)]^{-1/2},
$$

which is the $\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$ density. For a Normal mean with known variance, $I_1(\mu)$ is constant in $\mu$, so the Jeffreys rule gives $\pi_J(\mu)\propto1$.

The rationale is **invariant volume under a smooth one-to-one reparameterization**. It is incorrect to say that the Jeffreys prior simply “compensates” for low information by putting more weight where a likelihood is flat: the formula is proportional to the square root of information, and its density changes with the coordinate system. The invariant object is the induced measure, not the numerical height of a density on one chosen scale. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

```{admonition} Check the invariance numerically
:class: exercise
1. Draw a grid of probabilities $p$ and evaluate the $\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$ density.
2. Transform each point to $\eta=\operatorname{logit}(p)$.
3. Include the Jacobian $dp/d\eta=p(1-p)$.
4. Verify that deriving the Jeffreys measure directly for $\eta$ gives the same probability assignments after transformation.

Repeat the calculation for a uniform density on $p$. Why is the transformed density not uniform on $\eta$?
```

## Fisher information is not a complete measure of evidence

Fisher information is useful but local. It does not by itself reveal:

- multiple separated likelihood modes;
- weak identification along combinations of parameters;
- model misspecification or measurement error;
- dependence among observations;
- whether an estimand answers the substantive research question;
- whether a posterior predictive distribution reproduces important features of the data.

The Cramér–Rao inequality also has a narrower scope than is sometimes suggested: under regularity conditions, it bounds the variance of unbiased estimators by the inverse information. It is not a guarantee that every estimator attains that bound, nor is it a general criterion for choosing a Bayesian model.

## What to retain

- Information is expected local curvature, expressed for a particular parameter.
- Independent contributions add; standard-error scales therefore usually decrease as $1/\sqrt{N}$.
- Observed and expected information are related but not identical.
- The scalar Jeffreys rule uses $\sqrt{I(\theta)}$ to construct a reparameterization-invariant measure.
- Information geometry does not replace prior predictive checking, posterior predictive checking, or substantive model criticism.
