# Continuous Outcomes and Robust Models

## 1. Introduction & The Continuous Measurement Process

In applied empirical research, choosing a likelihood for a continuous outcome $y \in \mathbb{R}$ is not a mere curve-fitting exercise—it is a **substantive scientific claim** about the underlying data-generating and measurement process. 

When evaluating a continuous variable (e.g., reaction times, blood pressure, income, test scores, or physical constants), we must explicitly characterize:
1. **The Operational Support**: Is the outcome strictly positive ($y > 0$), bounded on an interval $[a, b]$, or theoretically unbounded $(-\infty, \infty)$?
2. **The Aggregation Mechanism**: Do perturbations accumulate additively (leading to Gaussian central limit behavior) or multiplicatively (leading to lognormal or heavy-tailed skew)?
3. **Extreme Observations & Outliers**: Are extreme deviations corrupted recording errors, or are they genuine, informative realizations from a heavy-tailed physical process?
4. **The Target Estimand**: Are we interested in the population location (mean vs. median), tail risk quantiles, group contrasts, or predictions for future individual units?

## 2. The Normal Location-Scale Baseline

The standard Gaussian model serves as our baseline when additive, symmetric, light-tailed conditional variation is plausible:

$$
y_i \mid \mu, \sigma \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu, \sigma), \quad i = 1, \dots, N
$$

In Session 5, we examined the analytic conjugacy of this model when variance was known. In empirical practice, both the **location $\mu$** and the **scale $\sigma$** are unknown parameters estimated jointly:

$$
p(\mu, \sigma \mid y) \propto p(\mu, \sigma) \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \exp\left( -\frac{(y_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)
$$

### Proper, Scale-Aware Priors
Under weakly informative modeling principles (Gelman et al., *ROS* Ch. 9):
- **Location Prior**: $\mu \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_0, \sigma_0)$, centered on domain-plausible baseline values.
- **Scale Prior**: $\sigma \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda)$ or $\sigma \sim \operatorname{Half-Normal}(0, s)$, ensuring strict positivity without the pathological infinite-spike behavior of inverse-gamma priors at $\sigma \to 0$.

```python
import pymc as pm

with pm.Model() as normal_baseline_model:
    # Scale-aware priors on observable units
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=100.0, sigma=15.0)
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=10.0)
    
    # Gaussian Likelihood
    y_obs = pm.Normal("y_obs", mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=y_data)
    
    # Inference
    idata_norm = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.9)
```

## 3. Robust Student-$t$ Likelihood & Tail Thickness

The fatal weakness of the Gaussian likelihood is its **quadratic penalty** in the log-likelihood: $\log p(y_i \mid \mu, \sigma) \propto -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(y_i - \mu)^2$. A single extreme observation exerts an **unbounded pulling force** on the estimated location $\mu$ and dramatically inflates the estimated scale $\sigma$.

To model data with heavy tails, unmodeled contamination, or rare extreme events without arbitrarily deleting data points, we replace the Gaussian likelihood with a **Student-$t$ distribution**:

$$
y_i \mid \mu, \sigma, \nu \sim t_\nu(\mu, \sigma)
$$

with probability density function:

$$
p(y_i \mid \mu, \sigma, \nu) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu + 1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right) \sqrt{\pi \nu} \sigma} \left[ 1 + \frac{1}{\nu}\left( \frac{y_i - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2 \right]^{-\frac{\nu + 1}{2}}
$$

### The Degrees-of-Freedom Parameter $\nu$
The parameter $\nu > 0$ controls the **tail thickness**:
- When $\nu = 1$, the distribution becomes the **Cauchy distribution** (tails so heavy that neither the mean nor the variance mathematically exist).
- When $\nu \in [3, 7]$, the distribution exhibits heavy tails robust to severe outliers.
- As $\nu \to \infty$, $t_\nu(\mu, \sigma) \to \operatorname{Normal}(\mu, \sigma)$.

```{important}
**Interpretation of Scale $\sigma$:**
In a Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu > 2$, the standard deviation is **not** $\sigma$, but rather $\operatorname{SD}(Y) = \sigma \sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}}$. The parameter $\sigma$ represents the *scale* (width of the central bell), not the total variance.
```

## 4. Empirical Case Study: Newcomb's Speed of Light

In 1882, Simon Newcomb conducted $N = 66$ experimental measurements of the time taken for light to travel from his laboratory at Fort Myer, Virginia, to a mirror at the Washington Monument and back (distance $\approx 7.4$ km). 

The recorded values are deviations in nanoseconds: 64 observations cluster neatly between $+16$ and $+40$ (mean $\approx 27.7$), but two extreme negative outliers are present: **$-2$** and **$-44$**.

### PyMC Robust Model Implementation

```python
with pm.Model() as robust_newcomb_model:
    # Priors
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=25.0, sigma=10.0)
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=10.0)
    
    # Gamma prior on nu (concentrating mass between 2 and 30)
    nu = pm.Gamma("nu", alpha=2.0, beta=0.1)
    
    # Robust Student-t Likelihood
    y_obs = pm.StudentT("y_obs", nu=nu, mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=newcomb_data)
    
    # Sample posterior
    idata_robust = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.95)
```

**Comparison of Posterior Inferences:**
- **Gaussian Model**: $\hat{\mu} = 26.2 \pm 1.3$, $\hat{\sigma} = 10.8 \pm 1.0$. (The outlier $-44$ pulls the estimated speed down and inflates uncertainty threefold!).
- **Robust Student-$t$ Model**: $\hat{\mu} = 27.5 \pm 0.6$, $\hat{\sigma} = 4.9 \pm 0.6$, $\hat{\nu} \approx 3.2$. (The model downweights $-44$ as a 5-standard-deviation tail event, preserving the precision of the remaining 64 measurements).

## 5. Positive and Right-Skewed Outcomes

Many empirical quantities in cognitive science and social science are strictly positive ($y > 0$) with long right tails (e.g., reaction times, saccade latencies, income, sentence reading times):

1. **Lognormal Model**: $\log(y_i) \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu, \sigma)$
   - Appropriate for **multiplicative growth** processes.
   - Note: The median on the original scale is $\exp(\mu)$, whereas the arithmetic mean is $\exp(\mu + \sigma^2/2)$.
2. **Gamma Model**: $y_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ or parameterized by mean $\mu$ and shape $\alpha$:
   - Appropriate when standard deviation scales proportionally with the mean ($\operatorname{SD}(Y) \propto \mu$).
3. **Shifted Lognormal / Ex-Gaussian**:
   - Decomposes reaction times into a sensory-motor transmission shift $\delta$ plus a decision process.

## 6. Group Contrasts with Heterogeneous Variance

When comparing two groups (e.g., Cognitive Control vs. Baseline), standard classical tests often enforce equal variance. In Bayesian workflow, we allow **group-specific scales** $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ and robust tails:

$$
y_{i, j} \sim t_\nu(\mu_j, \sigma_j), \quad j \in \{1, 2\}
$$

```python
with pm.Model() as robust_two_group_model:
    # Separate group locations and scales
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=0.0, sigma=10.0, shape=2)
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=5.0, shape=2)
    nu = pm.Gamma("nu", alpha=2.0, beta=0.1)
    
    # Likelihood
    y_obs = pm.StudentT("y_obs", nu=nu, mu=mu[group_idx], sigma=sigma[group_idx], observed=y_data)
    
    # Derived substantive contrast on observable outcome scale
    diff_means = pm.Deterministic("diff_means", mu[1] - mu[0])
    ratio_sd = pm.Deterministic("ratio_sd", sigma[1] / sigma[0])
    cohen_d = pm.Deterministic("effect_size", (mu[1] - mu[0]) / pm.math.sqrt((sigma[0]**2 + sigma[1]**2)/2))
```

## 7. Posterior Predictive Model Checking

Before accepting any continuous model, we must run **targeted posterior predictive checks** to audit:
1. **Replicated Extreme Minima/Maxima**: Does the Gaussian model generate negative reaction times or impossible speed values?
2. **Tail Asymmetry**: Does the symmetric Student-$t$ fit properly if the true data-generating mechanism has positive skew?

```python
# Generate synthetic replications from fitted posterior
ppc = pm.sample_posterior_predictive(idata_robust, model=robust_newcomb_model)
```

## Key Takeaways

- Distribution choice is a **structural assumption** about data generation, not a decorative heuristic.
- The **Student-$t$ likelihood** provides automatic Bayesian robustness against valid extreme values without discarding data.
- For positive, skewed outcomes, always evaluate predictions on the **original outcome scale**, distinguishing medians $\exp(\mu)$ from arithmetic means $\exp(\mu + \sigma^2/2)$.
- Group comparisons should permit **heterogeneous variances** ($\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2$) as the default baseline.
