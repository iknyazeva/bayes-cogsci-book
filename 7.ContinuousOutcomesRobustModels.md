## 1. From Proportions to Continuous Measurements

In Lessons 2–6, we worked mostly with proportions: how many people supported a housing subsidy out of 100 surveyed. But empirical research in psychology, cognitive science, and social sciences constantly deals with **continuous quantities**:
- Child cognitive development scores (e.g., KidIQ scores from 20 to 140).
- Reaction times in computer-based decision tasks (in milliseconds).
- Annual personal earnings or blood pressure.

When our outcome $y$ is continuous ($y \in \mathbb{R}$), we cannot just count "successes" and "failures." We need a probability density that describes:
1. **The Typical Value (Location $\mu$)**: where does the mass of observations sit?
2. **The Spread (Scale $\sigma$)**: how widely do individual measurements scatter around that center?

The simplest, most familiar candidate is the **Normal (Gaussian) distribution**.

---

## 2. The Normal Location-Scale Model

When additive, symmetric variation is plausible, we assume each observed data point $y_i$ is drawn from a Normal distribution:

$$
y_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu, \sigma), \quad i = 1, \dots, N
$$

Here, $\mu$ represents the population mean, and $\sigma$ represents the standard deviation (measurement spread).

```
                 The Normal Model
                     ___
                    /   \
                   /  |  \
             _____/   |   \_____
                      μ
             <------ 2σ ------>
```

### Choosing Scale-Aware Priors
In applied Bayesian modeling, we choose priors that make sense in the **actual units of measurement** (Gelman et al., *ROS*, Ch. 9):
- **For location $\mu$**: We center our prior around a domain-plausible baseline. If test scores are standardized to roughly 100 with a standard deviation of 15, we might choose $\mu \sim \operatorname{Normal}(100, 15)$.
- **For scale $\sigma$**: Spread must be strictly positive ($\sigma > 0$). A $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(0, 15)$ or $\operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda)$ prior gently encourages reasonable positive spreads without imposing hard barriers.

```python
import pymc as pm
import numpy as np
import arviz as az

with pm.Model() as normal_baseline:
    # Priors in observable test-score units
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=100.0, sigma=15.0)
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=15.0)
    
    # Gaussian Likelihood
    y_obs = pm.Normal("y_obs", mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=test_scores)
    
    idata_norm = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.9)
```

This model is clean, fast, and familiar. But what happens when real data contain extreme surprises?

## 3. What to Do with Outliers? Newcomb’s Speed of Light

To see why a bell curve can fail, consider a universal dilemma: **what happens when a few observations deviate wildly from the rest?**

### The Intuitive Scenario
Imagine measuring reaction times across 66 experimental trials:
- On **64 trials**, the participant responds consistently between $250\text{ ms}$ and $350\text{ ms}$ ($\text{mean} \approx 280\text{ ms}$).
- On **1 trial**, the participant sneezes, blinks, or gets distracted, and the recorded latency is **$1{,}500\text{ ms}$**.

Under a Normal likelihood, the probability of an observation $y_i$ drops off with squared distance:

$$
p(y_i \mid \mu, \sigma) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{(y_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)
$$

Because of this **quadratic penalty**, the Normal model considers an observation 5 standard deviations away to be an astronomical emergency. To make $1{,}500\text{ ms}$ remotely plausible, the model compromises: it **drags the estimated mean $\mu$ upward** and **inflates $\sigma$**, ruining our estimate of the participant's typical cognitive speed.

### The Historical Benchmark: Simon Newcomb (1882)
In 1882, the astronomer Simon Newcomb measured the time required for light to travel $7.4\text{ km}$ from Fort Myer to the Washington Monument and back ($N = 66$). 

Recorded deviations from $24{,}800$ nanoseconds were:
- **64 measurements** clustered tightly between $+16$ and $+40$ ($\text{mean} \approx +27.7$).
- **2 measurements** were extreme negative outliers: **$-2$** and **$-44$** (likely caused by optical vibrations or mirror misalignment).

```
   Newcomb's Measurements:
   [-44]  ...  [-2]  ...  [+16  +20  +24  +27  +28  +31  +36  +40]
     ^                      ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
   Outlier                      64 Consistent Measurements
```

If we fit a Gaussian model, the single $-44$ measurement drags the estimated mean from $27.5$ down to $26.2$, and nearly **triples** the estimated spread $\sigma$ from $4.9$ to $10.8$!

```
Normal Likelihood:    Outlier drags the mean down and inflates the spread.
Student-t Likelihood: Outlier is recognized as a heavy-tail event; central mean stays intact.
```

## 4. The Robust Solution: Student-$t$ Distribution

Rather than arbitrarily deleting extreme observations (which distorts scientific uncertainty), Bayesian modeling replaces the Gaussian likelihood with a **Student-$t$ distribution**:

$$
y_i \sim t_\nu(\mu, \sigma)
$$

The parameter $\nu > 0$ represents the **degrees of freedom**, which acts as our **outlier tolerance dial**:
- **When $\nu \to \infty$**: The Student-$t$ becomes identical to the standard Normal distribution.
- **When $\nu \in [3, 7]$**: The distribution has thick ("heavy") tails. It expects occasional wild extremes and therefore refuses to let them drag the center $\mu$ or inflate the scale $\sigma$.
- **When $\nu = 1$**: The distribution becomes a Cauchy distribution, with tails so heavy that the mathematical mean does not even exist.

```{important}
**Scale vs. Standard Deviation:**
In a Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu > 2$, the total standard deviation is $\operatorname{SD} = \sigma \sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}}$. The parameter $\sigma$ represents the *scale* (width of the central bell), not the total variance.
```

### PyMC Implementation
In PyMC, making our model robust to outliers requires changing exactly one line:

```python
with pm.Model() as robust_model:
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=25.0, sigma=15.0)
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=15.0)
    
    # Prior on outlier tolerance: Gamma(2, 0.1) concentrates mass between nu=2 and nu=30
    nu = pm.Gamma("nu", alpha=2.0, beta=0.1)
    
    # Robust Student-t likelihood
    y_obs = pm.StudentT("y_obs", nu=nu, mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=newcomb_data)
    
    idata_robust = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.95)
```

The data themselves inform the posterior of $\nu$: when outliers are present, the posterior over $\nu$ concentrates at low values ($\nu \approx 3.2$), automatically protecting our inferences without subjective data pruning.

## 5. Posterior Predictive Check: The Replicated Minimum

How do we check if our model is adequately handling extremes? We run a **targeted Posterior Predictive Check (PPC)**.

We generate synthetic datasets $y^{\text{rep}}$ from both the Normal and Student-$t$ posteriors, and inspect the test statistic $T(y) = \min(y)$:
- Under the **Normal model**, synthetic minima rarely reach below $-5$. The model cannot generate a value as extreme as $-44$ without distorting the entire distribution.
- Under the **Student-$t$ model**, synthetic datasets easily produce extreme tail values like $-44$ while keeping the central bulk of predictions centered at $+27.5$.

```python
# Generate synthetic replications from the fitted model
ppc_robust = pm.sample_posterior_predictive(idata_robust, model=robust_model)
```

## 6. Comparing Two Groups: KidIQ Child Scores

Now let us add our second core capability: **comparing two groups**.

In empirical cognitive research, we rarely measure just one sample. We want to know: *Do children whose mothers finished high school have higher test scores than children whose mothers did not?*

In the classic KidIQ dataset ($N = 434$, Gelman et al., *ROS*, Ch. 10):
- Group 0 (`mom_hs = 0`): Mothers without high school diploma ($n_0 = 93$).
- Group 1 (`mom_hs = 1`): Mothers with high school diploma ($n_1 = 341$).
- Outcome $y$: Child test score at age 3–4.

### Heterogeneous Variances by Default
Standard classical $t$-tests often assume equal variances ($\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$). In Bayesian modeling, we allow each group to have its own location **and** its own scale:

$$
y_{i, 0} \sim t_\nu(\mu_0, \sigma_0), \quad y_{i, 1} \sim t_\nu(\mu_1, \sigma_1)
$$

```python
with pm.Model() as two_group_model:
    # Separate locations and scales for each group
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=80.0, sigma=20.0, shape=2)
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=20.0, shape=2)
    nu = pm.Gamma("nu", alpha=2.0, beta=0.1)
    
    # Likelihood with group indexing
    y_obs = pm.StudentT("y_obs", nu=nu, mu=mu[group_idx], sigma=sigma[group_idx], observed=child_scores)
    
    # Substantive contrasts on the observable outcome scale:
    diff_means = pm.Deterministic("diff_means", mu[1] - mu[0])     # Score difference in points
    ratio_sigma = pm.Deterministic("ratio_sigma", sigma[1] / sigma[0]) # Ratio of spreads
    cohen_d = pm.Deterministic("cohen_d", (mu[1] - mu[0]) / pm.math.sqrt((sigma[0]**2 + sigma[1]**2)/2.0))
    
    idata_groups = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.95)
```

### Deciding Without p-Values: The HDI + ROPE Framework
Rather than asking if $p < 0.05$, we inspect the **95% Highest Density Interval (HDI)** of the difference $\Delta\mu = \mu_1 - \mu_0$:
- The posterior mean difference is approximately $+11.8$ points ($95\%\text{ HDI}: [8.1, 15.5]$).
- If we define a **Region of Practical Equivalence (ROPE)** of $[-2, +2]$ points (differences so small as to be practically negligible), the entire 95% HDI lies far to the right of the ROPE. We conclude with high certainty that maternal high school education is associated with a practically meaningful increase in child test scores.

## 7. The Conceptual Bridge: Two Groups Is a Regression!

Notice something remarkable about our two-group model.

If we write the expected child score as a function of the binary group indicator $z_i \in \{0, 1\}$:

$$
\mu_i = \alpha + \beta z_i
$$

then:
- For children of mothers without high school ($z_i = 0$): $\mu_i = \alpha = \mu_0 \approx 78.0$.
- For children of mothers with high school ($z_i = 1$): $\mu_i = \alpha + \beta = \mu_1 \approx 89.8$.
- The slope coefficient $\beta = \mu_1 - \mu_0 = \Delta\mu \approx +11.8$ points!

```
                Two-Group Comparison AS A Line
         Score
          100 |                       • (Group 1 mean: α + β)
              |                      /
           80 |  • (Group 0 mean: α)/
              |  |                 |
              +--+-----------------+----> mom_hs (z)
                 0                 1
```

A two-sample comparison is **not** a separate branch of statistics. It is simply a linear regression with a single $0/1$ predictor.

This directly unlocks Lesson 8: what happens when our predictor $x$ is not just $0$ or $1$, but a continuous number like maternal IQ?

## 8. What If the Outcome Is Strictly Positive?

Before moving to regression lines, a word on physical constraints: what if an outcome cannot be negative?

Quantities like reaction times, reading durations, or household income are strictly positive ($y > 0$) and typically right-skewed. For such data:
1. **Never use an unconstrained Normal model** if predictions could generate negative times.
2. The most common solution is modeling on the **log scale**: $\log(y_i) \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu, \sigma)$.
3. **Watch out for means vs. medians**: On the original scale, the median is $\exp(\mu)$, but the arithmetic mean is $\exp(\mu + \sigma^2/2)$. At large $\sigma$, the mean sits far above the median. Always state which summary you report!

## 10. Concept Check

```{dropdown} Concept Check: Deleting Outliers vs. Heavy-Tailed Likelihoods
A researcher proposes removing all reaction times beyond 2.5 standard deviations from the sample mean before fitting a Normal model. Why does the Bayesian workflow recommend fitting a Student-$t$ model instead?

**Answer:** Trimming data using an arbitrary cutoff (like 2.5 SDs) artificially deflates uncertainty, discards genuine information about process variability, and introduces researcher degrees of freedom. In contrast, the Student-$t$ model retains all data points, lets the data estimate the tail-thickness parameter $\nu$, and automatically downweights extreme perturbations without subjective intervention.
```

## Key Takeaways

- A continuous outcome model requires estimating both a **center ($\mu$)** and a **spread ($\sigma$)**.
- The **Student-$t$ distribution** adds an outlier tolerance dial ($\nu$), preventing extreme observations from distorting parameter estimates.
- Two-group comparisons should default to **heterogeneous variances** ($\sigma_0 \neq \sigma_1$).
- A two-group comparison is mathematically identical to a **linear regression with a binary $0/1$ predictor**.
