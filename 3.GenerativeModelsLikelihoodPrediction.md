## 1. Recap: what changes when we observe data?

Start with an unfinished example from Lesson 2. Then consider a survey in which 13 of 20 respondents support a policy.

Discuss before calculating:

1. What is observed, and what are we uncertain about?
2. What is held fixed when we evaluate the likelihood?
3. What else is needed to obtain a posterior distribution?

:::{dropdown} Answers
The observed count is $k=13$ among $N=20$ respondents. The unknown parameter $\theta$ is the support probability represented by our model. To evaluate the likelihood, we hold the observed count and sample size fixed and vary $\theta$. A posterior also requires a prior and normalization, as in Lesson 2.
:::

## 2. A model for repeated surveys

A **generative model** specifies how to produce possible observations. It lets us ask what results we would expect if its assumptions held.

For this example, suppose that:

- each respondent gives one binary answer: support ($1$) or no support ($0$);
- each survey has 20 respondents;
- responses are conditionally independent given a common support probability $\theta$.

An individual response follows a Bernoulli distribution. The total number of supporters follows a Binomial distribution:

$$
k\mid N,\theta\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(N,\theta).
$$

| Quantity | Meaning in this example |
|---|---|
| $\theta$ | Support probability; we choose its value for simulation and estimate it when analyzing data |
| $N=20$ | Number of respondents in each survey |
| $k$ | Number of supporters, an integer from 0 to 20 |
| $k_{\text{sim}}$ | A count generated by the model |

```{mermaid}
flowchart LR
    A["Choose N = 20 and theta = 0.65"] --> B["Draw a Binomial count"]
    B --> C["Repeat to generate many surveys"]
    C --> D["Inspect the resulting counts"]
```

The arrows show the simulation steps. A model that generates survey responses does not, by itself, explain what causes people to support a policy.

**Check the study design.** Shared households, repeated responses from the same people, or differences between groups can make this simple model inadequate. A simulation also cannot establish whether respondents represent the target population; that depends on sampling and nonresponse.

## 3. Run the model and inspect the results

Set $\theta=0.65$. Across repeated surveys of 20 people, the expected count is $20\times0.65=13$. This is an average across repetitions; individual surveys can have more or fewer supporters.

**Before running:** Will every simulated survey contain exactly 13 supporters?

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
theta = 0.65
N = 20
n_repetitions = 1_000

counts = rng.binomial(n=N, p=theta, size=n_repetitions)
proportions = counts / N

print("First ten counts:", counts[:10])
print("Expected count:", N * theta)
print("Average simulated count:", counts.mean())
print("Average simulated proportion:", proportions.mean())
print("SD of simulated proportions:", proportions.std(ddof=1))
```

Each draw represents a new survey under the same assumptions. Running more simulated surveys improves our description of what the model produces; it supplies no new evidence about the real population.

### Change one thing

Keep $\theta=0.65$ and change $N$ from 20 to 200. Predict what will happen to the simulated **proportions**, then rerun the code. Compare their average and spread. Use proportions because the possible counts also increase with $N$.

:::{dropdown} What to look for
Both sets of proportions should center near 0.65. Proportions from surveys of 200 people should vary less. A larger survey still contains different individual answers; its aggregate proportion is more stable.

Small differences between the simulated average and 0.65 are expected with finitely many repetitions.
:::

### What would make us reconsider the model?

Check the range first: a survey of 20 respondents cannot have 23 supporters or a negative count. Then ask whether the amount and pattern of variation are plausible. A model can produce only valid counts and still miss differences between groups or dependence among responses.

Simulation helps expose problems in the chosen distribution, parameter values, or code. Plausible simulated data alone do not establish that a model is adequate for the research question.

## 4. Return to the likelihood

In simulation, we fixed $\theta$ and generated different counts. Now hold the observed count $k=13$ fixed and compare two candidate values of $\theta$.

| Candidate support probability | Probability of exactly 13 supporters among 20 |
|---|---:|
| $\theta=0.65$ | 0.1844 |
| $\theta=0.50$ | 0.0739 |

The likelihood ratio is $0.1844/0.0739\approx2.49$. The observed count is about 2.49 times as probable under $\theta=0.65$ as under $\theta=0.50$.

This does **not** mean that $\theta=0.65$ has posterior probability 0.1844, or that one candidate is 2.49 times as probable as the other. The posterior also depends on the prior.

:::{dropdown} Answer
For this Binomial model, $k/N$ maximizes the likelihood. Other parameter values can also generate the observed count. The maximum likelihood estimate is a single estimate, not a statement that uncertainty has disappeared.
:::

## 5. What remains uncertain?

In the simulation, we chose $\theta=0.65$, yet the survey results varied. With real data, we also have uncertainty about $\theta$ itself.

| Source of uncertainty | Question |
|---|---|
| Variation in observations at a fixed parameter | If support probability were 0.65, how much would new survey results vary? |
| Uncertainty about the parameter | Given the observed survey, which support probabilities remain plausible? |

More informative observations can reduce uncertainty about $\theta$. They do not make an individual person's answer certain. Dependence, biased sampling, or a poorly specified model can also limit what we learn from a larger dataset.

In Lesson 4, we will predict new observations by drawing a parameter value from the posterior and then simulating a new sample at that value. This includes both sources of uncertainty. The [Stan User’s Guide](https://mc-stan.org/docs/2_38/stan-users-guide/posterior-prediction.html) describes this sampling procedure.
