# Normal Models: Baseline, Conjugacy and Predictive Consequences

The Normal model is a useful first continuous-outcome model because its updating can be derived exactly and its parameters have familiar interpretations. It is not a default justified by the word “continuous.” The scientific claim is that, conditional on the predictors and grouping structure in the model, observations vary approximately symmetrically around a location through an additive, light-tailed process.

This subchapter develops the closed-form Normal baseline used for simulation and parameter-recovery exercises. Robust and positive-outcome alternatives are introduced here and developed more fully in Session 7.

## Learning objectives

After this subchapter, you should be able to:

- state the conditional assumptions of a Normal likelihood;
- derive the Normal-Normal posterior when observation variance is known;
- interpret precision-weighted updating and its model-specific prior effective sample size;
- distinguish uncertainty about a population mean from prediction of a new observation;
- derive the Normal-Inverse-Gamma update when mean and variance are unknown;
- identify predictive patterns that motivate Student-$t$, lognormal, Gamma, censored, or mixture alternatives.

## 1. When is a Normal likelihood plausible?

For observations $y_i$,

$$
y_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2)
$$

asserts more than real-valued support. Conditional on the model:

- values are centered symmetrically around $\mu$;
- the scale $\sigma$ summarizes light-tailed additive variation;
- observations are conditionally independent unless dependence is modeled elsewhere;
- extreme values are as rare as the Normal tails imply;
- there is no unmodeled floor, ceiling, censoring, or mixture process.

The marginal histogram of $y$ need not be Normal. For example, group differences can make a pooled outcome multimodal even when within-group residuals are approximately Normal. Distributional checking should therefore examine conditional residuals and replicated datasets, not only the pooled outcome.

| Outcome pattern | Why a simple Normal likelihood may fail | Candidate response |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetric with occasional valid extremes | Tails too light | Student-$t$ likelihood |
| Positive and right-skewed | Wrong support and symmetry | Lognormal or Gamma likelihood |
| Bounded score with a ceiling | Predicts impossible values | Bounded, ordinal, censored, or item-level model |
| Several latent subpopulations | One center and scale are inadequate | Predictors, hierarchy, or mixture model |
| Repeated observations | Independence is false | Multilevel or time-dependent model |

## 2. Known variance, unknown mean: the analytic scaffold

Assume

$$
y_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2),
\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,
$$

where $\sigma^2$ is known, and place the prior

$$
\mu\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_0,\tau_0^2).
$$

The data contribute precision $n/\sigma^2$, while the prior contributes precision $1/\tau_0^2$. The posterior is

$$
\mu\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_n,\tau_n^2),
$$

with

$$
\frac{1}{\tau_n^2}
=
\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}
+
\frac{n}{\sigma^2},
$$

$$
\mu_n
=
\frac{\mu_0/\tau_0^2+(n/\sigma^2)\bar y}
{1/\tau_0^2+n/\sigma^2}.
$$

The posterior precision is the sum of prior and data precision. The posterior mean is a precision-weighted compromise, not a mechanical average of two numbers.

```{mermaid}

```

### Prior effective sample size

The posterior mean can be written as

$$
\mu_n
=
\frac{W}{W+n}\mu_0
+
\frac{n}{W+n}\bar y,
\qquad
W=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau_0^2}.
$$

$W$ is the number of observations with variance $\sigma^2$ that would contribute the same precision as this particular Normal prior. It is a model- and scale-specific interpretation, not a general property of a prior distribution.

## 3. Social-science example: combining election information

Jackman combines a model-based forecast of George W. Bush's Florida vote share in the 2000 election with a large-sample Normal approximation to a poll. The historical model supplies

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0.491,0.000484),
$$

and the poll supplies the approximate likelihood

$$
0.548\mid\theta
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\theta,0.000486).
$$

The posterior is approximately

$$
\theta\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0.519,0.000243).
$$

The result lies between the forecast and poll and has smaller variance than either source. The example is pedagogically useful because the two sources have almost equal precision. It also illustrates a limitation: vote share is bounded, so a Normal approximation is defensible only when its probability mass is concentrated far from 0 and 1. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`


## 4. Estimating a mean is not predicting a person

For known $\sigma^2$, the posterior for the population mean is

$$
\mu\mid y\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_n,\tau_n^2).
$$

A new observation contains both uncertainty about $\mu$ and new sampling variation:

$$
\widetilde y\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_n,\sigma^2+\tau_n^2).
$$

Thus an interval for the population mean is narrower than a predictive interval for a new person, school, or organization under the same model.

```{admonition} Illustration: parameter uncertainty versus outcome uncertainty
:class: tip
Plot the posterior density of $\mu$ above the posterior predictive density of $\widetilde y$ on the same horizontal scale. Use arrows to identify $\tau_n^2$ as uncertainty about the mean and $\sigma^2$ as new-observation variability.
```

## 5. Unknown mean and variance

In applied work, $\sigma^2$ is rarely known. A conjugate specification is

$$
y_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2),
$$

$$
\mu\mid\sigma^2
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}\!\left(\mu_0,\frac{\sigma^2}{\kappa_0}\right),
\qquad
\sigma^2\sim\operatorname{InverseGamma}(\alpha_0,\beta_0).
$$

Here the Inverse-Gamma density is parameterized with kernel

$$
p(\sigma^2)
\propto
(\sigma^2)^{-(\alpha_0+1)}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{\beta_0}{\sigma^2}\right).
$$

After observing $n$ values with mean $\bar y$,

$$
\kappa_n=\kappa_0+n,
\qquad
\mu_n=\frac{\kappa_0\mu_0+n\bar y}{\kappa_n},
$$

$$
\alpha_n=\alpha_0+\frac n2,
$$

$$
\beta_n
=
\beta_0
+
\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar y)^2
+
\frac{\kappa_0n}{2\kappa_n}(\bar y-\mu_0)^2.
$$

The posterior factorizes as

$$
\sigma^2\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{InverseGamma}(\alpha_n,\beta_n),
$$

$$
\mu\mid\sigma^2,y
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}\!\left(\mu_n,\frac{\sigma^2}{\kappa_n}\right).
$$

Marginalizing over uncertainty in $\sigma^2$ gives

$$
\mu\mid y
\sim
t_{2\alpha_n}\!\left(
\mu_n,
\sqrt{\frac{\beta_n}{\alpha_n\kappa_n}}
\right),
$$

where the second argument is a scale parameter. The posterior predictive distribution is wider:

$$
\widetilde y\mid y
\sim
t_{2\alpha_n}\!\left(
\mu_n,
\sqrt{\frac{\beta_n(\kappa_n+1)}{\alpha_n\kappa_n}}
\right).
$$

## 6. From conjugate baseline to modern applied specification

The closed-form model is valuable for understanding information aggregation and for testing simulation code. In modern applied modeling, it is often clearer to specify independent, interpretable priors such as

$$
\mu\sim\operatorname{Normal}(m,s_m^2),
\qquad
\sigma\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(s_\sigma),
$$

and fit the nonconjugate posterior computationally. The scale priors must be calibrated through prior predictive outcomes. Computational fitting is introduced in Session 6; the present chapter retains the conjugate result as an analytic benchmark.

## 7. Normal likelihood or robust alternative?

The most useful diagnostic question is not “Does the histogram look Normal?” but “Which observable features would differ under plausible alternatives?”

| Feature to check | Normal-model implication | Possible alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum and maximum | Extremes follow light tails | Student-$t$ or contamination model |
| Skewness | Approximately symmetric residuals | Lognormal, Gamma, or skewed model |
| Floor or ceiling counts | Few boundary accumulations | Censored, ordinal, or bounded model |
| Between-group variation | One conditional scale unless modeled | Group-specific scales or hierarchy |
| Repeated-unit dependence | None after conditioning | Random effects or time dependence |

A Student-$t$ likelihood can preserve the center of the main data cloud when a small number of valid observations are extreme. Kruschke uses this property to motivate robust estimation. The degrees-of-freedom parameter controls tail thickness; it should not be treated as a test that labels particular observations “outliers.” {cite}`kruschke2015doing`

For positive outcomes such as income or duration, compare Normal, lognormal, and Gamma models on the **original outcome scale**. A model fitted on the log scale can represent a geometric center well while giving a different arithmetic mean and upper-tail prediction.

## 8. Posterior predictive checking for the Normal model

Choose checks that target the assumptions under consideration:

1. simulate replicated datasets $y^{\mathrm{rep}}$ from the posterior predictive distribution;
2. compare observed and replicated minima, maxima, skewness, and within-group standard deviations;
3. inspect whether predicted values cross impossible boundaries;
4. compare the same statistics under a Student-$t$ or positive-outcome alternative;
5. translate any consequential difference back to the estimand.

The Newcomb speed-of-light example in *Regression and Other Stories* demonstrates that a fitted Normal model can reproduce the center while failing badly for extreme observations. The observed minimum is far outside what Normal posterior replications typically generate. This is a model check, not merely an instruction to delete the extreme value. {cite}`gelman2021regression`

## 10. Guided practice

````{dropdown} Question 1: known variance
A Normal prior has $\mu_0=50$ and $\tau_0^2=25$. Ten observations have known variance $\sigma^2=16$ and sample mean $\bar y=52$. Which source receives more posterior weight?

**Answer.** The prior effective sample size is $W=16/25=0.64$, much smaller than $n=10$. The posterior mean is approximately 51.88 and the posterior variance approximately 1.50.
````

````{dropdown} Question 2: mean or new observation?
Why is an interval for the average attitude score narrower than a prediction interval for a new respondent's score?

**Answer.** The first describes uncertainty about the population location. The second additionally includes person-level outcome variation around that location.
````

````{dropdown} Question 3: robust model
A Student-$t$ model changes the estimated mean substantially relative to a Normal model. What should be checked before choosing the Student-$t$ result?

**Answer.** Verify the extreme observations, inspect whether skewness or a mixture rather than symmetric heavy tails generated them, compare targeted posterior predictive checks, and determine whether the difference matters for the substantive estimand.
````

## 11. Connection to the Session 5 protocol

Use the Normal model as an analytic recovery benchmark:

1. generate $\mu$ and $\sigma$ on a substantively meaningful scale;
2. simulate observations at several sample sizes;
3. verify the closed-form posterior numerically;
4. estimate fixed-parameter coverage across repeated datasets;
5. stress-test the procedure by generating Student-$t$ or lognormal data while fitting a Normal model;
6. compare bias, interval width, coverage, and posterior predictive discrepancies.
