# Bayesian Analysis of Empirical Data: Course Overview

This course teaches you to build, check, and interpret Bayesian models for empirical research. You will work with examples from the cognitive, social, and behavioural sciences, then develop a research protocol for your own question or dataset.

**At a glance**

- **Format:** 14 lessons, each lasting four academic hours — 56 academic hours in total.
- **Approach:** concepts, interactive demonstrations, worked examples, and practical notebooks.
- **Main tools:** Python, PyMC, and ArviZ.
- **Final project:** a reproducible Bayesian research protocol.
- **Starting point:** introductory statistics and basic programming; no previous Bayesian modelling experience is required.

## Who is this course for?

The course is for graduate students, researchers, analysts, and practitioners who work with empirical data. It is especially relevant when observations are repeated, grouped, heterogeneous, or incomplete.

You should be comfortable with:

- introductory probability and statistics: random variables, distributions, estimation, and regression;
- basic Python or another programming language;
- elementary mathematical notation and statistical graphs.

Bring an empirical question or dataset to develop throughout the course. The book provides explanations, demonstrations, data, and laboratory notebooks to support your learning.

## What will you learn?

### Ask a clear question and build a model

- **Define the target of your analysis:** the constructs, measurements, observational units, population, and estimand — the quantity you want to learn about.
- **Represent uncertainty:** connect Bayesian probability to repeated-sampling interpretations and choose likelihoods and priors that fit the scientific question and measurement process.
- **Understand Bayesian updating:** begin with models whose posteriors can be calculated analytically, then use simulation and MCMC for more complex models.

### Fit the model and check its implications

- **Work with different data structures:** fit regression, generalized linear, robust, and hierarchical models, including interactions, repeated observations, and partial pooling.
- **Check computation:** use multiple chains, rank-normalized $\hat{R}$, bulk and tail effective sample sizes, divergences, and graphical diagnostics in PyMC and ArviZ.
- **Check the model:** examine prior and posterior predictions, investigate parameter recovery and empirical coverage, and distinguish predictive checks from model comparison using LOO or other cross-validation suited to the prediction task.

### Interpret and communicate the results

- **Explain posterior uncertainty:** report probabilities, credible intervals, contrasts, and predictions on the observable scale; distinguish credible intervals from confidence intervals.
- **State what the evidence supports:** separate description, prediction, and causal inference, and examine limits from measurement error, missingness, selection, and population coverage.
- **Make the analysis reproducible:** document data sources, priors, simulation, diagnostics, sensitivity analyses, conclusions, and ethical considerations in a research protocol.

## How the course develops

The course follows a complete modelling workflow: **ask a question → build a model → simulate and fit → check → interpret**.

You will first learn how probability distributions describe uncertainty about observations, parameters, and future outcomes. You will then use simulation to examine priors, plan studies, and understand computation. Later lessons extend this foundation to applied models, causal questions, and generalization.

### Part I — Foundations · Lessons 1–4

Start with the research question, then learn how likelihoods and priors lead to posterior uncertainty and predictions.

| Lesson | Topic and empirical example | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Research questions vs hypothesis testing** | Bayesian vs. frequentist statistics (ground truth as a point estimate vs. distribution of truth), uncertainties, random variables, distribution, Central Limit Theorem (CLT), operating with the mean, statistics of random variables | Differences and uncertainty simulator |
| **2. Bayes theorem and its components** | Conditional probability, likelihood, interactive examples (e.g., Monty Hall paradox) | Monty Hall and conditional probability explorer |
| **3. Generative models and likelihood** | Trial accuracy and simulated polling (*McElreath*, Ch. 2–3) | Generative data-process simulator |
| **4. Using the posterior** | Housing-policy support and earnings (*BDA*, Sec. 2.3; *ROS*, Ch. 15) | Credible intervals, hypothesis probabilities, transformed quantities, and predictions |

### Part II — Simulation, design, and computation · Lessons 5–6

Explore what a study can tell you before collecting data, and learn how to sample from a posterior distribution.

| Lesson | Topic and empirical example | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **5. Simulation, recovery, and design** | Educational intervention evaluation (*ROS*, Ch. 5 and 16) | Parameter recovery with simulated data |
| **6. Bayesian computation** | MCMC, HMC, and NUTS; one- and two-dimensional random walks and a PyMC baseline (*Jackman*, Ch. 3; *McElreath*) | Metropolis–Hastings visual sampler |

### Part III — Applied models · Lessons 7–10

Choose models for different outcomes and account for interactions, group differences, and repeated observations.

| Lesson | Topic and empirical example | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **7. Continuous outcomes and robust models** | Reaction times, earnings, and Newcomb's light-speed measurements (*ROS*, Ch. 11; *Maier et al.*) | Normal versus Student-$t$ outlier comparison |
| **8. Regression and interactions** | KidIQ cognitive development and earnings (*ROS*, Ch. 6–10) | Regression and interaction surfaces |
| **9. Generalized linear models** | Bangladesh well switching, cockroach counts, and ordinal attitudes (*ROS*, Ch. 13–15; *Jackman*, Ch. 8) | Inverse-link and threshold visualizer |
| **10. Hierarchical models** | Mathematics achievement across schools (*Jackman*, Ch. 7) | Partial-pooling and multilevel simulator |

### Part IV — Checking, interpretation, and the final project · Lessons 11–14

Assess the analysis, examine the assumptions behind broader claims, and bring the full workflow together.

| Lesson | Topic and empirical example | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **11. Diagnostics and model comparison** | Unemployment time series (*ROS*, Ch. 11; *Vehtari et al.*) | Diagnostic failures, posterior predictive checks, and LOO |
| **12. Causal inference and DAGs** | Electric Company and Sesame Street evaluations (*ROS*, Ch. 18–20) | Collider and confounder simulator |

| **14. Complete research protocol** | Cognitive and sociological capstone analyses | Protocol builder, checklist, and defence |

## How to work through a chapter

Applied chapters follow five steps:

1. **Read the concept.** Identify the research question, the quantity of interest, and the model's assumptions.
2. **Explore the demonstration.** Change inputs in an interactive figure to see how the model behaves before running a sampler.
3. **Follow the worked example.** Read the model and code walkthrough using PyMC, Bambi, and ArviZ.
4. **Complete the laboratory notebook.** Fit models, check computation, and generate predictions on the observable scale.
5. **Add to your research protocol.** Document one part of your own analysis, such as the priors, causal diagram, diagnostics, or sensitivity checks.

These tasks build toward the final project throughout the course.

## Software and access

**Computational tools:** Python 3.12, PyMC 5, ArviZ, Bambi, NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, and Plotly.

**Reading and exploration:** chapters are web pages with embedded Plotly demonstrations. You can explore updating, MCMC, regression, and partial pooling directly in the page.

**Practical work:** laboratory notebooks include optional Google Colab launch links. Colab provides a browser-based environment, subject to its availability and lesson resource limits.

## Optional advanced study

This independent-study block is **outside the required 56 academic hours**. It extends the course without adding a required fifteenth lesson.

Start with [](Advanced.BayesianInference) for the scope, prerequisites, and reading guide. Then choose a topic:

- **[](Advanced.VariationalInference)** — How can optimization approximate a posterior when MCMC is too costly? How should approximation error be checked? Extends Lesson 6 and the calibration work in Lesson 5.
- **[](Advanced.DecisionTheoryPrediction)** — How do posterior predictions and explicit loss functions guide an action? Extends Lesson 11 and item 15 of the research protocol.
- **[](Advanced.ProbabilisticProgrammingWorkflow)** — How does a PyMC program support an iterative, auditable analysis from measurement to decision? Brings together Lessons 1–14.
