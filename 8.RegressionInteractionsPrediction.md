> **Organizing Question:** How does an expected outcome change when predictors vary, and how do we make predictions and contrasts on the natural scale of the data?

## 1. Regression as a Line of Expected Values

In Lesson 7, we discovered that comparing two groups is secretly a linear regression:

$$
\mu_i = \alpha + \beta z_i
$$

where $z_i \in \{0, 1\}$ was a binary indicator for maternal high school completion. When $z_i = 0$, the expected score was $\alpha \approx 78.0$; when $z_i = 1$, it was $\alpha + \beta \approx 89.8$.

Now we take the natural next step: **what if our predictor $x$ is not a binary $0/1$ switch, but a continuous measurement?**

For example, how does a child's cognitive test score relate to the mother’s score on an IQ test?

In Bayesian linear regression, we do not simply search for a single "best-fitting line." We build a **generative model for conditional expectations**:

$$
y_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma), \quad \mu_i = \alpha + \beta x_i
$$

- The parameters $(\alpha, \beta)$ define the **expected mean line** $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$.
- The parameter $\sigma$ describes the **individual scatter** of real observations around that line.

```
          Test Score
             120 |                     •  / (Line: μ = α + β·x)
                 |                 •     /  •
                 |              •       / •
              80 |          •   •      /
                 |             •      /
                 +-------------------+---------> Maternal IQ (x)
                                    100
```

## 2. Meaningful Centering: Saving the Intercept $\alpha$

Consider what happens if we fit the raw formula $\mu_i = \alpha + \beta x_i$ with maternal IQ:
- The slope $\beta$ tells us how many test score points a child gains for each 1-point increase in maternal IQ.
- But what is the intercept $\alpha$? Mathematically, $\alpha$ is the predicted child score when maternal IQ is **zero** ($x = 0$).

In cognitive science, an IQ of 0 is biologically impossible and substantively absurd. Estimating $\alpha$ at $x = 0$ requires extrapolating our model hundreds of points away from any observed data. Furthermore, in MCMC sampling, an uncentered predictor creates a strong correlation between $\alpha$ and $\beta$, slowing down sampler convergence.

### The Solution: Center on a Meaningful Baseline
We subtract a reference value from our predictor (e.g., the population mean of 100):

$$
x_{c, i} = x_i - 100
$$

Our regression line becomes:

$$
\mu_i = \alpha + \beta (x_i - 100)
$$

Now:
1. When a mother has an average IQ of 100 ($x_{c} = 0$), the expected child score is simply $\alpha$.
2. In the KidIQ dataset, $\alpha \approx 86.8$ points: the expected score for the child of an average mother. The intercept is instantly interpretable!
3. The posterior correlation between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ drops to near zero, making MCMC sampling fast and robust.

```python
import pymc as pm
import numpy as np
import arviz as az

# Center maternal IQ around 100
mom_iq_c = kidiq_df["mom_iq"].values - 100.0
child_score = kidiq_df["kid_score"].values

with pm.Model() as simple_regression:
    # Priors in real score points
    alpha = pm.Normal("alpha", mu=85.0, sigma=15.0)  # Expected score at average IQ
    beta = pm.Normal("beta", mu=0.5, sigma=0.5)      # Expected points gained per IQ point
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=15.0)
    
    # Conditional mean
    mu = alpha + beta * mom_iq_c
    
    # Gaussian likelihood
    y_obs = pm.Normal("y_obs", mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=child_score)
    
    idata_simple = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.9)
```

## 3. Seeing the Line in Prediction Space: Two Kinds of Uncertainty

What does our Bayesian posterior give us? Not a single rigid line, but a **distribution of plausible lines** $(\alpha^{(s)}, \beta^{(s)})$.

Crucially, we must distinguish between two questions:
1. *Where is the true average relationship?*
2. *Where will a specific individual child's score land?*

Panel (b) shows 25 lines sampled from the posterior. ### 1. Epistemic Uncertainty (The Mean Line $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x^*]$)
This represents our uncertainty about where the true regression line sits:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mu(x^*)) \propto \frac{\sigma^2}{N}
$$

Because this uncertainty comes from having finite data, it is **reducible**. As our sample size $N$ grows larger, the band of candidate lines narrows and eventually collapses to a razor-thin curve.

### 2. Aleatory Scatter (A New Individual $y^*_{\text{new}}$)
Even if we had a billion data points and knew the mean line with absolute mathematical certainty, individual children do not sit directly on the line. Individual lives have variation:

$$
y^*_{\text{new}} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu(x^*), \sigma) \implies \operatorname{Var}(y^*_{\text{new}}) = \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\mu(x^*))}_{\text{Reducible (vanishes)}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible (scatter)}}
$$

This scatter ($\sigma \approx 18$ points) is **irreducible** by sample size. No amount of data can eliminate individual variability.

## 4. Two Predictors: The Additive Model

Now let us combine what we learned in Lessons 7 and 8. What if we include **both** maternal IQ ($x_c$) and high school completion ($z \in \{0, 1\}$)?

$$
\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 (x_i - 100) + \beta_2 z_i
$$

This model describes **two parallel lines**:
- For mothers without high school ($z = 0$): $\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 (x_i - 100)$.
- For mothers with high school ($z = 1$): $\mu_i = (\alpha + \beta_2) + \beta_1 (x_i - 100)$.

The slope with respect to maternal IQ ($\beta_1 \approx 0.56$) is assumed to be **identical** for both groups; high school education simply shifts the line upward by $\beta_2 \approx 5.9$ points.

```
        Score
         100 |                    /  HS = 1: Intercept (α + β₂)
             |                   /
          80 |                  /  HS = 0: Intercept α
             |                 /
             +----------------+--------> IQ (centered)
                              0
```

Parallel lines are a clean baseline. But what if the effect of maternal IQ is different for mothers with and without high school diplomas?

---

## 5. The Interaction Model: When Slopes Depend on Groups

To allow the slope to differ across groups, we add an **interaction term** (the product of maternal IQ and the high school indicator):

$$
\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 (x_i - 100) + \beta_2 z_i + \beta_3 \big( (x_i - 100) \times z_i \big)
$$

```python
import bambi as bmb

# Fitting using Bambi formula syntax: mom_iq_c * mom_hs includes main effects and interaction
model_kidiq = bmb.Model(
    "kid_score ~ mom_iq_c * mom_hs", 
    data=kidiq_df,
    priors={
        "Intercept": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=80, sigma=15),
        "mom_iq_c": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=0.5, sigma=0.5),
        "mom_hs": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=5, sigma=10),
        "mom_iq_c:mom_hs": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=0, sigma=0.5),
        "sigma": bmb.Prior("HalfNormal", sigma=15)
    }
)
idata_kidiq = model_kidiq.fit(draws=2000, tune=1000)
```

### Reading the Non-Parallel Lines
Let us evaluate the conditional slope for each group:

1. **For mothers without high school ($z = 0$)**:
   The interaction term disappears ($z=0$):

   $$
   \mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 (x_i - 100) \approx 77.5 + 0.97 \cdot (x_i - 100)
   $$

   The slope is steep: $\beta_1 \approx 0.97$ points gained per IQ point.

2. **For mothers with high school ($z = 1$)**:
   The interaction term activates ($z=1$):

   $$
   \mu_i = (\alpha + \beta_2) + (\beta_1 + \beta_3) \cdot (x_i - 100) \approx 89.2 + (0.97 - 0.48) \cdot (x_i - 100)
   $$

   The slope is roughly halved: $\beta_1 + \beta_3 \approx 0.49$ points per IQ point!

### Substantive Interpretation Table

| Question you might ask | Formula from the model | Value in test points |
|---|---|---|
| Expected score: mother without HS at average IQ (100) | $\alpha$ | $\approx 77.5$ |
| Expected score: mother with HS at average IQ (100) | $\alpha + \beta_2$ | $\approx 89.2$ |
| High school score advantage at IQ = 80 | $\beta_2 + \beta_3 (80 - 100)$ | $\approx 11.4 + (-0.48 \times -20) \approx +21.0$ points |
| High school score advantage at IQ = 100 | $\beta_2$ | $\approx +11.4$ points |
| High school score advantage at IQ = 120 | $\beta_2 + \beta_3 (120 - 100)$ | $\approx 11.4 + (-0.48 \times +20) \approx +1.8$ points |

```{tip}
**Notice the key insight:** There is no single "effect" of maternal high school education. For mothers with lower IQ scores, having completed high school is associated with a massive advantage ($+21$ points). At high maternal IQ scores, the lines converge ($+1.8$ points). An interaction means that one predictor's effect depends on the value of another predictor!
```

## 6. Regression Coefficients Are Not Causal Effects

```{warning}
**Correlation is Not Intervention:**
A regression coefficient $\beta = 0.97$ means: *"In our sample, children of mothers who scored 1 point higher on the IQ test scored on average 0.97 points higher, holding observed education constant."*

It does **not** prove that if we intervened to artificially raise a mother's IQ score by 1 point, her child's cognitive score would increase by 0.97. Unmeasured variables (socioeconomic resources, home environment, genetics, school quality) may influence both variables. Establishing causal claims requires formal causal diagrams (DAGs), which we cover in Lesson 12.
```

## 8. Concept Check

```{dropdown} Concept Check: The "Main Effect" Myth in Interactions
In an interaction model $\mu = \alpha + \beta_1 x_c + \beta_2 z + \beta_3 (x_c \times z)$, a student reports $\beta_1 = 0.97$ as "the overall effect of maternal IQ on child test scores." Why is this report incomplete and misleading?

**Answer:** When an interaction term $\beta_3$ is in the model, there is no single "main effect" of $x_c$. The parameter $\beta_1$ is the conditional slope *only* when $z = 0$ (mothers without high school). For mothers with high school ($z = 1$), the slope changes to $\beta_1 + \beta_3 \approx 0.49$. Slopes must always be reported conditionally on the values of the interacting variables.
```

## Key Takeaways

- Mean-centering continuous predictors gives the intercept $\alpha$ an immediate, transparent physical interpretation.
- Bayesian regression yields a distribution of plausible lines, distinguishing **epistemic uncertainty in the mean line** from **aleatory scatter of individual observations**.
- An **interaction** means that the slope of one predictor depends on the level of another.
- Always translate interaction parameters into **concrete contrasts at explicit baseline values**.
