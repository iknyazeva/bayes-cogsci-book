> **Organizing Question:** How does an expected outcome vary with predictors, and which conditional prediction or contrast answers the substantive question?

## 1. Regression as a Model for Conditional Expectations

Linear regression is not a deterministic curve-fitting exercise; in the Bayesian framework, it is a generative model for **conditional probability distributions**:

$$
y_i \mid x_i, \beta, \sigma \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma), \quad \mu_i = \mathbb{E}[y_i \mid x_i] = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ik}
$$

The parameters $(\alpha, \beta)$ define the **conditional expectation surface** $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$, while $\sigma$ quantifies the irreducible scatter (individual variability) of observations around that surface.

---

## 2. Meaningful Centering and the Interpretation of Intercepts

In raw uncentered models $y = \alpha_{\text{raw}} + \beta x$, the intercept $\alpha_{\text{raw}}$ represents the expected outcome when $x = 0$. In cognitive science and social science, $x = 0$ is often physically impossible or substantively meaningless (e.g., an IQ of 0, a body height of 0 cm, or an age of 0 years).

By **mean-centering** predictors ($x^c = x - \bar{x}$):

$$
\mu_i = \alpha + \beta (x_i - \bar{x})
$$

1. $\alpha$ acquires an immediate, transparent interpretation: the **expected outcome for the average individual** ($\bar{x}$).
2. The posterior correlation between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ drops to zero (the posterior covariance matrix becomes diagonal), accelerating MCMC sampling convergence and eliminating parameter degeneracy.

---

## 3. Sequential Learning: Parameter Space vs. Prediction Space

Following the foundational pedagogical approach of [Sergey Nikolenko](https://www.sergeynikolenko.ru/) (*04_linbayes*) and Christopher Bishop (*PRML* Ch. 3), understanding Bayesian linear regression requires tracking information simultaneously in two dual spaces:
- **Parameter Space $(\alpha, \beta)$**: where our state of knowledge is represented as a 2D probability density contour, and each incoming observation defines a **linear likelihood ridge**.
- **Data/Prediction Space $(x, y)$**: where draws from the parameter distribution manifest as **candidate regression lines** $y = \alpha^{(s)} + \beta^{(s)} x$.

### The Geometry of Sequential Updating
When a new data point $(x_t, y_t)$ is observed:
1. **The Likelihood Ridge**: In parameter space, the likelihood $\mathcal{L}(\alpha, \beta \mid x_t, y_t) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{(y_t - \alpha - \beta x_t)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ is constant along the line $\alpha + \beta x_t = y_t$. This forms an infinite diagonal ridge perpendicular to the vector $(1, x_t)$.
2. **Posterior Multiplication**: Multiplying the prior contour by this ridge carves out a contracted 2D Gaussian ellipse.
3. **Line Pivoting**: In data space, all regression lines drawn from this updated posterior are constrained to pass near the observed point $(x_t, y_t)$, pivoting around it like a hinge!
4. **Ridge Intersection**: A second data point $(x_{t+1}, y_{t+1})$ introduces a non-parallel likelihood ridge. The intersection of the two ridges constrains both the slope $\beta$ and intercept $\alpha$, locking down the regression line.

### Reducible (Epistemic) vs. Irreducible (Aleatory) Uncertainty: $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x^*]$ vs. $y^*_{\text{new}}$

*Figure 8.2: Distinguishing Mean Curve Uncertainty from Individual Predictive Scatter. Panel (a) shows the posterior contour in $(\alpha, \beta)$ space. Panel (b) shows 25 sampled posterior lines. Panel (c) compares the 95% credible interval for the expected mean $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$ against the 95% posterior predictive interval for a new observation $y_{\text{new}}$:*

<iframe src="../_static/fig8_bayesian_regression_lines.html" width="100%" height="400px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

A fundamental distinction in statistical modeling is separating **what we can learn with more data (Reducible / Epistemic)** from **what is inherently noisy (Irreducible / Aleatory)**:

- **Reducible Uncertainty (Epistemic / Mean Regression Line $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x^*]$)**:
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mu(x^*)) = \operatorname{Var}(\alpha) + (x^*)^2 \operatorname{Var}(\beta) + 2x^* \operatorname{Cov}(\alpha, \beta) \propto \frac{\sigma^2}{N}
$$
This represents our ignorance about the true regression slope and intercept. As sample size $N \to \infty$, this uncertainty **shrinks to zero**, and the credible band for the mean collapses to a single sharp line.

- **Irreducible Uncertainty (Aleatory / Observation Scatter $y^*_{\text{new}}$)**:
$$
y^*_{\text{new}} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu(x^*), \sigma) \implies \operatorname{Var}(y^*_{\text{new}}) = \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\mu(x^*))}_{\text{Reducible}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible}}
$$
Even with infinite data ($N \to \infty$), individual observations still scatter around the mean line with residual variance $\sigma^2$. No amount of additional data can eliminate this intrinsic variability.

## 4. Empirical Case Study: Maternal IQ and Child Cognitive Development (KidIQ)

From Gelman et al. (*Regression and Other Stories*, Ch. 10–11), we examine child test scores ($y$) predicted by maternal high school graduation status ($z \in \{0, 1\}$) and maternal IQ ($x$):

### The Interaction Model
Does the association between maternal IQ and child test scores differ depending on maternal education? We specify an **interaction term**:

$$
y_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma), \quad \mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 (x_i - 100) + \beta_2 z_i + \beta_3 (x_i - 100) z_i
$$

### Anchor Model Card: KidIQ Child Test Scores

| Field | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Outcome** | Child cognitive test score ($y \in \mathbb{R}$) |
| **Unit** | Child/mother pair in documented KidIQ dataset ($N = 434$) |
| **Primary Estimand** | Predicted score difference for specified maternal IQ and high-school combinations |
| **Conditional Mean** | $\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1\,\text{IQ}_{c,i} + \beta_2\,\text{HS}_i + \beta_3(\text{IQ}_{c,i} \times \text{HS}_i)$ where $\text{IQ}_{c,i} = \text{IQ}_i - 100$ |
| **Likelihood** | Gaussian baseline: $y_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma)$ |
| **Priors** | $\alpha \sim \operatorname{Normal}(80, 15)$, $\beta_{\text{iq}} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0.5, 0.5)$, $\beta_{\text{hs}} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(5, 10)$, $\beta_{\text{inter}} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, 0.5)$, $\sigma \sim \operatorname{HalfNormal}(15)$ |
| **Hierarchy** | None (single-level regression) |
| **Interpretation** | Conditional association on the outcome scale (non-causal without identification DAG) |

```python
import pymc as pm
import bambi as bmb

# Using Bambi for high-level formula specification
model_kidiq = bmb.Model(
    "kid_score ~ mom_iq_c * mom_hs", 
    data=kidiq_df, 
    priors={
        "Intercept": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=80, sigma=15),
        "mom_iq_c": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=0.5, sigma=0.5),
        "mom_hs": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=5, sigma=10),
        "mom_iq_c:mom_hs": bmb.Prior("Normal", mu=0, sigma=0.5),
        "sigma": bmb.Prior("HalfNormal", sigma=15)
    }
)
idata_kidiq = model_kidiq.fit(draws=2000, tune=1000)
```

### Interpreting Heterogeneous Slopes

The slope with respect to maternal IQ is conditional on education:
- For mothers **without** high school diplomas ($z = 0$): $\text{Slope} = \beta_1 \approx 0.97$ points of child score per IQ point.
- For mothers **with** high school diplomas ($z = 1$): $\text{Slope} = \beta_1 + \beta_3 \approx 0.97 - 0.48 = 0.49$.

This demonstrates **heterogeneous association**: maternal IQ matters more when high-school education is absent — the IQ slope is nearly halved for HS graduates, who instead start from an elevated baseline ($59.4$ vs. $48.0$ points at IQ $= 100$).

**How to read this figure (practitioner's route):**

1. **Panel (a) — two lines, not one.** An interaction means the predictor's slope depends on the group. Read each line separately first: no-HS families gain $\approx 0.97$ test points per maternal IQ point; HS families gain $\approx 0.49$.
2. **Panel (a) — where the lines cross.** The lines converge near IQ $\approx 124$: at high maternal IQ, the education advantage has nearly vanished; at low IQ it is large. If your sample has few low-IQ mothers, the interaction is estimated mostly from the thin region — check the data density.
3. **Panel (b) — contrasts at named baselines.** The education effect is not one number. At the reference IQ $= 100$ it is $11.4$ points $[95\% \approx \pm 3.9]$; at IQ $= 80$ it grows to $\approx 21$ points; at IQ $= 120$ it collapses toward $1.8$ and its interval crosses zero. Report the baseline, always.

| Question you might ask | Read it off the model as | Value |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Expected score, no-HS mother at IQ 100 | $\alpha$ | $\approx 48$ |
| Expected score, HS mother at IQ 100 | $\alpha + \beta_2$ | $\approx 59.4$ |
| HS boost at IQ 80 / 100 / 120 | $\beta_2 + \beta_3 \,(\text{IQ} - 100)$ | $\approx 21.0\ /\ 11.4\ /\ 1.8$ points |
| IQ slope, no-HS / HS mothers | $\beta_1\ /\ \beta_1 + \beta_3$ | $\approx 0.97\ /\ 0.49$ |

These are **conditional associations on the outcome scale, not causal effects** — see the warning in Section 6.

## 5. Posterior Contrasts and Average Predictive Comparisons

Rather than reporting raw coefficients on arbitrary standardized scales, Bayesian reporting emphasizes **substantive predictions on the natural outcome scale**:

```python
# Compute expected difference between mom with HS vs without HS at mom_iq = 100
posterior = idata_kidiq.posterior
diff_at_100 = posterior["mom_hs"] # Since mom_iq_c = 0
print(f"Expected Score Boost from HS at IQ=100: {diff_at_100.mean().values:.2f} [95% HDI: {pm.hdi(diff_at_100).values}]")
```

## 6. Regression Coefficients vs. Causal Effects

```{warning}
**Correlation is Not Identification:**
A regression coefficient $\beta_j$ represents the expected difference in $y$ between two groups differing by one unit of $x_j$, holding all other observed predictors constant. It does **not** estimate what would happen if we intervened to change $x_j$, unless unconfoundedness, positivity, and correct causal DAG structures are established (covered in Session 12).
```

## 7. Protocol Milestone: Regression & Conditional Predictions

Add the following section to your cumulative research protocol:

1. **Predictor Roles & Coding**: Document the substantive meaning of every continuous predictor and categorical indicator, noting centering choices (e.g. $\text{IQ}_c = \text{IQ} - 100$) and reference categories.
2. **Substantive Intercept**: Interpret the intercept $\alpha$ explicitly at the declared reference profile ($x_c = 0, z = 0$).
3. **Conditional Contrasts**: Report treatment differences and interaction slopes at explicit, meaningful values of the interacting covariates, computed from posterior draws.
4. **Epistemic vs. Aleatory Distinctions**: Explicitly report whether predictions represent the expected mean $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x^*]$ or an individual future observation $y^*_{\text{new}}$.
5. **Supported Predictor Range & Non-Causal Boundary**: State the convex hull of observed predictors to avoid extrapolation, and declare the non-causal limitation unless a formal DAG identification strategy is defended.

````{dropdown} Concept Check: The "Main Effect" Fallacy in the Presence of Interactions
In the model $\mu = \alpha + \beta_1 x_c + \beta_2 z + \beta_3 x_c z$, a researcher reports $\beta_1$ as the "overall main effect of maternal IQ on child test scores." Why is this report misleading?

**Answer.** When an interaction term $\beta_3$ is present, there is no single "main effect" of $x_c$. The parameter $\beta_1$ represents the conditional slope of $x_c$ *only* when $z = 0$ (mothers without high school degrees). When $z = 1$, the slope changes to $\beta_1 + \beta_3$. Interpreting $\beta_1$ in isolation misrepresents the relationship for half the sample. Slopes and contrasts must always be reported at explicit, meaningful values of the interacting covariate.
````

## Key Takeaways

- Mean-centering predictors makes the intercept $\alpha$ immediately interpretable as the baseline mean and orthogonalizes parameter posteriors.
- Track models in both **parameter space** $(\alpha, \beta)$ and **data prediction space** $(x, y)$.
- Always distinguish uncertainty in the **expected mean line** $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$ from uncertainty in **individual future outcomes** $y_{\text{new}}$.
- Interactions represent **heterogeneous conditional slopes** and must be interpreted through outcome-scale counterfactual plots.
