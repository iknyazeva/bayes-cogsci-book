## 1. Warm-up is not retained sampling

During **warm-up** (`tune` in PyMC), NUTS adapts its step size and mass matrix and moves away from possibly poor starting values ([Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS](5.HamiltonianMonteCarloNUTS.md)). Warm-up draws do not come from a chain with fixed transition rules and must **never** be used for inference. After adaptation ends and the settings freeze, the sampler produces the **retained draws** used for posterior summaries.

This distinction is more informative than the older idea of discarding a fixed fraction as "burn-in". A longer warm-up may improve adaptation; more retained draws reduce Monte Carlo error. These changes solve different problems.

## 2. Trace plots and rank plots

A **trace plot** shows the sampled values of a parameter over the iterations, one line per chain. Look for:

1. **Stationarity ("fuzzy caterpillar"):** each chain fluctuates around a constant level without upward or downward drift.
2. **Mixing:** chains started from different values overlap throughout the retained draws.
3. **Rank plots:** pool all draws, rank them, and draw a histogram of the ranks for each chain. If the chains explore the same distribution, every histogram is roughly uniform. A chain whose ranks pile up at one end has been exploring a different region from the others.

Rank plots are easier to read than traces when there are many draws or heavy tails.

## 3. Autocorrelation and effective sample size (ESS)

MCMC draws are dependent: $\theta^{(t+1)}$ depends on $\theta^{(t)}$. The autocorrelation at lag $k$ is

$$
\rho_k = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\theta^{(t)}, \theta^{(t+k)})}{\operatorname{Var}(\theta)} .
$$

The effective sample size is the number of independent draws that would give the same precision ([Markov chains](6.MarkovChains.md), section 4). Modern diagnostics {cite}`vehtari2021rank` report two versions:

- **bulk ESS:** precision of central summaries (mean, median);
- **tail ESS:** precision of tail quantities, such as the 2.5% and 97.5% interval endpoints or a tail probability.

> **Course threshold.** Bulk and tail ESS should both be at least **400** (100 per chain for 4 chains). This is a screening minimum, not a guarantee; for a reported quantity, check its Monte Carlo standard error directly.

**Thinning is usually unnecessary.** Keeping only every $k$-th draw reduces autocorrelation but throws information away; it never improves the precision of posterior summaries. Thin only when storage or memory is the binding constraint.

## 4. Rank-normalised split $\widehat R$

$\widehat R$ (the Gelman–Rubin statistic {cite}`gelman1992inference`) compares the variance **between** chains with the variance **within** chains. The modern version {cite}`vehtari2021rank` splits each chain in half (to detect drift within a chain) and works with rank-normalised draws (to handle heavy tails and boundaries):

$$
\widehat R = \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}^+(\theta)}{W}},
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}^+(\theta) = \frac{N-1}{N}\,W + \frac{1}{N}\,B,
$$

where $W$ is the average within-chain variance, $B$ is the between-chain variance and $N$ is the number of draws per (split) chain. If all chains explore the same distribution, $\widehat R\approx1$.

> **Course threshold.** $\widehat R \le 1.01$. Larger values mean that the chains have not converged to a common distribution, and the model cannot be interpreted yet.

## 5. Diagnostics specific to HMC and NUTS

1. **Divergences.** The leapfrog integrator met a region of high curvature (for example, the funnel of a hierarchical model) and could not follow the trajectory. Even one divergence signals that part of the posterior may be unexplored. Remedies: reparameterise (for example, a non-centred hierarchical model) or increase `target_accept` (for example, from 0.8 to 0.95).
2. **Energy (E-BFMI).** Measures how well momentum refreshes move the sampler between energy levels. Values below about 0.3 suggest sluggish exploration, often with heavy tails or funnels.
3. **Maximum tree depth.** Trajectories hitting the maximum depth indicate inefficient sampling, often caused by poor scaling or strong correlations.

## 6. The complete gate

No single number proves convergence. Inspect several complementary signals for every primary estimand and important transformed quantity.

| Question | Evidence | Course rule | What failure may indicate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do the chains explore the same distribution? | rank plots, rank-normalised split $\widehat R$ | $\widehat R\le1.01$ | non-stationarity, multiple modes, poor mixing |
| Are central summaries precise enough? | bulk ESS, MCSE | bulk ESS $\ge 400$; justify precision for the estimand | autocorrelation, too few retained draws |
| Are intervals and tail probabilities precise enough? | tail ESS, MCSE of quantiles/probabilities | tail ESS $\ge 400$; check the reported tail quantity | poorly explored tails |
| Did HMC meet problematic geometry? | divergences, E-BFMI, maximum tree depth | zero unresolved divergences | curvature, boundaries, weak identification, poor scaling |
| Do traces and ranks tell a coherent story? | trace and rank plots across chains | no drift, separation or sticking | chains do not represent the same target |

The course uses four chains as a transparent default, $\widehat R\le1.01$ as a screening threshold, and zero unresolved divergences as a reporting requirement. These rules are safeguards. They do not show that the likelihood, priors, measurement model or causal interpretation are adequate.

## 7. Validate the sampler on a known answer

Before trusting a sampler on a new kind of model, run it where the exact answer is known. The conjugate Beta–Binomial benchmark is

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(2,2),
\qquad
y\mid\theta\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(80,\theta),
\qquad
y=48,
$$

with exact posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(50,34)$: mean $50/84\approx0.595$ and 95% equal-tailed interval $[0.489,\,0.697]$.

```python
import arviz as az
import numpy as np
import pymc as pm
import scipy.stats as stats

exact_mean = 50 / 84                                    # 0.5952
exact_eti = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], 50, 34)      # [0.489, 0.697]

with pm.Model() as beta_binom_model:
    theta = pm.Beta("theta", alpha=2, beta=2)           # prior
    pm.Binomial("y", n=80, p=theta, observed=48)        # likelihood: 48 of 80
    idata = pm.sample(draws=1000, tune=1000, chains=4, random_seed=2026)

print(az.summary(idata, var_names=["theta"]))           # mean, sd, interval, ESS, R-hat, MCSE
draws = idata.posterior["theta"].values.ravel()
print("sampled 95% interval:", np.percentile(draws, [2.5, 97.5]))
az.plot_rank(idata, var_names=["theta"])
```

The sampled mean ($\approx0.595$) and interval should agree with the exact values within Monte Carlo error. Compare **like with like**: equal-tailed intervals with equal-tailed intervals at the same level (the default interval in `az.summary` depends on the ArviZ version), and judge interval endpoints by their own MCSE, not by the MCSE of the mean. Passing this benchmark shows that the tool works; it does not show that the sampler will handle a hierarchical funnel or a ridge without divergences.

## 8. A remedy should follow the diagnosis

When a check fails, work through the likely causes in order:

1. verify data coding, parameter support, likelihood and deterministic transformations;
2. inspect prior predictive implications and parameter scaling;
3. look for weakly identified parameters and strong posterior correlations;
4. rescale predictors or reparameterise the model, comparing centred and non-centred forms where appropriate;
5. adjust sampler settings such as `target_accept` when the geometry is otherwise defensible;
6. add warm-up or retained draws only when adaptation or Monte Carlo precision is the actual limitation.

Running longer cannot fix structural non-identification or persistent divergent geometry.

## 10. Practice

````{dropdown} Question 1. Matching means, different ranks
Two chains have nearly identical posterior means, but their rank histograms occupy different parts of the rank range. What claim is not yet justified?

**Answer.** Equal means do not show that the chains explored the same distribution. They may differ in spread, skewness, tails or modes. Do not interpret the posterior until the discrepancy is explained.
````

````{dropdown} Question 2. Divergences alongside a good R-hat
A model has $\widehat R=1.00$ and large ESS for every displayed coefficient, but reports 17 divergences. What should happen next?

**Answer.** Do not interpret the model yet. Locate the divergent draws; examine scaling, constraints, identification and hierarchical parameterisation; refit. A higher `target_accept` may reduce discretisation error, but it does not replace investigating the geometry.
````

````{dropdown} Question 3. Acceptance rate and efficiency
Why can a chain with a 95% acceptance rate estimate a posterior mean less efficiently than a chain with a lower acceptance rate?

**Answer.** Very small proposals are accepted almost every time but produce highly autocorrelated draws. Judge acceptance together with movement, ESS and MCSE.
````
