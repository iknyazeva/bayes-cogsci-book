# Lesson 4: What Can We Learn from a Posterior?

In [Lesson 2](2.ProbabilityBayesianUpdating.md), we updated probabilities. In [Lesson 3](3.GenerativeModelsLikelihoodPrediction.md), we simulated observations. Here we start with a posterior distribution already available and use it to answer research questions.

A posterior mean is one summary. We can also describe uncertainty, calculate the probability of a substantively important difference, compare groups, transform quantities, and predict new observations. The main operation is simple: **calculate the quantity of interest for each posterior draw, then summarize those calculated values**. This use of simulation is developed in *Bayesian Data Analysis*, Sections 1.9 and 2.3. {cite}`gelman2013bda`

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Interpret a credible interval and distinguish it from a confidence interval.
2. Calculate posterior probabilities for directional, interval, and joint hypotheses.
3. Obtain a posterior distribution for a difference, ratio, or other function of parameters.
4. Distinguish uncertainty about a parameter or expected outcome from variation in future observations.
5. Report a substantive conclusion together with its uncertainty and assumptions.

:::{tip} Before we begin
We assume that the posterior is proper and available either as a known distribution or as reliable draws from a fitted model. Here we can sample directly from Beta distributions. Checking numerical sampling methods comes in Lesson 6.

Prior elicitation, conjugate updating, and prior sensitivity examples are detailed in the [prior preparation appendix](4.PriorPreparation.md). ## 1. Start with the research question and the posterior

**Teaching example: support for a housing subsidy.** Suppose a survey samples 100 lower-income and 100 higher-income households. There are 58 supporters in the first group and 40 in the second. These are invented counts for this lesson, not findings from a published survey.

Let $\theta_L$ and $\theta_H$ denote the two support probabilities. Assume independent random samples within groups, conditionally independent binary responses, a common probability within each group, and independent $\operatorname{Beta}(2,2)$ priors. The posteriors are

$$
\theta_L\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(60,44),
\qquad
\theta_H\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(42,62).
$$

Under this model they are posterior-independent. We have finished the updating calculation; the next question is what to do with the result.

```{mermaid}
flowchart TD
    P["Available joint posterior"] --> Q["Specify a quantity or hypothesis"]
    Q --> F["Calculate it for each posterior draw"]
    F --> S["Summarize its distribution or probability"]
    S --> R["Report in substantive units"]
    P --> Y["Simulate future or replicated observations"]
    Y --> C["Predict outcomes or check the model"]
    R --> A["Check sensitivity to assumptions"]
    C --> A
```

Generate draws once and retain them for the calculations that follow:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta, betabinom

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
S = 1_000_000
p_low = rng.beta(60, 44, size=S)
p_high = rng.beta(42, 62, size=S)

# Each row is one draw of the parameter pair.
posterior = np.column_stack([p_low, p_high])
```

These 1,000,000 draws describe uncertainty conditional on the 200 observed households. They are not additional households or new evidence.

## 2. Describe uncertainty with a credible interval

The posterior mean of $\theta_L$ is $60/104\approx0.577$. A **95% credible interval** contains 95% of its posterior probability, conditional on the data, prior, and model.

For a 95% **equal-tailed interval (ETI)**, use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles:

```python
print("Posterior mean:", p_low.mean())
print("95% ETI from draws:", np.quantile(p_low, [0.025, 0.975]))
print("95% ETI from the Beta distribution:", beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], 60, 44))
```

The interval is approximately **[0.481, 0.670]**. A suitable report is:

> Under the survey model and specified prior, the support probability among lower-income households has a 95% credible interval from 48.1% to 67.0%.

This is uncertainty about a population probability. It does not say that 95% of households or 95% of future survey results lie in this interval.

### Equal-tailed and highest-density intervals

| Summary | Construction | What to keep in mind |
|---|---|---|
| Equal-tailed interval | Excludes 2.5% probability in each tail for a 95% interval | Easy to calculate; quantiles transform consistently under monotonic transformations |
| Highest-density interval or set (HDI/HDS) | Collects the highest-density values totaling 95% probability | May be asymmetric or disconnected; changes under nonlinear reparameterization |

Both summarize posterior uncertainty. Neither is universally preferable. Report the interval type and inspect the distribution, especially when it is skewed or multimodal. A posterior mean can fall in a low-density region between modes.

<iframe src="../_static/lesson4_eti_hdi.html" width="100%" height="420px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

:::{dropdown} Example: intervals for a skewed posterior
Suppose a rare response occurs once in 100 observations. With a uniform prior, its probability has posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(2,100)$.

- The 95% ETI is approximately $[0.0024,0.0539]$.
- The 95% HDI is approximately $[0.0004,0.0463]$.

Both contain 95% posterior probability before rounding. The HDI is shorter here because the density is strongly right-skewed. Transforming the endpoints of an ETI gives the corresponding interval for a monotonic transformation, with endpoint order reversed if necessary. Transforming HDI endpoints generally does not give the HDI on the new scale.
:::

### Credible interval versus confidence interval

| | 95% credible interval | 95% confidence interval |
|---|---|---|
| Probability statement | Given the observed data and model, 95% posterior probability lies within the interval | Under the sampling assumptions, the procedure covers the fixed parameter in 95% of repeated samples, exactly or approximately depending on the method |
| What varies in that statement? | The unknown parameter is described by its posterior; the observed data are conditioned on | The data and resulting interval vary across hypothetical repetitions |
| Role of a prior | Part of the posterior calculation | No prior is required for the usual construction |
| Reading a realized interval | A posterior probability statement about the parameter is available | The coverage guarantee alone does not assign 95% probability to the fixed parameter lying inside this particular interval |

<iframe src="../_static/lesson4_cred_vs_conf.html" width="100%" height="470px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

For example, a Normal posterior for an average social-attitude score might give **[50.04, 53.96]**. Under the matching Normal sampling model and a flat prior, a classical confidence interval can have exactly the same endpoints. The interpretations above still differ. The derivation is in the [Bayesian–frequentist comparison appendix](4.BayesianFrequentistConnections.md).

**Check your interpretation:** “The interval contains 95% of individual attitude scores.” Is that justified? No: an interval for a population mean concerns the mean, not the spread of individual scores.

## 3. Evaluate hypotheses as posterior probabilities

Define the support gap

$$
\Delta=\theta_L-\theta_H.
$$

For every posterior row, calculate the difference. To evaluate an event, record whether each draw satisfies it and average the resulting zeros and ones:

$$
P(H\mid D)\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}I\{H(\theta^{(s)})\}.
$$

```python
gap = p_low - p_high

print("Gap: mean and 95% ETI", gap.mean(), np.quantile(gap, [0.025, 0.975]))
print("P(gap > 0):", np.mean(gap > 0))
print("P(gap > 5 percentage points):", np.mean(gap > 0.05))
print("P(gap within +/-5 percentage points):", np.mean(np.abs(gap) < 0.05))
```

Approximate results from the posterior draws are shown below. Simulation introduces small numerical error; extra draws improve numerical precision without adding information about the population.

| Research question | Posterior result |
|---|---:|
| How large is the gap? | Mean 17.3 percentage points; 95% ETI about [3.9, 30.5] points |
| Is support higher in the lower-income group? $P(\Delta>0\mid D)$ | 0.994 |
| Does the gap exceed five percentage points? $P(\Delta>0.05\mid D)$ | 0.964 |
| Are the probabilities within five percentage points? $P(|\Delta|<0.05\mid D)$ | 0.036 |

<iframe src="../_static/lesson4_rope.html" width="100%" height="420px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

The five-point threshold is a stipulated teaching choice. In research, justify a practically relevant threshold before inspecting the result where possible. These are probabilities of specified claims under the model, not p-values.

The credible intervals for the two group probabilities overlap slightly, yet the credible interval for their difference excludes zero. **Calculate the difference directly**; overlap between marginal intervals is not a test of a contrast.

:::{dropdown} What about an exact null hypothesis?
With a continuous posterior for $\Delta$, $P(\Delta=0\mid D)=0$, just as the probability of any single exact value is zero. This does not prove a scientifically meaningful difference. Use a justified interval of practically negligible differences, or specify a separate model with positive prior probability on an exact null if that is the question. Posterior event probabilities are not automatically Bayes factors.
:::

## 4. Ask joint questions and apply functions

Suppose external population information says that 60% of households belong to the lower-income group and 40% to the higher-income group. Treat these weights as known for this example. Overall support is

$$
q=0.60\theta_L+0.40\theta_H.
$$

The survey sampled equal numbers from the two groups, so its unweighted average need not estimate this population quantity appropriately.

Consider a joint hypothesis: **the group gap exceeds ten percentage points and overall support exceeds 50%**.

```python
overall = 0.60 * p_low + 0.40 * p_high
joint = (gap > 0.10) & (overall > 0.50)
print("Overall support: mean and 95% ETI", overall.mean(),
      np.quantile(overall, [0.025, 0.975]))
print("P(gap > 10 points AND overall support > 50%):", joint.mean())
```

Overall support has posterior mean about **50.8%**, with a 95% ETI about **[43.9%, 57.5%]**. The joint probability is about **0.524**. Strong evidence for a group gap does not establish that overall support exceeds 50%.

### Other functions of the same posterior

| Quantity or hypothesis | Calculate within each draw |
|---|---|
| Relative support probability | $R^{(s)}=\theta_L^{(s)}/\theta_H^{(s)}$ |
| Largest group probability, with several groups | $M^{(s)}=\max_j\theta_j^{(s)}$ |
| Whether group A ranks first | $I\{\theta_A^{(s)}>\max_{j\ne A}\theta_j^{(s)}\}$ |
| A benefit condition or a harm condition | Evaluate the union of the two events within the draw |
| A function $g$ specified by the research question | $z^{(s)}=g(\theta^{(s)})$, then summarize the resulting distribution |

For example:

```python
relative_support = p_low / p_high
print("Mean ratio:", relative_support.mean())
print("Ratio of means:", p_low.mean() / p_high.mean())
print("95% ETI for the ratio:", np.quantile(relative_support, [0.025, 0.975]))
```

The posterior mean ratio is about **1.449**, whereas the ratio of posterior means is about **1.429**. The ratio's 95% ETI is approximately **[1.08, 1.93]**. In general,

$$
\mathbb E[g(\theta)\mid D]\ne g(\mathbb E[\theta\mid D]).
$$

For nonlinear functions, transform the draws first. For sums and fixed weighted averages, expectations do commute with the function, but draws are still useful for intervals and event probabilities.

:::{important} Keep the joint posterior intact
In a fitted model, keep parameters from the same posterior draw together. Independently shuffling columns, subtracting interval endpoints, or combining separate marginal means loses dependence needed for differences, ratios, rankings, and joint events. Sampling the two group probabilities independently is valid here because our priors and group likelihoods factorize.

The function must be defined on the relevant posterior values. Ratios can be unstable when denominators approach zero, and some transformed distributions have no finite mean. Use plots, quantiles, and event probabilities as appropriate. Finite posterior draws also introduce numerical error; correlated sampler output requires effective sample size and Monte Carlo error checks.
:::

## 5. A book example from sociology: earnings

*Regression and Other Stories*, Chapter 15 (pp. 283–284), models annual earnings using a 1990 survey. Some respondents have zero earnings, so the authors model whether earnings are positive and then model log earnings among positive earners. Their [published code](https://avehtari.github.io/ROS-Examples/Earnings/earnings_compound.html) generates predictions in those two stages. This illustrates why a single mean or coefficient does not describe the full outcome. {cite}`gelman2021regression`

The following questions extend that example for teaching. They are not additional empirical findings from the book.

For a specified respondent profile, suppose a posterior draw gives:

- $\pi$: probability of positive earnings;
- $\mu$: mean log earnings among positive earners;
- $\sigma$: standard deviation of those log earnings.

Assume positive earnings are lognormal. Different functions answer different questions:

| Question | Quantity calculated at each posterior draw |
|---|---|
| What is the median among positive earners? | $\exp(\mu)$ |
| What is the mean among positive earners? | $\exp(\mu+\sigma^2/2)$ |
| What are expected earnings including zeros? | $\pi\exp(\mu+\sigma^2/2)$ |
| What share has earnings below a positive threshold $T$? | $1-\pi+\pi\Phi((\log T-\mu)/\sigma)$ |

Here $\Phi$ is the standard Normal cumulative distribution function. The threshold must use the same currency, year, and earnings definition as the data. Low personal earnings are not automatically household poverty: household resources and needs require their own measurements.

For an **illustrative single draw** with $\pi=0.8$, $\mu=\log(30{,}000)$, and $\sigma=0.6$, the conditional median is 30,000, the conditional mean is about 35,917, and expected earnings including zeros are about 28,733. These are different quantities. This calculation is not a posterior summary until repeated over the posterior draws.

We could then ask whether the low-earnings share differs by more than five points between two profiles, or calculate a credible interval for their expected-earnings ratio. Use paired draws for both profiles. Such contrasts describe associations under the fitted model; interpreting them as effects of education, gender, or another exposure requires additional causal assumptions.

## 6. Predict a new sample

A posterior for $\theta_L$ describes uncertainty about a probability. A new sample of 100 lower-income households will also have sampling variation:

```python
future_supporters = rng.binomial(n=100, p=p_low)
print("95% predictive interval:", np.quantile(future_supporters, [0.025, 0.975]))
print("P(at least 60 future supporters):", np.mean(future_supporters >= 60))

# Exact Beta-Binomial results for comparison:
print(betabinom.ppf([0.025, 0.975], 100, 60, 44))
print(betabinom.sf(59, 100, 60, 44))
```

Compare two intervals on the **count scale**:

| Quantity | Approximate 95% interval |
|---|---|
| Expected count $100\theta_L$ | Credible interval [48.1, 67.0] |
| Realized count in the next sample | Predictive interval [44, 71] |

<iframe src="../_static/lesson4_prediction.html" width="100%" height="470px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

The probability of at least 60 supporters in the next sample is about **0.401**. The predictive interval includes variation in new responses as well as parameter uncertainty. Because counts are discrete, its probability content need not be exactly 95%.

Prediction assumes the new sample follows the specified population and observation model. Generating replicated data with the original design and comparing them with observed features is instead a **posterior predictive check**. For this survey, checks of household clustering or differences across neighborhoods would require those identifiers and a model that represents them; two aggregate counts cannot assess every aspect of the design.

## 7. Report the result and its limits

For the teaching survey, one report could read:

> Under independent Binomial sampling and independent Beta(2,2) priors, estimated support is 17.3 percentage points higher among lower-income households, with a 95% equal-tailed credible interval of about 3.9–30.5 points. The posterior probability that the gap exceeds five points is about 0.964. Using the assumed 60/40 population composition, overall support is estimated at 50.8%, with a 95% credible interval of 43.9–57.5%. The contrast describes an association between income group and support.

Check whether the relevant conclusions change under defensible alternative priors, population weights, or observation models. Reusing one posterior is sufficient for new functions of its parameters. Changing the prior, likelihood, or the modeled population generally requires a new posterior calculation or a justified reweighting method.

If a conclusion will guide an action, specify the consequences of the available actions. A posterior probability alone does not determine an acceptable decision threshold.

## 8. Practice and research protocol milestone 4

1. Choose a substantive contrast or function of your project's parameters. Give its units.
2. State an interval or joint hypothesis with a justified threshold.
3. Calculate or describe how to obtain its posterior probability and a credible interval for the relevant quantity.
4. State whether your target is a parameter, an expected outcome, or a future observation.
5. Write a short interpretation and identify one assumption to examine through sensitivity analysis or predictive checking.

**Discussion:** Why would it be incorrect to claim that the 95% interval for the support gap contains 95% of differences between individual households? What changes if population weights are uncertain rather than known?

:::{dropdown} Answers
The gap concerns two population probabilities, not individual binary responses. With uncertain population weights, model that uncertainty and calculate the weighted quantity from appropriate joint draws of the weights and group probabilities. Treating estimated weights as exact can understate uncertainty.
:::
