# Session 4: Priors, Posterior Uncertainty and Predictive Checking

A prior distribution describes which parameter values are plausible before the current data are used. It is part of the statistical model: together with the likelihood, it determines the posterior and the predictions implied by the analysis. The practical question is therefore not whether an analysis has assumptions, but whether its assumptions are visible, substantively defensible and compatible with observable data.

This session distinguishes four ideas that are often incorrectly treated as synonyms:

- an **informative prior** represents substantial domain knowledge;
- a **weakly informative prior** supplies modest, deliberate regularization;
- a **flat, diffuse or reference prior** is constructed to have limited influence under specified conditions;
- an **improper prior** is a non-normalizable mathematical device whose posterior must be proved proper.

The last two categories are sometimes called “uninformative.” This course retains that term when discussing the literature, but not as a literal claim that a prior contains no assumptions. A distribution that is flat on one scale is generally not flat after reparameterization. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian` {cite}`gelman2021regression`

## Learning objectives

After completing this session and its subchapters, you should be able to:

- distinguish informative, weakly informative, flat/reference and improper priors;
- translate substantive expectations into prior distributions on an interpretable scale;
- use prior predictive simulation to identify implausible assumptions before fitting a model;
- derive and interpret the Beta-Binomial posterior;
- explain how prior strength and sample size jointly determine posterior updating;
- distinguish posterior prediction from posterior predictive checking;
- explain why Jeffreys prior is invariant under one-to-one reparameterization in regular one-parameter models;
- identify exact and asymptotic cases in which Bayesian summaries resemble frequentist summaries without confusing their interpretations.

## 1. What a prior does

The prior has three related roles:

1. **Representation:** it records plausible values and constraints before the current observations are used.
2. **Regularization:** it reduces unstable estimates in directions where the likelihood is weak.
3. **Generation:** combined with the likelihood, it implies a distribution of possible datasets before data collection.

```{mermaid}
flowchart LR
    P["Prior over parameters<br/>p(theta)"] --> G["Generative model<br/>p(y | theta)"]
    G --> PP["Prior predictive outcomes<br/>p(y_sim)"]
    D["Observed data<br/>y"] --> L["Likelihood<br/>p(y | theta)"]
    P --> B["Bayesian update"]
    L --> B
    B --> POST["Posterior<br/>p(theta | y)"]
    POST --> REP["Posterior predictive replications<br/>p(y_rep | y)"]
```

:::{important}
The most useful first question about a prior is often not “Does this density look broad?” but “What observable datasets does this prior expect?” A prior can look harmless in parameter space and still imply absurd outcomes.
:::

### A practical vocabulary

| Prior type | Intended role | Example | Principal risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Informative | Represent substantial external knowledge | A prior for a small sex-ratio difference based on demographic evidence | Historical information may not transport to the new setting |
| Weakly informative | Exclude implausible magnitudes while allowing the data to move the posterior | A regularizing prior for a standardized regression coefficient | “Weak” has no meaning without the parameter scale |
| Flat or diffuse | Reduce local prior influence on a chosen scale | Constant density for a Normal location parameter | Flatness changes under transformation and can create improper posteriors |
| Reference or Jeffreys | Follow a formal information or invariance criterion | Beta(1/2, 1/2) for a Binomial probability | The rule can be difficult or controversial in multiparameter models |
| Improper | Supply a limiting reference calculation | p(mu) proportional to 1 on the real line | It is not a probability distribution and cannot be used for prior prediction |

The extended discussion is in {doc}`4.NonInformativePriors` and {doc}`4.JeffreysPrior`.

## 2. Eliciting a Beta prior on an interpretable scale

Suppose theta is a population proportion, such as support for a policy. For a Beta(alpha, beta) prior,

$$
\mathbb{E}(\theta)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}.
$$

In the conjugate Beta-Binomial model it is convenient to write

$$
\mu_0=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},
\qquad
n_0=\alpha+\beta,
$$

so that

$$
\alpha=n_0\mu_0,
\qquad
\beta=n_0(1-\mu_0).
$$

Here, n0 is an effective prior sample-size convention for this model: it controls concentration and makes the posterior mean a transparent weighted average. It should not be interpreted as literal previous observations unless the prior was actually constructed from comparable historical data.

:::{admonition} Demonstration: same center, different commitments
:class: tip
Beta(2, 2) and Beta(20, 20) are both centered at 0.5, but they do not express the same belief. For a future survey of 20 respondents, Beta(2, 2) allows a wide range of support counts, whereas Beta(20, 20) expects results much closer to 10 out of 20.

Before looking at either density, ask which future counts each prior should make plausible. Then inspect the prior predictive distribution, not only the prior density.
:::

For $y_{\mathrm{sim}}$ successes among $N$ future observations:

$$
p(y_{\mathrm{sim}})
=
\int_0^1
p(y_{\mathrm{sim}}\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$

Under a Beta prior and Binomial likelihood this is a Beta-Binomial distribution.

## 3. Exact Beta-Binomial updating

Let

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),
\qquad
k\mid\theta,N\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(N,\theta).
$$

Multiplication of the prior and likelihood kernels gives

$$
p(\theta\mid k,N)
\propto
\theta^{\alpha+k-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+N-k-1},
$$

and therefore

$$
\theta\mid k,N
\sim
\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+k,\beta+N-k).
$$

### The Posterior Mean as an Information Balance (The Seesaw Principle)

The posterior mean can be written exactly as a weighted compromise:

$$
\mathbb{E}(\theta\mid k,N)
=
\underbrace{\left(\frac{n_0}{n_0+N}\right)}_{\text{Prior Weight } w_{\text{prior}}} \mu_0
+
\underbrace{\left(\frac{N}{n_0+N}\right)}_{\text{Data Weight } w_{\text{data}}} \left(\frac{k}{N}\right).
$$

:::{admonition} Concrete Numerical Walkthrough: The Bayesian Seesaw
:class: tip
Suppose an organizational researcher studies employee training completion. The prior represents moderate baseline experience ($\mu_0 = 0.50$) with prior strength $n_0 = 20$ ($\text{Beta}(10, 10)$).

1. **Small Pilot Study ($N = 10, k = 7$, sample proportion $= 70\%$)**:
   $$w_{\text{prior}} = \frac{20}{20 + 10} = 66.7\%, \quad w_{\text{data}} = \frac{10}{20 + 10} = 33.3\%$$
   $$\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = (0.667 \times 0.500) + (0.333 \times 0.700) = 0.333 + 0.233 = \mathbf{0.567} \quad (\text{Posterior: } \text{Beta}(17, 13))$$
   *Takeaway*: Because $n_0 > N$, the prior acts as a regularizing anchor, keeping the posterior closer to $0.50$ than to the noisy sample proportion ($0.70$).

2. **Large Replication ($N = 200, k = 140$, sample proportion $= 70\%$)**:
   $$w_{\text{prior}} = \frac{20}{20 + 200} = 9.1\%, \quad w_{\text{data}} = \frac{200}{20 + 200} = 90.9\%$$
   $$\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = (0.091 \times 0.500) + (0.909 \times 0.700) = 0.045 + 0.636 = \mathbf{0.682} \quad (\text{Posterior: } \text{Beta}(150, 70))$$
   *Takeaway*: As sample evidence accumulates, the data weight overwhelms the fixed prior, pulling the posterior tightly toward $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = 0.70$.
:::

````{dropdown} Check the limiting cases
- If N is small relative to n0, the posterior mean remains close to the prior mean.
- If N grows while alpha and beta remain fixed, the sample proportion receives increasing weight.
- If the prior assigns zero density to a neighborhood of the data-supported value, ordinary “the data will overwhelm the prior” intuition can fail.
````

## 4. Prior influence decreases with sample size

Under regularity conditions, the data dominate fixed priors as the sample size grows.

In the Beta-Bernoulli model, the posterior mean is an explicit weighted average of the prior mean and the sample proportion:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid y]
=
\frac{\alpha+k}{\alpha+\beta+N}
=
\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+N}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\right)
+
\frac{N}{\alpha+\beta+N}\left(\frac{k}{N}\right).
$$

As $N\to\infty$, the weight on the prior mean, $(\alpha+\beta)/(\alpha+\beta+N)$, goes to zero. The data mean, $k/N$, receives weight approaching one.

The speed of this shift depends on the effective sample size of the prior, $\alpha+\beta$, relative to $N$.

### Worked example: weighted average arithmetic

Let the prior be $\text{Beta}(10, 10)$ ($\mu_0 = 0.50$, effective sample size $n_0 = \alpha + \beta = 20$). Suppose we observe $70\%$ support in our survey sample ($k/N = 0.70$):

1. **Small Sample ($N = 10$, $k = 7$)**:
   The prior carries $\frac{20}{20+10} = 66.7\%$ of the weight, and the data carries only $33.3\%$:
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = (0.667 \times 0.500) + (0.333 \times 0.700) = 0.333 + 0.233 = \mathbf{0.567} \quad (\text{Posterior: } \text{Beta}(17, 13))
   $$
2. **Large Sample ($N = 200$, $k = 140$)**:
   The prior weight collapses to $\frac{20}{20+200} = 9.1\%$, and the sample data dominates with $90.9\%$:
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = (0.091 \times 0.500) + (0.909 \times 0.700) = 0.045 + 0.636 = \mathbf{0.682} \quad (\text{Posterior: } \text{Beta}(150, 70))
   $$

## 5. Posterior uncertainty: ETI and HDI

A 95% credible interval contains 95% of the posterior probability under the specified model and observed data. Two common summaries are:

| Interval | Construction | Useful property | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equal-tailed interval (ETI) | Leaves 2.5% in each tail | Quantiles transform consistently under monotonic transformations | Can include relatively low-density tail values |
| Highest-density interval or set (HDI/HDS) | Contains the highest-density region totaling 95% probability | Concentrates on the most locally probable region | Not invariant to nonlinear reparameterization; multimodal regions can be disconnected |

:::{admonition} Illustrative Case Study: Election Night Rare Event & Interval Divergence
:class: tip
Consider an early vote sample for a minor candidate: $N = 100$ ballots sampled with $k = 1$ vote for the candidate ($\hat{\theta} = 0.010$ or 1.0%). Under a uniform prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$, the posterior is heavily right-skewed:

$$
\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(1 + 1, 1 + 99) = \text{Beta}(2, 100)
$$

The posterior mode is at $\frac{2-1}{2+100-2} = 0.010$ (1.0%), with severe positive skew:

* **95% Equal-Tailed Interval (ETI)**: $[0.0024, 0.0539]$ (0.24% to 5.39%, cutting off 2.5% probability in each tail; width 5.15%).
* **95% Highest Density Interval (HDI)**: $[0.0004, 0.0463]$ (0.04% to 4.63%, starting near zero at the peak; spans only 4.59% width vs. 5.15% for ETI).

*Guidance*: For symmetric, bell-shaped posteriors, ETI and HDI are virtually identical. For skewed, bounded, or sparse-count posteriors, always inspect whether the HDI better captures the parameter values with greatest probability density.
:::

For a symmetric unimodal posterior, ETI and HDI are often nearly identical. For skewed or multimodal posteriors, the difference can be consequential. The interval type must be named rather than reported generically as “the Bayesian interval.”

## 6. Prediction is not predictive checking

The posterior predictive distribution forecasts or describes new observations:

$$
p(\widetilde y\mid y)
=
\int p(\widetilde y\mid\theta)p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta.
$$

A **posterior predictive check** uses replicated data to examine whether the fitted model can reproduce features of the observed data:

1. draw theta^(s) from the posterior;
2. generate y_rep^(s) from the likelihood using theta^(s);
3. compare a substantively chosen discrepancy T(y_rep^(s)) with T(y).

For a survey model, useful checks might include the total number of supporters, variation across organizations, the number of unanimous groups or the distribution of item nonresponse. A model can predict the overall proportion adequately while failing to reproduce between-organization heterogeneity.

:::{warning}
A posterior predictive check does not test whether the model is “true.” It identifies ways in which simulated data from the fitted model differ from observations that matter for the intended use.
:::

## 7. Case study: beauty and variation in the sex ratio

Gelman, Hill and Vehtari discuss a published claim that parents rated as highly attractive had more daughters. In the survey, the estimated difference in the proportion of girl births was 8 percentage points with a standard error of about 3 percentage points. The comparison passed a conventional statistical-significance threshold.

The relevant prior information concerned not the overall proportion of girl births, but the **difference between two groups**. Known variation in human sex ratios suggested that a group difference larger than about half a percentage point was implausible. The authors represented this knowledge with an approximately Normal prior centered at 0 percentage points with standard deviation 0.25 percentage points.

Combining the noisy survey estimate with this prior yields an estimate of roughly 0.06 percentage points with posterior standard deviation about 0.25 percentage points. The substantive lesson is not that an informative prior automatically “prevents false positives.” It is that the parameter, scale and external information must correspond: prior knowledge about small between-group differences can be far more informative than a sample that estimates that difference imprecisely. {cite}`gelman2021regression`
