# Optional recap: random variables and the Central Limit Theorem

:::{admonition} Optional foundation
:class: note
This subsection reviews notation used from Session 2 onward. It is optional for students who are already comfortable with random variables, probability distributions, expectation, variance, and the Central Limit Theorem (CLT). It does not introduce Bayesian updating.
:::

## 1. From uncertain outcomes to numerical variables

A **random variable** assigns a numerical value to every possible outcome of an uncertain process. The randomness concerns which outcome will be observed; after an outcome occurs, the value is determined.

Suppose two fair six-sided dice are rolled and

$$
X=\text{number of dice showing a six}.
$$

Then $X$ can take the values $0$, $1$, or $2$:

| $x$ | Event | $P(X=x)$ |
|---:|---|---:|
| 0 | Neither die shows six | $25/36$ |
| 1 | Exactly one die shows six | $10/36$ |
| 2 | Both dice show six | $1/36$ |

The possible values of the random variable are not the same as the elementary outcomes. There are 36 ordered dice outcomes, but only three possible values of $X$.

Random variables connect an empirical question to a probability model. In this course they may represent a binary response, number of correct answers, response time, rating, participant effect, or future observation.

## 2. Discrete and continuous random variables

A **discrete** random variable takes values in a finite or countable set. Examples include number correct, number of events, or a binary response coded as 0 and 1.

Its probability mass function (PMF) is

$$
p_X(x)=P(X=x),
$$

where $0\le p_X(x)\le 1$ and $\sum_x p_X(x)=1$.

A **continuous** random variable can take values across an interval. Examples include duration, distance, or a measurement treated as continuous.

Its probability density function (PDF), $f_X(x)$, satisfies

$$
f_X(x)\ge 0,
\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\,dx=1.
$$

For continuous $X$, probability is area rather than curve height:

$$
P(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx.
$$

The value $f_X(x)$ is a density and may exceed 1. The probability of any exact point is zero when the distribution is genuinely continuous: $P(X=x)=0$.

## 3. The cumulative distribution function

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) works for both discrete and continuous variables:

$$
F_X(x)=P(X\le x).
$$

Every CDF:

- remains between 0 and 1;
- is non-decreasing, although it need not be strictly increasing;
- approaches 0 in the far left tail and 1 in the far right tail;
- has jumps for discrete probability masses and is continuous for a continuous distribution.

Interval probabilities can be recovered from the CDF. For example,

$$
P(a<X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).
$$

## 4. Expectation and variance

Expectation describes the probability-weighted centre of a random variable. For a discrete variable,

$$
\mathbb E[X]=\sum_x x\,P(X=x),
$$

and for a continuous variable,

$$
\mathbb E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx.
$$

For the number of sixes in two rolls,

$$
\mathbb E[X]
=0\frac{25}{36}+1\frac{10}{36}+2\frac{1}{36}
=\frac13.
$$

Expectation is linear even when variables are dependent:

$$
\mathbb E[aX+bY+c]
=a\mathbb E[X]+b\mathbb E[Y]+c.
$$

Variance describes squared dispersion around the mean:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)
=\mathbb E\!\left[(X-\mathbb E[X])^2\right].
$$

For two variables,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)
=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)
+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).
$$

Variances therefore add when covariance is zero. Independence is sufficient for zero covariance when the relevant moments exist, but it is not necessary.

## 5. Functions and summaries of random variables

If $X$ is random, then $g(X)$ is also a random variable. This simple fact supports most Bayesian summaries:

- an indicator $I(X>c)$ represents whether a threshold is crossed;
- a difference $X_1-X_2$ represents a contrast;
- an average $\bar X$ represents an aggregate across observations;
- a transformed parameter such as $\exp(\beta)$ represents an odds or rate ratio.

The expectation of a nonlinear transformation is generally not the transformation of the expectation:

$$
\mathbb E[g(X)]\ne g(\mathbb E[X]).
$$

This is why posterior transformations and contrasts will later be calculated draw by draw.

## 6. The Central Limit Theorem

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be independent and identically distributed observations with finite mean $\mu$ and finite, non-zero variance $\sigma^2$. Their sample mean is

$$
\bar X_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.
$$

The classical Central Limit Theorem states that, as $n$ grows,

$$
\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{d}
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1).
$$

Equivalently, for sufficiently large $n$ under these conditions,

$$
\bar X_n\approx
\operatorname{Normal}\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right).
$$

The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the mean is therefore approximately

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.
$$

### Example: average of repeated dice rolls

For one fair die, $\mu=3.5$ and $\sigma^2=35/12$. For the mean of $n=36$ independent rolls,

$$
\mathbb E[\bar X]=3.5,
\qquad
\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)
=\sqrt{\frac{35/12}{36}}
\approx 0.285.
$$

The individual outcomes remain discrete and non-Normal. It is the distribution of the average across repeated samples that becomes approximately Normal.

## 7. What the CLT does not say

The CLT does **not** imply that:

- the raw observations become Normally distributed;
- every sample of size 30 is automatically large enough;
- strong dependence, changing distributions, extreme skew, or infinite variance can be ignored;
- a representative sample has been obtained;
- measurement bias disappears as $n$ increases;
- a Bayesian posterior must be Normal in every finite dataset.

The required sample size depends on the distribution and dependence structure. Heavy tails, rare events, clustering, repeated measures, and time dependence may slow or invalidate the simplest approximation.

### CLT versus the law of large numbers

The **law of large numbers** says that $\bar X_n$ converges to $\mu$ under suitable conditions. The **Central Limit Theorem** describes the approximate distribution and $1/\sqrt n$ scale of the remaining sampling fluctuation. They answer related but different questions.

## 8. Connection to the course

The CLT helps explain why Normal approximations often work for sums and averages. Bayesian analysis, however, begins with the observation process and the quantity of interest rather than assuming that every outcome is Normal. Later sessions will model binary, count, positive, ordered, and hierarchical outcomes directly.
