# Hierarchical Models and Partial Pooling

## 1. The Multilevel Challenge: Structured Heterogeneity

In psychological, cognitive, and social research, data rarely consist of isolated, independent individuals. Instead, observations are naturally **nested in clusters**:
- Repeated reaction time trials nested within **human participants**.
- Students nested within **classrooms and schools**.
- Patients nested within **clinics and hospitals**.
- Voters nested within **counties and regions**.

When analyzing clustered data, simple classical models face a dilemma:
1. **Complete Pooling (Underfitting)**: Lump all groups together into a single global model ($y_i = \alpha + \beta x_i + \epsilon_i$). This ignores group differences and understates uncertainty by pretending all observations are independent.
2. **No Pooling (Overfitting)**: Estimate a completely independent model for every group ($y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta_j x_{ij} + \epsilon_{ij}$). For groups with small sample sizes ($n_j = 3$), estimates are wildly noisy and overfit to sampling noise.

**Hierarchical (Multilevel) Bayesian Modeling** resolves this dilemma through **Partial Pooling**: estimating group-specific parameters $\alpha_j$ that are drawn from a shared population hyperdistribution.

## 2. Exchangeability and Adaptive Shrinkage

Assume group means $\alpha_j$ are conditionally exchangeable draws from a common Gaussian population with grand mean $\mu_\alpha$ and between-group standard deviation $\tau$:

$$
\alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha, \tau), \quad j = 1, \dots, J
$$

$$
y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j, \sigma), \quad i = 1, \dots, n_j
$$

The Bayesian posterior mean for group $j$ is an exact **precision-weighted compromise**:

$$
\hat{\alpha}_j = (1 - B_j) \bar{y}_j + B_j \mu_\alpha
$$

where the **shrinkage factor $B_j \in [0, 1]$** is:

$$
B_j = \frac{\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j}}{\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j} + \tau^2} = \frac{1}{1 + n_j \frac{\tau^2}{\sigma^2}}
$$

### The Three Rules of Adaptive Shrinkage
1. **Sample Size ($n_j$)**: Groups with few observations ($n_j = 4$) have large measurement noise ($\sigma^2 / n_j$), so $B_j \approx 1$ and they **shrink heavily** toward the grand mean $\mu_\alpha$. Groups with large samples ($n_j = 100$) shrink very little.
2. **Between-Group Variance ($\tau$)**: If groups are genuinely diverse ($\tau \gg \sigma$), shrinkage is minimal. If groups are nearly identical ($\tau \approx 0$), the model collapses automatically to complete pooling.
3. **Information Borrowing**: Small groups borrow statistical strength from the entire population, preventing extreme false discoveries.

## 3. Centered vs. Non-Centered Parameterizations (Neal's Funnel)

When fitting hierarchical models with MCMC (HMC/NUTS), the joint geometry of $(\alpha_j, \tau)$ forms a steep, narrow geometry known as **Neal's Funnel**: as $\tau \to 0$, the plausible space for $\alpha_j$ contracts sharply. Standard samplers encounter **divergent transitions** in the neck of the funnel.

### The Solution: Non-Centered Transformation
Instead of sampling $\alpha_j$ directly from $\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha, \tau)$, we sample independent standard normal offsets $z_j$ and scale them deterministically:

$$\begin{array}{l|l}
\textbf{Centered Parameterization (Fails when } N \textbf{ is small)} & \textbf{Non-Centered Parameterization (Robust MCMC)} \\
\hline
\tau \sim \operatorname{HalfNormal}(s) & \tau \sim \operatorname{HalfNormal}(s) \\
\alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha, \tau) & z_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, 1) \\
& \alpha_j = \mu_\alpha + \tau \cdot z_j \\
y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j, \sigma) & y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j, \sigma)
\end{array}$$

```python
import pymc as pm

with pm.Model() as non_centered_hierarchical:
    # Hyperpriors
    mu_alpha = pm.Normal("mu_alpha", mu=50.0, sigma=15.0)
```

## 4. Varying Intercepts and Varying Slopes

When both baseline performance $\alpha_j$ and treatment sensitivity $\beta_j$ vary across participants, they may be correlated (e.g., participants with higher baseline skill show less improvement under training).

We model varying effects as draws from a **2D Multivariate Normal distribution**:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \alpha_j \\ \beta_j \end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}\left( \begin{bmatrix} \mu_\alpha \\ \mu_\beta \end{bmatrix}, \, \mathbf{\Sigma} \right), \quad \mathbf{\Sigma} = \begin{bmatrix} \tau_\alpha & 0 \\ 0 & \tau_\beta \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{\Omega} \begin{bmatrix} \tau_\alpha & 0 \\ 0 & \tau_\beta \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $\mathbf{\Omega}$ is the correlation matrix given an **LKJ Cholesky correlation prior**:

```python
with pm.Model() as varying_slopes_model:
    mu_fixed = pm.Normal("mu_fixed", mu=[50.0, 0.0], sigma=10.0, shape=2)
    tau_vec = pm.HalfNormal("tau_vec", sigma=5.0, shape=2)
    
    # LKJ Cholesky decomposition of correlation matrix
    chol, corr, stds = pm.LKJCholeskyCov("chol_cov", n=2, eta=2.0, sd_dist=pm.HalfNormal.dist(sigma=5.0))
    
    # Non-centered MVN draws
    z_mat = pm.Normal("z_mat", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0, shape=(2, n_groups))
    effects = pm.Deterministic("effects", mu_fixed[:, None] + pm.math.dot(chol, z_mat))
```

## 5. Crossed Participant and Item Effects in Cognitive Science

In experimental psychology, trials cross **participants** ($j = 1, \dots, J$) with **stimuli/words/items** ($k = 1, \dots, K$). The hierarchical model accommodates crossed random effects naturally:

$$
y_{ijk} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_{ijk}, \sigma), \quad \mu_{ijk} = \alpha + u_j + v_k + \beta x_{ijk}
$$

where $u_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \tau_{\text{subj}})$ and $v_k \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \tau_{\text{item}})$. This simultaneously controls for participant idiosyncrasies and item difficulty.

## 6. Group Rankings and Rank Uncertainty

When policymakers or school administrators publish "league tables" of hospital quality or school performance:
- Standard ranking of raw sample means $\bar{y}_j$ heavily over-ranks small outlier clinics due to sampling noise.
- Hierarchical models provide the full **posterior distribution over ranks** ($P(\text{Rank}_j = 1 \mid y)$).
- A school with the highest point estimate may have a 95% credible interval for its rank spanning from 1st to 28th!

---

## Key Takeaways

- Hierarchical models provide **optimal adaptive shrinkage**, protecting against both underfitting (complete pooling) and overfitting (no pooling).
- The shrinkage factor $B_j$ depends dynamically on **group sample size $n_j$** and the variance ratio $\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}$.
- Always use **non-centered parameterizations** for variance components when group sizes are moderate or small to eliminate MCMC divergences.
- League tables and school rankings must always report **rank uncertainty distributions**, never point rankings alone.
