## 1. Clustered Data Break the Independence Assumption

In Lessons 7 and 8, each row in our dataset represented an independent individual (one mother–child pair in KidIQ). But in experimental psychology, cognitive neuroscience, and social science, data are rarely collected as isolated, independent points. Instead, measurements are naturally **nested in clusters**:
- Multiple reaction time trials from the **same human participant**.
- Multiple students inside the **same classroom or school**.
- Multiple patients treated at the **same hospital or clinic**.

When measurements come from repeated observations, standard regression faces a major dilemma:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                  THE POOLING DILEMMA                                    |
+---------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
| 1. Complete Pooling (Underfitting)          | 2. No Pooling (Overfitting)               |
|                                             |                                           |
| Lump all data together into one global      | Fit 18 completely separate regressions.   |
| line: y_i = α + β·x_i + ε_i.                | y_ij = α_j + β_j·x_ij + ε_ij.             |
|                                             |                                           |
| Ignores real individual differences.        | Small clusters with few trials produce    |
| Falsely pretends all N = 180 trials are     | wildly noisy, unreliable estimates        |
| independent people, giving spuriously tiny  | that overfit to sample noise.             |
| uncertainty intervals.                      |                                           |
+---------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
```

**Hierarchical (Multilevel) Bayesian Modeling** resolves this dilemma through **Partial Pooling**: we estimate individual parameters $\alpha_j$ for each participant, but assume these parameters come from a **shared population distribution**.

### Anchor Model Card: Sleep Deprivation Experiment (`sleepstudy`)

| Field | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Outcome** | Average reaction time in milliseconds ($y \in \mathbb{R}^+$) |
| **Structure** | Longitudinal trials: 10 daily measurements across $J = 18$ subjects ($N = 180$) |
| **Predictor** | `Days` of sleep restriction ($0$ to $9$ days, 3 hours of sleep per night) |
| **Primary Estimand** | Population baseline latency $\mu_\alpha$, sleep deprivation slowdown $\mu_\beta$, and subject-specific trajectories |
| **Observation Model** | $y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j + \beta_j \text{Days}_{ij}, \, \sigma)$ |
| **Hierarchy** | Subject intercepts $\alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha, \tau_\alpha)$ with non-centered parameterization |
| **Substantive Goal** | Distinguish general cognitive vulnerability to sleep loss from stable individual differences |

## 2. Exchangeability and Adaptive Shrinkage

Suppose each participant $j$ has a baseline reaction time $\alpha_j$. We treat the participants as draws from a shared population distribution with average latency $\mu_\alpha$ and between-person spread $\tau$:

$$
\alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha, \tau), \quad j = 1, \dots, J
$$

$$
y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j, \sigma), \quad i = 1, \dots, n_j
$$

The Bayesian posterior estimate for participant $j$ is an exact **precision-weighted compromise**:

$$
\hat{\alpha}_j = (1 - B_j) \bar{y}_j + B_j \mu_\alpha
$$

where the **shrinkage factor $B_j \in [0, 1]$** is:

$$
B_j = \frac{\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j}}{\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j} + \tau^2} = \frac{1}{1 + n_j \frac{\tau^2}{\sigma^2}}
$$

*Figure 9.1: Partial Pooling and Adaptive Shrinkage. Panel (a) shows how extreme, noisy raw sample means shrink inward toward the grand mean (dashed line). Panel (b) shows that shrinkage decreases smoothly as cluster sample size $n_j$ increases:*

<iframe src="../_static/fig10_partial_pooling_shrinkage.html" width="100%" height="400px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

### The Three Rules of Adaptive Shrinkage
1. **Cluster Sample Size ($n_j$)**: A participant with only 3 trials has large sampling noise ($\sigma^2 / n_j$). The model shrinks their estimate heavily toward the grand mean $\mu_\alpha$. A participant with 50 trials shrinks barely at all.
2. **Between-Person Heterogeneity ($\tau$)**: If people are wildly different from each other ($\tau \gg \sigma$), shrinkage is minimal. If everyone is nearly identical ($\tau \approx 0$), the model automatically collapses to complete pooling.
3. **Information Borrowing**: Smaller clusters borrow statistical strength from the rest of the sample, preventing extreme false alarms without discarding individual variation.

## 3. Centered vs. Non-Centered Parameterizations: Escaping Neal’s Funnel

In Lesson 6, we introduced the gallery of failed sampler fits. One of the classic failures was the **funnel geometry** (Rubin’s Eight Schools), which generated over 200 divergent transitions. Now we learn the mathematical cure.

### The Geometric Trap
When between-group spread $\tau$ is small, the plausible range for individual effects $\alpha_j$ contracts sharply around $\mu_\alpha$. In parameter space $(\alpha_j, \tau)$, this creates a steep, narrow neck called **Neal’s Funnel**. The MCMC sampler's trajectory cannot resolve the sharp curvature in the neck and registers **divergent transitions**.

```
                NEAL'S FUNNEL
         τ  ^      \        /
            |       \  ••  /     <-- Wide opening (easy sampling)
            |        \ •  /
            |         \••/
            |          ||        <-- Narrow neck: MCMC hits divergences!
            +----------+---------> α_j
                       μα
```

### The Solution: Non-Centered Transformation
Instead of sampling $\alpha_j$ directly from the shrinking distribution $\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha, \tau)$, we sample independent standard normal deviations $z_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, 1)$ and scale them deterministically:

$$\begin{array}{l|l}
\textbf{Centered (Fails when data are sparse)} & \textbf{Non-Centered (MCMC is robust and divergence-free)} \\
\hline
\tau \sim \operatorname{HalfNormal}(s) & \tau \sim \operatorname{HalfNormal}(s) \\
\alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha, \tau) & z_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, 1) \\
& \alpha_j = \mu_\alpha + \tau \cdot z_j \\
y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j, \sigma) & y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j, \sigma)
\end{array}$$

In the non-centered parameterization, $z_j$ and $\tau$ are completely independent in the prior. The geometry becomes an open cylinder, and divergences vanish!

```python
import pymc as pm
import numpy as np
import arviz as az

with pm.Model() as non_centered_sleep:
    # 1. Population hyperpriors
    mu_alpha = pm.Normal("mu_alpha", mu=250.0, sigma=30.0) # Population baseline RT (~250 ms)
    tau_alpha = pm.HalfNormal("tau_alpha", sigma=30.0)      # Between-person spread
    sigma_y = pm.HalfNormal("sigma_y", sigma=25.0)          # Trial-to-trial noise
    
    # 2. Non-centered individual offsets
    z_alpha = pm.Normal("z_alpha", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0, shape=n_subjects)
    alpha = pm.Deterministic("alpha", mu_alpha + tau_alpha * z_alpha)
    
    # 3. Overall effect of sleep deprivation
    beta_days = pm.Normal("beta_days", mu=10.0, sigma=5.0) # Slowdown per day
    
    # 4. Conditional mean: mu_ij = alpha_j + beta * Days_ij
    mu_ij = alpha[subject_idx] + beta_days * days_data
    y_obs = pm.Normal("y_obs", mu=mu_ij, sigma=sigma_y, observed=reaction_times)
    
    idata_hier = pm.sample(draws=2000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.95)
```

## 4. Varying Intercepts and Varying Slopes

In the model above, every participant has their own baseline speed $\alpha_j$, but they are assumed to slow down at the exact same rate $\beta$.

In reality, some people are highly resilient to sleep deprivation, while others deteriorate rapidly. We allow both the **intercept and the slope to vary across participants**:

$$
\mu_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta_j \text{Days}_{ij}
$$

In Python, high-level modeling libraries like **Bambi** allow us to specify this using intuitive formula syntax matching `lme4`:

```python
import bambi as bmb

# Fit varying intercepts and varying slopes by Subject
model_sleep = bmb.Model(
    "Reaction ~ Days + (Days | Subject)",
    data=sleep_df
)
idata_sleep = model_sleep.fit(draws=2000, tune=1000)
```

Bambi automatically implements the non-centered parameterization and places an LKJ correlation prior on the joint distribution of intercepts and slopes.

## 5. Prediction Targets: Existing Unit vs. New Unit

When generating predictions from a hierarchical model, we must always clarify our scientific target:

1. **Predicting for an Existing Participant ($j$)**:
   We use their specific posterior parameter draws $\alpha_j^{(s)}$:

   $$
   \widetilde{y}_{j, \text{new}}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_j^{(s)} + \beta_j^{(s)} \text{Days}^*, \, \sigma^{(s)})
   $$

   This prediction captures that participant's known personal traits.

2. **Predicting for a New, Unobserved Participant**:
   We do not know where a new person sits in the distribution. We must sample a fresh draw from the population hyperdistribution:

   $$
   \alpha_{\text{new}}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha^{(s)}, \tau_\alpha^{(s)}), \quad \widetilde{y}_{\text{new}}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_{\text{new}}^{(s)} + \beta_{\text{new}}^{(s)} \text{Days}^*, \, \sigma^{(s)})
   $$

   Predictions for new participants are much wider because they include between-person variability $\tau$.

## 8. Concept Check

```{dropdown} Concept Check: Complete Pooling vs. No Pooling vs. Partial Pooling
A clinic tests a new therapy on 10 patients across 5 doctors. Doctor A treated 1 patient who recovered (100% success rate). Doctor B treated 8 patients with 4 recoveries (50% success rate). What will partial pooling do to Doctor A's estimate?

**Answer:** Under No Pooling, Doctor A's estimated success rate would be 100%, but with massive uncertainty. Under Complete Pooling, Doctor A's data would be averaged with everyone else, ignoring any doctor differences. Partial Pooling recognizes that Doctor A has a tiny sample size ($n=1$), so it heavily shrinks Doctor A's estimate toward the overall population recovery rate, while keeping Doctor B's estimate close to 50%.
```

## Key Takeaways

- Hierarchical models provide **optimal adaptive shrinkage**, balancing underfitting (complete pooling) and overfitting (no pooling).
- The shrinkage factor $B_j$ depends on cluster sample size $n_j$ and the variance ratio $\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}$.
- **Non-centered parameterizations** eliminate MCMC divergences in Neal's Funnel when cluster sample sizes are small.
- Always distinguish predictions for an **observed unit** from predictions for a **new, unseen unit**.
