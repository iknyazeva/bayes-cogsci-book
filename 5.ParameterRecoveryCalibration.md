## 1. Why Test Parameter Recovery Before Real Data Collection?

Before deploying a Bayesian model on empirical observations, researchers check that the estimation workflow can learn the generating parameters when the truth is known with certainty.

**Parameter recovery** is a controlled experiment on your statistical estimator:
1. We choose or sample true parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{true}}$ from a prior or grid.
2. We simulate synthetic observations forward from the likelihood: $y_{\text{sim}} \sim p(y \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{true}})$.
3. We fit the planned Bayesian model to the synthetic dataset to obtain the posterior $p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid y_{\text{sim}})$.
4. We compare the posterior center $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and credible interval $[L, U]$ directly against $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{true}}$.

```{important}
**What recovery establishes — and what it does not:**
Successful recovery provides evidence that the selected parameters can be estimated under the *tested* scenarios (parameter values, sample sizes, summaries, computational conditions) and that the implementation passes these checks. It does **not** prove identifiability, does not rule out other implementation errors, and does not test the model against the real world. A finite simulation cannot deliver such guarantees, and the generator and the fitting procedure can even share the same mistake.

Two distinct concepts help here:
* **Structural identifiability** — whether distinct parameter values imply distinct observable *distributions*. If two parameters always produce the same data distribution, no amount of data separates them.
* **Practical identifiability** — how well *your design and sample size* constrain parameters that are structurally distinguishable. A strong prior can stabilize estimation without making the likelihood identifiable.

Recovery checks practical identifiability empirically, for the scenarios you ran. Real-world adequacy is a separate question requiring posterior predictive checking and domain validation.
```

:::{note} Recovery tests the model you assume — not the model you need
Parameter recovery asks whether parameters can be estimated *within a specified model*. It does not show that two competing cognitive mechanisms produce distinguishable behavior: two different mechanisms can generate observationally similar data, and a recovery study inside either one will look perfect. If scientific claims rest on distinguishing mechanisms, design the simulation to compare the *competing* models' predictions — alternative conditions, stimulus choices, or additional measurements — rather than only recovering each model's own parameters.
:::

**What to record in a recovery study** (practitioner's checklist):

- [ ] Bias and RMSE *at each simulated truth* (not only pooled across truths)
- [ ] Average interval width per truth (precision, not just accuracy)
- [ ] Prior-to-posterior contraction (how much the data actually moved the estimate)
- [ ] Joint parameter relationships (correlations, funnel shapes), not just one parameter at a time
- [ ] The exact scenarios tested — and a sentence stating that conclusions are conditional on them

## 2. The $45^\circ$ Diagonal Recovery Plot

In a repeated recovery study across $M = 100 \dots 1{,}000$ simulated datasets, we plot estimated posterior centers $\hat{\theta}^{(m)}$ (y-axis) against generating truths $\theta_{\text{true}}^{(m)}$ (x-axis), accompanied by the identity line $y = x$:

```
        θ̂ (Posterior Estimate)
          ▲
          │          .  /  (Ideal Recovery: hugs 45° diagonal)
          │            / .
          │           /.
          │          /  .
          │       . /
          │        / .
          │       /
          └─────────────────────► θ_true (Known Generating Value)
```

### Reading the Recovery Grid

Treat the patterns below as **possible explanations to investigate**, not verdicts — each visual pattern has more than one possible cause, and a high true-versus-estimated *correlation* can coexist with substantial estimation error (it also depends on the range of truths simulated):

| Visual Pattern | Possible Explanations to Investigate | Next Step |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Points hug the $45^\circ$ line** | Estimates track the truths *under the tested scenarios*; check width and coverage too — closeness to the diagonal alone does not establish unbiasedness or joint identification. | Complement with per-truth bias/RMSE and coverage. |
| **Consistent vertical shift above/below $y = x$** | Structural bias, indexing bug, or prior–data conflict. | Audit code for indexing offsets and likelihood sign errors. |
| **Horizontal flat cloud across x-axis** | The data add little information: possible non-identification, strong shrinkage, or computational failure — the posterior may be echoing the prior. | Check prior vs posterior; reparameterize or alter the design. |
| **Funnel shape (spread widens at large $\theta$)** | Heteroscedasticity or scale-dependent information (e.g. variance increasing with mean). | Check link functions or variance transformations. |
| **Slanted line with slope $< 1$ (attenuation)** | Expected Bayesian shrinkage toward the prior mean under weakly informative priors. | Normal behavior; evaluate whether shrinkage improves out-of-sample RMSE. |

## 3. Overfitting, Flexible Models, and Bayesian Regularization

Simulation is indispensable for visualizing what occurs when a flexible statistical model is given the capacity to overfit empirical noise. Consider fitting a high-degree polynomial basis ($M = 9$) to $N = 12$ noisy observations:

1. **Unregularized MLE / Flat Prior**: The estimator chases idiosyncratic sample noise, oscillating wildly near boundaries (the Runge phenomenon) and producing catastrophic out-of-sample prediction errors.
2. **Bayesian Regularization (Gaussian Prior $\beta_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, 1/\lambda)$)**: Placing a zero-centered prior on coefficients acts as a soft shrinkage mechanism, pulling high-order polynomial weights toward zero while retaining flexible curvature where data evidence demands it.

## 4. Calibration and Empirical 95% Coverage

A Bayesian credible interval is calibrated, for a stated way of repeating the experiment, if nominal $100(1-\alpha)\%$ intervals contain the true parameter value with the declared probability:

$$
\widehat{C} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbb{I}\left( L^{(m)} \le \theta_{\text{true}}^{(m)} \le U^{(m)} \right).
$$

**Three ways to repeat the experiment — three different questions:**

| Experiment | How truths are chosen | What the coverage number answers |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **1. Fixed-truth coverage** | $\theta_{\text{true}}$ held at one value (e.g. $0.20$) | A frequentist operating characteristic of the Bayesian interval *at that truth*: how often the interval captures this value across hypothetical datasets. |
| **2. Chosen-truth grid** | A grid of scientifically selected truths | How the interval performs across the scenarios you care about — the natural design-analysis view. |
| 3. **Prior-averaged calibration** | $\theta^{(m)} \sim p(\theta)$, the *same proper prior* used in the analysis | Whether simulation and inference are internally consistent, averaging over the model's own assumptions. |

Two facts prevent common misreadings:

* **Fixed-truth coverage of a Bayesian interval need not equal its posterior probability.** Undercoverage can be substantial and need not occur only near boundaries — a concentrated prior in conflict with an *interior* truth is enough. This is a property of the procedure, not automatically a bug; the design lesson is to evaluate coverage at your actual scenarios (row 2), not one convenient point.
* **The prior-averaged identity has conditions.** If each set carries posterior probability exactly $100(1-\alpha)\%$, then the joint prior-predictive coverage is exactly $100(1-\alpha)\%$ — *provided* the generating prior matches the analysis prior, the likelihoods match, and the credible sets are computed correctly. A finite empirical estimate fluctuates around that value with Monte Carlo error, and coverage averaged over an arbitrary grid or a *different* design prior does **not** inherit this identity {cite}`talts2018validating`.

### Monte Carlo Uncertainty of Coverage Audits

Because coverage is estimated from a finite number of simulations $M$, the empirical estimate $\widehat{C}$ carries Monte Carlo Standard Error (MCSE):

$$
\operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat{C}) = \sqrt{\frac{C(1 - C)}{M}}.
$$

* For $M = 1{,}000$ replications and nominal $C = 0.95$:
  $$\operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat{C}) = \sqrt{\frac{0.95 \times 0.05}{1{,}000}} \approx 0.0069 \quad (\approx 0.7\%).$$
* An empirical coverage of $94.1\%$ therefore falls inside the approximate 95% *reference range* $[93.6\%, 96.4\%]$ expected under nominal coverage — it is **compatible with Monte Carlo variation**. Note the direction of the claim: being inside the range is what noise alone could produce, so this check does not *establish* calibration; being far outside it is evidence of a problem.

## 5. Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC)

**Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC)** is a rigorous computational diagnostic that checks the *consistency of data simulation and posterior computation* under the assumed model {cite}`talts2018validating`.

**Algorithm:**
1. Draw true parameters from the prior: $\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \sim p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$.
2. Simulate a full dataset: $y^* \sim p(y \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)$.
3. Sample $L$ posterior draws using MCMC: $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)}, \dots, \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(L)} \sim p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid y^*)$.
4. Compute the **rank statistic**: count how many posterior draws are smaller than the true value $\theta^*$:
   $$r = \sum_{l=1}^L \mathbb{I}\left( \theta^{(l)} < \theta^* \right) \in \{0, 1, \dots, L\}.$$
5. Repeat across $M = 1{,}000$ iterations and plot a histogram of the ranks $r$.

```
Rank Histogram Shapes & Diagnostic Interpretation:
┌─────────────────────┬─────────────────────┬─────────────────────┐
│      UNIFORM        │      U-SHAPED       │     HUMP-SHAPED     │
│   ┌─────────────┐   │   █         █       │          ▄█▄        │
│   │█████████████│   │   ██       ██       │        ▄█████▄      │
│   └─────────────┘   │   ██▄▄▄▄▄▄▄██       │      ▄█████████▄    │
│  No problem found   │   Overconfident     │   Underconfident    │
│  by this diagnostic │  Posterior too thin │ Posterior too broad │
└─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┘
```

* **Uniform**: this diagnostic has found no problem.
* **U-shaped**: consistent with an overconfident posterior (variance too narrow; true parameters fall into the extreme tails too often).
* **Hump-shaped**: consistent with an underconfident posterior (intervals excessively conservative).
* **Sloped (left or right)**: consistent with systematic bias upward or downward.

### Two qualifications that keep SBC honest

**1. The rank construction assumes effectively independent posterior draws.** The algorithm inserts consecutive MCMC draws directly into the rank, and the discrete-uniform reference distribution assumes conditionally independent draws for continuous parameters. Autocorrelated chains can distort rank histograms *even when the chain targets the correct posterior*. Use enough posterior draws per fit that the draws are effectively independent, or a dependence-aware variant; SBC is one of the special settings where thinning can genuinely help — unlike routine inference, where Session 6 correctly advises against thinning by default. For discrete parameters, ties require explicit handling.

**2. Uniformity is necessary, not sufficient.** A detectable rank discrepancy is evidence of a problem in the tested workflow; **no detectable discrepancy means only that this diagnostic has not found one**. A simple counterexample: a faulty procedure that ignores every dataset and returns fresh draws from the prior produces *perfectly uniform* ranks — the generating parameter and the "posterior" draws are identically distributed — despite the procedure never learning anything from data. So pair SBC with analytic benchmarks, recovery and contraction checks, and rank checks on the *scientifically relevant* quantities (derived contrasts, predictions), not only on raw parameters. SBC checks internal consistency of simulation and inference under the assumed model; it says nothing about the model's adequacy for human behavior.
