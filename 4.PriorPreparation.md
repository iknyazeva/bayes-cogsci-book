# Lesson 4 Appendix: Prior Choice and Updating

This appendix retains the prior preparation material associated with [Lesson 4](4.PriorsPosteriorPredictiveChecks.md). Use it when choosing a prior or reviewing how the posterior was obtained. The main lesson starts from an available posterior and follows the questions we can answer with it.

An informative prior represents substantial external knowledge. A weakly informative prior provides modest constraints on plausible parameter values. Flatness depends on the parameter scale, and an improper prior is not a probability distribution. The existing [prior reference](4.NonInformativePriors.md), [Jeffreys prior appendix](4.JeffreysPrior.md), and [conjugacy appendix](4.ConjugatePriors.md) develop these distinctions.

## 1. What a prior does

The prior has three related roles:

1. **Representation:** it records plausible values and constraints before the current observations are used.
2. **Regularization:** it reduces unstable estimates in directions where the likelihood is weak.
3. **Generation:** combined with the likelihood, it implies a distribution of possible datasets before data collection.

```{mermaid}
flowchart LR
    P["Prior over parameters<br/>p(theta)"] --> G["Generative model<br/>p(y | theta)"]
    G --> PP["Prior predictive outcomes<br/>p(y_sim)"]
    D["Observed data<br/>y"] --> L["Likelihood<br/>p(y | theta)"]
    P --> B["Bayesian update"]
    L --> B
    B --> POST["Posterior<br/>p(theta | y)"]
    POST --> REP["Posterior predictive replications<br/>p(y_rep | y)"]
```

:::{important}
The most useful first question about a prior is often not “Does this density look broad?” but “What observable datasets does this prior expect?” A prior can look harmless in parameter space and still imply absurd outcomes.
:::

### A practical vocabulary

| Prior type | Intended role | Example | Principal risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Informative | Represent substantial external knowledge | A prior for a small sex-ratio difference based on demographic evidence | Historical information may not transport to the new setting |
| Weakly informative | Exclude implausible magnitudes while allowing the data to move the posterior | A regularizing prior for a standardized regression coefficient | “Weak” has no meaning without the parameter scale |
| Flat or diffuse | Reduce local prior influence on a chosen scale | Constant density for a Normal location parameter | Flatness changes under transformation and can create improper posteriors |
| Reference or Jeffreys | Follow a formal information or invariance criterion | Beta(1/2, 1/2) for a Binomial probability | The rule can be difficult or controversial in multiparameter models |
| Improper | Supply a limiting reference calculation | p(mu) proportional to 1 on the real line | It is not a probability distribution and cannot be used for prior prediction |

The extended discussion is in {doc}`4.NonInformativePriors` and {doc}`4.JeffreysPrior`.

## 2. Eliciting a Beta prior on an interpretable scale

Suppose theta is a population proportion, such as support for a policy. For a Beta(alpha, beta) prior,

$$
\mathbb{E}(\theta)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}.
$$

In the conjugate Beta-Binomial model it is convenient to write

$$
\mu_0=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},
\qquad
n_0=\alpha+\beta,
$$

so that

$$
\alpha=n_0\mu_0,
\qquad
\beta=n_0(1-\mu_0).
$$

Here, n0 is an effective prior sample-size convention for this model: it controls concentration and makes the posterior mean a transparent weighted average. It should not be interpreted as literal previous observations unless the prior was actually constructed from comparable historical data.

:::{admonition} Demonstration: same center, different commitments
:class: tip
Beta(2, 2) and Beta(20, 20) are both centered at 0.5, but they do not express the same belief. For a future survey of 20 respondents, Beta(2, 2) allows a wide range of support counts, whereas Beta(20, 20) expects results much closer to 10 out of 20.

Before looking at either density, ask which future counts each prior should make plausible. Then inspect the prior predictive distribution, not only the prior density.
:::

For $y_{\mathrm{sim}}$ successes among $N$ future observations:

$$
p(y_{\mathrm{sim}})
=
\int_0^1
p(y_{\mathrm{sim}}\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$

Under a Beta prior and Binomial likelihood this is a Beta-Binomial distribution.

## 3. Exact Beta-Binomial updating

Let

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),
\qquad
k\mid\theta,N\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(N,\theta).
$$

Multiplication of the prior and likelihood kernels gives

$$
p(\theta\mid k,N)
\propto
\theta^{\alpha+k-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+N-k-1},
$$

and therefore

$$
\theta\mid k,N
\sim
\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+k,\beta+N-k).
$$

### How the prior and data contribute to the posterior mean

The posterior mean can be written exactly as a weighted compromise:

$$
\mathbb{E}(\theta\mid k,N)
=
\underbrace{\left(\frac{n_0}{n_0+N}\right)}_{\text{Prior Weight } w_{\text{prior}}} \mu_0
+
\underbrace{\left(\frac{N}{n_0+N}\right)}_{\text{Data Weight } w_{\text{data}}} \left(\frac{k}{N}\right).
$$

:::{admonition} Example: training completion
:class: tip
Suppose an organizational researcher studies employee training completion. The prior represents moderate baseline experience ($\mu_0 = 0.50$) with prior strength $n_0 = 20$ ($\text{Beta}(10, 10)$).

1. **Small Pilot Study ($N = 10, k = 7$, sample proportion $= 70\%$)**:

$$
w_{\text{prior}} = \frac{20}{20 + 10} = 66.7\%, \quad w_{\text{data}} = \frac{10}{20 + 10} = 33.3\%
$$

$$
\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = (0.667 \times 0.500) + (0.333 \times 0.700) = 0.333 + 0.233 = \mathbf{0.567} \quad (\text{Posterior: } \text{Beta}(17, 13))
$$

   *Takeaway*: Because $n_0 > N$, the prior acts as a regularizing anchor, keeping the posterior closer to $0.50$ than to the noisy sample proportion ($0.70$).

2. **Large Replication ($N = 200, k = 140$, sample proportion $= 70\%$)**:

$$
w_{\text{prior}} = \frac{20}{20 + 200} = 9.1\%, \quad w_{\text{data}} = \frac{200}{20 + 200} = 90.9\%
$$

$$
\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = (0.091 \times 0.500) + (0.909 \times 0.700) = 0.045 + 0.636 = \mathbf{0.682} \quad (\text{Posterior: } \text{Beta}(150, 70))
$$

   *Takeaway*: As sample evidence accumulates, the data weight overwhelms the fixed prior, pulling the posterior tightly toward $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = 0.70$.
:::

````{dropdown} Check the limiting cases
- If N is small relative to n0, the posterior mean remains close to the prior mean.
- If N grows while alpha and beta remain fixed, the sample proportion receives increasing weight.
- If the prior assigns zero density to a neighborhood of the data-supported value, ordinary “the data will overwhelm the prior” intuition can fail.
````

### Demonstration: four priors under the same evidence

The following code reproduces the comparison as three layers: prior, likelihood normalized to integrate to one, and posterior. Run it once with $N=20$, then change to $N=200$ while keeping $k/N=0.70$.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import beta

x = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 1_000)
N, k = 20, 14

priors = {
    "Jeffreys Beta(0.5, 0.5)": (0.5, 0.5),
    "Uniform Beta(1, 1)": (1.0, 1.0),
    "Weakly informative Beta(2, 2)": (2.0, 2.0),
    "Contextual Beta(6, 4)": (6.0, 4.0),
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(10, 7), sharex=True)
for ax, (name, (a, b)) in zip(axes.flat, priors.items()):
    prior = beta.pdf(x, a, b)
    likelihood = x**k * (1-x)**(N-k)
    likelihood /= np.trapezoid(likelihood, x)
    posterior = beta.pdf(x, a + k, b + N - k)

    ax.plot(x, prior, "--", label="prior")
    ax.plot(x, likelihood, ":", label="normalized likelihood")
    ax.plot(x, posterior, linewidth=2.5, label="posterior")
    ax.set_title(name)
    ax.set_xlabel(r"$\theta$")

axes[0, 0].set_ylabel("density")
axes[1, 0].set_ylabel("density")
axes[0, 0].legend()
fig.tight_layout()
```

## Using Conjugate Priors with Poisson Data


###  Poisson Data

In cognitive science, count data arises naturally: the number of attention lapses per testing block, the number of fixations during a visual search trial, the number of errors per trial block in a learning task, or the number of action potentials a neuron fires per second. These counts are well modeled by the **Poisson distribution**.

Consider a researcher studying sustained attention using the psychomotor vigilance task (PVT). In each testing block, participants must respond quickly to a stimulus that appears at random intervals. Responses slower than 500 ms are counted as **attention lapses**. The number of lapses per block can be modeled as a Poisson random variable.

### Poisson Distribution and Likelihood

We model the number of attention lapses per block $Y_i$ as a Poisson random variable with parameter $\lambda$, which represents the average number of lapses per block. For a set of observations $y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n$, the likelihood function given $\lambda$ is:

$$
L(\lambda | y) = \lambda^{\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i} e^{-n\lambda} \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{y_i!}
$$

where:

- $\lambda > 0$ is the rate parameter (average lapses per block).
- $\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i$ is the total number of lapses observed across all blocks.
- $n$ is the number of testing blocks.

### Choosing a Prior for $\lambda$

Since $\lambda$ must be positive, we choose a prior distribution that is defined for positive values. A natural choice is the **Gamma distribution**, which is conjugate to the Poisson distribution.


The **Gamma distribution** is defined as:

$$
f(\lambda | \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta \lambda}, \quad \lambda > 0
$$

where:

- $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are shape and rate parameters, respectively.
- $\Gamma(\alpha)$ is the gamma function.

### Posterior Distribution

The posterior distribution is obtained by multiplying the likelihood by the prior. This gives:

$$
f(\lambda | y) \propto \lambda^{\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i + \alpha - 1} e^{-(n + \beta) \lambda}
$$

```{important}
**Gamma-Poisson posterior.** With a $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior and $n$ Poisson observations:

$$
\lambda \mid y \sim \text{Gamma} \left( \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i, \;\beta + n \right)
$$

The prior parameter $\alpha$ acts as a pseudo-count of prior events, and $\beta$ acts as a pseudo-count of prior observation periods. The data adds $\sum y_i$ observed events over $n$ periods.
```

### Posterior Mean

The mean of a Gamma random variable is given by $\frac{\alpha}{\beta}$. So, the posterior mean is:

$$
\text{Posterior Mean} = \frac{\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i}{\beta + n}
$$

```{important}
**Posterior mean as weighted average.** The posterior mean can be rewritten as:

$$
\text{Posterior Mean} = \frac{\beta}{\beta + n} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}_{\text{prior mean}} + \frac{n}{\beta + n} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i}{n}}_{\text{data mean}}
$$

As more data is collected ($n$ grows), the weight shifts from the prior mean to the data mean. This is a general property of conjugate models.
```

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example: Neural Spike Counting
:class: tip
A neuroscientist records the number of action potentials (spikes) from a single neuron in a series of 1-second time bins during a sustained stimulus. Spike counts per bin are naturally modeled as Poisson with rate $\lambda$ (the neuron's firing rate).

Based on prior recordings from similar neurons, the researcher expects a firing rate around 8 spikes/second, so they set a $\text{Gamma}(16, 2)$ prior (mean = $16/2 = 8$).

After observing $n = 10$ bins with a total of $\sum y_i = 120$ spikes, the posterior is:

$$
\lambda \mid y \sim \text{Gamma}(16 + 120, \; 2 + 10) = \text{Gamma}(136, 12)
$$

The posterior mean is $136/12 \approx 11.3$ spikes/second — the data (mean 12 spikes/s) pulls the estimate upward from the prior mean of 8.
```

## Choosing Hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$

### Strategy 1: Use Prior Knowledge

1. **Prior Mean**: Set based on prior knowledge or belief about the rate parameter (e.g., average lapses per block, spikes per second).
2. **Prior Variance**: Set based on the uncertainty in our belief. For the Gamma distribution, the variance is $\frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}$.

Given these two values, we can solve for $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

### Strategy 2: Effective Sample Size

- The effective sample size for the prior is $\beta$. We can set $\beta$ based on how confident we are in our prior knowledge relative to the data size.

## Using Vague Priors & The Boundary Warning

Historically, researchers attempted to represent "complete ignorance" by setting hyperparameters to near-zero values:

1. **Vague Gamma Prior**: Setting $\alpha = \epsilon$ and $\beta = \epsilon$ (e.g. $\text{Gamma}(0.001, 0.001)$).
2. **Posterior Under Vague Prior**:
$$
\text{Posterior Mean} = \frac{\epsilon + \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i}{\epsilon + n}
$$

:::{warning}
**The Boundary Sensitivity Trap (Gelman, 2006)**:
While $\text{Gamma}(\epsilon, \epsilon)$ appears visually flat on a wide scale, it places massive, infinite spike density near zero ($x \to 0$). For variance components or sparse count processes, setting $\epsilon = 0.001$ vs $\epsilon = 0.0001$ can dramatically alter posterior inferences. Modern Bayesian workflow recommends weakly informative scale priors (such as Half-Normal or Exponential) rather than limiting inverse-Gamma/Gamma vague defaults (see {doc}`4.NonInformativePriors` and {doc}`4.InverseGammaDistribution`).
:::

## Hierarchical Models

Conjugate models can be extended into hierarchical structures by placing priors on the hyperparameters themselves:

1. **Observations**: $Y_1, \ldots, Y_n$ follow a Bernoulli likelihood with parameter $\theta$.
2. **Parameter $\theta$**: Follows a Beta prior, $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$.
3. **Hyperparameters**: $\alpha$ and $\beta$ may also have their own priors (**hyperpriors**), creating additional levels in the hierarchy.

Hierarchical models provide greater flexibility for capturing uncertainty at multiple levels and are explored in detail in {doc}`10.HierarchicalModelsPartialPooling`.

## Key Takeaways

- **Beta-Bernoulli conjugacy**: A $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior with Bernoulli/Binomial data yields a $\text{Beta}(\alpha + \sum y_i, \; \beta + n - \sum y_i)$ posterior. The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ act as pseudo-counts.
- **Gamma-Poisson conjugacy**: A $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior with Poisson data yields a $\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum y_i, \; \beta + n)$ posterior.
- **Posterior mean as weighted average**: In both conjugate models, the posterior mean is a weighted combination of the prior mean and the data mean, with weights determined by the effective sample size.
- **Hyperparameter choice**: Set hyperparameters using prior knowledge (mean and variance) or effective sample size reasoning.
- **Vague scale priors caution**: Setting near-zero hyperparameters ($\epsilon \to 0$) does not represent neutral ignorance; it creates sharp density spikes near zero that can distort inferences in small samples. Prefer weakly informative priors scaled to the domain.

## Summary of Practice Tasks

### Task 1: Multiple-Choice Exam Success (Accuracy vs. Latent Mastery)
* **Context**: $N = 40$ questions, 4 options per question. Random guessing success rate is $0.25$.
* **Cognitive Model Distinction**: In a 4-alternative forced-choice test, observable accuracy $\theta$ reflects a mixture of true knowledge and chance guessing. Under a classical know-or-guess model, if a student has probability $q \in [0, 1]$ of knowing the answer and guesses with chance probability $0.25$ otherwise, the observable accuracy is:
  $$\theta = q + (1 - q) \times 0.25 = 0.25 + 0.75q$$
  Therefore, if true mastery requires knowing at least 60% of the material ($q \ge 0.60$), the required observable test score is $\theta \ge 0.25 + 0.75(0.60) = 0.70$ (70% accuracy). Evaluating $P(\theta > 0.60 \mid D)$ measures the probability that overall observable accuracy exceeds 60% (corresponding to latent knowledge $q > 0.467$).
* **Data**: Student A scores $k_A = 24/40$; Student B scores $k_B = 34/40$.
* **Core Question**: Under an informative prior centered above guessing (e.g. $\operatorname{Beta}(4, 6)$, prior mean $0.40$), what are the updated posterior means and credible intervals for both students? What is the posterior probability that Student A achieves observable accuracy $\theta_A > 0.60$, and what does this imply for latent mastery?

:::{dropdown} 💡 Hint & Key Formulas
* **Prior**: $\theta \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ (Note: standard Beta support covers $[0, 1]$, permitting values below chance unless explicitly truncated).
* **Likelihood**: $k \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N, \theta)$
* **Posterior**: $\theta \mid k \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha + k, \; \beta + N - k)$
* **Posterior Mean**: $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid k] = \frac{\alpha + k}{\alpha + \beta + N}$
:::

### Task 3: Dietary Intake & Public Health Guidelines (Rate vs. Individual Threshold)
* **Context**: Daily servings of fruits and vegetables recorded in the BRFSS dataset (`fruit_per_day` and `vege_per_day`).
* **Estimand Distinction**: $P(\lambda \ge 4.0 \mid D)$ assesses posterior certainty regarding the *population mean consumption rate* $\lambda$ under a homogeneous Poisson model. It does *not* measure the fraction of individual citizens who consume $\ge 4$ portions per day. Even when the population rate is $\lambda = 4.0$, individual daily servings fluctuate according to $Y_{\text{new}} \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$, meaning many individuals eat fewer than 4 servings. Calculating individual guideline compliance requires the posterior predictive probability $P(Y_{\text{new}} \ge 4 \mid D) = \sum_{y=4}^\infty P(Y_{\text{new}} = y \mid D)$. Furthermore, count data often exhibit zero-inflation or overdispersion, so integer counts alone do not guarantee a simple Poisson likelihood.
* **Core Question**: Estimate the posterior distribution for the population mean rate $\lambda$ of fruit consumption, and contrast the mean rate with individual predicted intake.

:::{dropdown} 💡 Hint & Key Formulas
* **Prior**: $\lambda \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ with prior mean $\alpha / \beta$ and effective prior sample size $\beta$.
* **Likelihood**: $Y_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$.
* **Posterior**: $\lambda \mid y_1, \dots, y_n \sim \operatorname{Gamma}\left(\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^n y_i, \; \beta + n\right)$.
* **Posterior Mean**: $\mathbb{E}[\lambda \mid y] = \frac{\alpha + \sum y_i}{\beta + n}$.
:::
