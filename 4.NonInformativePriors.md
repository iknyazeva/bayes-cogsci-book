# Weakly Informative, Flat and Improper Priors

The phrase **non-informative prior** suggests that a probability distribution can represent complete ignorance. In most applied problems this is too strong. Every prior privileges a scale, assigns relative plausibility to parameter regions and combines with a specific likelihood. It is therefore more precise to distinguish weakly informative priors, flat or diffuse priors, formal reference priors and improper priors. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

## 1. Four different ideas

| Term | Meaning in this course | Proper probability distribution? |
|---|---|---:|
| Weakly informative | Contains modest real-world information that keeps estimates in a plausible range | Yes |
| Flat or diffuse | Has nearly constant density over a chosen region or is very broad on a chosen scale | Sometimes |
| Reference prior | Constructed by a formal rule intended to limit prior influence or obtain an invariance property | Sometimes |
| Improper prior | Has infinite total mass and is used only as a limiting calculation | No |

These labels are not ordered from “most Bayesian” to “least Bayesian.” They answer different design questions.

:::{important}
An improper prior is not a probability distribution. It cannot generate prior predictive data, cannot be assigned a literal prior probability interpretation, and must not be used unless the resulting posterior is shown to be proper.
:::

## 2. Why a uniform prior is not assumption-free

For a Binomial probability theta, a common default is

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)=\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).
$$

It is flat on the probability scale. If eta=logit(theta), however, the induced prior on eta is not flat:

$$
p(\eta)
=
p(\theta)\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta)
\left[1-\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta)\right].
$$

Thus “uniform” is incomplete unless the parameterization is named.

The prior predictive implication is also informative. If

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1),
\qquad
y\mid\theta,N\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(N,\theta),
$$

then the Beta-Binomial prior predictive distribution is uniform over the N+1 possible counts:

$$
p(y=k)=\frac{1}{N+1},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,N.
$$

Before observing any data, 0 supporters, N/2 supporters and N supporters receive the same probability as count outcomes. That may be reasonable for a teaching example, but it is not automatically weak in an applied survey.

````{dropdown} Intuition check: flat density versus flat predictions
A flat density over theta does not make individual binary outcomes equally surprising in every sense; it implies a particular mixture over Binomial datasets. Always inspect the implied distribution of the observable quantity.
````

## 3. Smaller Beta parameters are not simply “less information”

The limiting prior

$$
p(\theta)\propto\theta^{-1}(1-\theta)^{-1},
$$

is sometimes written Beta(0,0) and called the **Haldane prior**. It is improper and concentrates its limiting mass near 0 and 1. Beta(epsilon,epsilon) with small positive epsilon is proper but strongly U-shaped.

This prior can reproduce the sample proportion as a posterior mean when 0<k<N:

$$
\theta\mid k,N\sim\operatorname{Beta}(k,N-k),
\qquad
\mathbb{E}(\theta\mid k,N)=\frac{k}{N}.
$$

That algebraic match does not make the prior neutral. If k=0 or k=N, the posterior remains improper. For model comparison, near-Haldane alternatives can also behave pathologically because they assign almost no prior mass to moderate parameter values supported by ordinary data. Kruschke uses this case to show why a mathematically diffuse default need not represent a credible scientific alternative. {cite}`kruschke2015doing`

:::{admonition} Illustrative Scenario: The “Zero Errors” Usability Testing Dilemma
:class: tip
A cognitive engineering team tests a safety-critical cockpit alert system on $N = 5$ pilots during flight simulation. All 5 pilots complete the emergency maneuver without making an error ($k = 0$ errors).

What is the estimated pilot error probability $\theta$?

1. **Maximum Likelihood (Frequentist MLE)**:
   $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{0}{5} = 0.0$ (0.0%).
   *The Pathological Claim*: The model asserts that human error is strictly physically impossible in the broader pilot population.

2. **Haldane Prior $\operatorname{Beta}(0, 0)$**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(0 + 0, 0 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(0, 5)$ (Non-integrable / Improper).
   *The Breakdown*: Fails completely when no events are observed in either direction ($k = 0$ or $k = N$).

3. **Jeffreys Reference Prior $\operatorname{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)$**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(0.5 + 0, 0.5 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(0.5, 5.5) \implies \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{0.5}{6.0} \approx 0.083$ (8.3%).

4. **Uniform Prior $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$ (Laplace's Rule of Succession)**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(1 + 0, 1 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(1, 6) \implies \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{1}{7.0} \approx 0.143$ (14.3%).

5. **Weakly Informative Baseline $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 9)$ ($\mu_0 = 0.10$ historical error rate, $n_0 = 10$)**:
   $\text{Posterior} = \operatorname{Beta}(1 + 0, 9 + 5) = \operatorname{Beta}(1, 14) \implies \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{1}{15.0} \approx 0.067$ (6.7%).

*Key Takeaway*: In small samples, weakly informative priors and reference rules protect empirical researchers from asserting absolute certainty (0% or 100%) based solely on small-sample stochastic absences.
:::

## 4. Improper reference calculations for Normal data

### Known variance, unknown mean

If


$$
y_i\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2)
$$

with known sigma, the flat prior

$$
p(\mu)\propto 1
$$

gives the proper posterior

$$
\mu\mid y
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}\left(\bar y,\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right).
$$

This is an exact case in which the posterior mean equals the maximum-likelihood estimate and the equal-tailed credible interval has the same endpoints as the familiar z confidence interval. Their interpretations remain different.

### Unknown mean and variance

A widely used reference specification is

$$
p(\mu,\sigma^2)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^2}.
$$

It yields

$$
\frac{\mu-\bar y}{s/\sqrt{N}}
\mid y
\sim t_{N-1},
$$

so the usual posterior interval for mu has the same numerical endpoints as the classical t interval. Jackman presents this as a direct example of a Bayesian calculation reproducing a standard frequentist result. {cite}`jackman2009bayesian`

:::{warning}
Do not call p(mu,sigma^2) proportional to 1/sigma^2 “the joint Jeffreys prior” without qualification. In a multiparameter Normal model, the full determinant-based Jeffreys rule and commonly used independent/reference constructions are not identical. This distinction is developed in {doc}`4.JeffreysPrior`.
:::

## 5. Weakly informative priors

A weakly informative prior is proper and deliberately broader than the analyst's best substantive knowledge while still excluding unreasonable values. Gelman and colleagues recommend this strategy in many applied problems because it provides stability without pretending to encode complete knowledge. {cite}`mcelreath2020statistical` {cite}`gelman2021regression`

### The scale must be stated

The statement

$$
\beta\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)
$$

cannot be judged until beta is interpreted. It can be weak for a standardized mean difference, strong for an annual change measured in percentage points, or extremely broad for a probability expressed on the raw scale.

| Parameter | Possible weakly informative prior | Necessary qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Standardized continuous effect | Normal(0, 1) | Outcome and predictor scaling must be documented |
| Logistic coefficient | Normal(0, 1.5) | Interpret implied changes in probability for realistic predictor contrasts |
| Standard deviation | Half-Normal(0, s) | Choose s on the observed outcome scale |
| Common population proportion | Beta(2, 2) or a context-specific Beta prior | Inspect prior predictive counts for the planned N |

These are templates, not universal defaults.

### Two construction routes

1. Begin with a reference or very broad prior and add enough information to prevent implausible predictions.
2. Begin with the strongest plausible substantive prior and widen it to acknowledge uncertainty and limited transportability.

Both routes should end with a prior predictive audit.

:::{admonition} Sociological demonstration: rare event prevalence
:class: tip
Suppose a severe administrative event is known to occur in fewer than 1 in 1,000 cases. A Uniform(0,1) prior is not weak in the relevant scientific sense: after observing 0 events in 100 cases, it gives Beta(1,101), whose mean is about 0.98%. That is almost ten times the upper prevalence believed plausible before the study.

A contextually weak prior should operate on the rare-event scale and should be checked by simulating event counts for the planned sample size.
:::

## 6. Nonregular Models and Boundary Estimation: The German Tank Problem

A fundamental challenge to the notion that "flat priors are always safe" arises in **nonregular models**, where the support of the observed data depends on the unknown parameter itself.

A classic example is estimating the total population size $N \in \{1, 2, \dots\}$ from a sample of $k$ serial numbers $x = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k\}$ drawn uniformly without replacement.

### The Likelihood

Let $M = \max(x_1, \dots, x_k)$ be the sample maximum. The probability of observing this specific set of $k$ distinct numbers is:

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_k \mid N) = \frac{1}{\binom{N}{k}} = \frac{k!(N-k)!}{N!} \qquad \text{for } N \ge M,
$$

and $0$ for $N < M$.

Because the likelihood is strictly decreasing for $N \ge M$, the Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) is $\widehat{N}_{\text{MLE}} = M$. However, the sample maximum $M$ is guaranteed to underestimate the true population bound $N$ whenever $k < N$.

### Frequentist Unbiased Estimator (MVUE)

The minimum-variance unbiased estimator corrects for the downward bias of the maximum:

$$
\widehat{N}_{\text{MVUE}} = M + \frac{M}{k} - 1 = M\left(1 + \frac{1}{k}\right) - 1.
$$

### Bayesian Posterior Analysis

1. **Under an Improper Flat Prior $p(N) \propto 1$ ($N \ge 1$):**

$$
p(N \mid x) = \frac{\frac{(N-k)!}{N!}}{\sum_{N'=M}^\infty \frac{(N'-k)!}{N'!}} = \frac{k-1}{k} \frac{\binom{M-1}{k-1}}{\binom{N}{k}} = (k-1) \frac{(M-1)!}{(M-k)!} \frac{(N-k)!}{N!} \qquad (N \ge M).
$$

For $k \ge 2$, the posterior sum is finite and proper. The posterior expectation is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[N \mid x] = (M - 1)\frac{k - 1}{k - 2} \qquad (\text{defined for } k > 2).
$$

*(Note: For $k = 1$, the flat prior produces an improper posterior; for $k = 2$, the posterior is proper but has an infinite expectation).*

2. **Under an Invariant Scale Prior $p(N) \propto 1/N$ (Jeffreys-like discrete prior):**

$$
p(N \mid x) \propto \frac{(N-k)!}{N \cdot N!} \qquad (N \ge M).
$$

For $k \ge 2$, the posterior expectation is finite. For the worked example ($M = 60, k = 4$), the exact numeric expectation is **$78.9$ tanks** (with large-$M$ asymptotic approximation $\mathbb{E}[N \mid x] \approx M \frac{k}{k - 1} = 80.0$).

*Core insights from this nonregular problem:*
- **Parameter-dependent support**: The hard lower boundary at $N = M$ violates standard Cramér–Rao regularity, causing standard asymptotic normality to fail.
- **Sensitivity of improper priors**: A flat prior on $N$ requires $k \ge 3$ observations just to obtain a finite posterior mean, whereas the scale prior $1/N$ provides sensible finite regularisation with only $k \ge 2$ observations.

## 7. Demonstration protocol

For every candidate prior:

1. state the parameter and scale;
2. graph the prior density;
3. generate at least 1,000 prior predictive datasets;
4. calculate the frequency of impossible or substantively implausible outcomes;
5. compare at least two defensible priors after fitting the same likelihood;
6. report whether the scientific conclusion changes.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n_sim = 2_000
n_respondents = 100

# Replace these values with each candidate Beta prior.
alpha, beta = 2.0, 2.0
theta_sim = rng.beta(alpha, beta, size=n_sim)
y_sim = rng.binomial(n_respondents, theta_sim)

print(np.quantile(y_sim, [0.025, 0.5, 0.975]))
```


## 8. When flat-prior Bayesian results resemble frequentist results

Numerical agreement arises in some exact conjugate models and, under regularity conditions, approximately in large samples. It does not arise because the Bayesian analysis has ceased to be Bayesian. The two frameworks continue to condition on different objects and assign different meanings to intervals and probabilities.

The full treatment is in {doc}`4.BayesianFrequentistConnections`.

## Key takeaways

- “Uninformative” is a goal or historical label, not a property that can be established by visual flatness.
- Uniform priors depend on parameterization.
- Small Beta shape parameters can increase commitment to boundary values rather than remove information.
- Improper priors require a proof or reliable check that the posterior is proper.
- Weakly informative priors should be calibrated on the outcome scale and checked through prior predictive simulation.
- Similar Bayesian and frequentist numerical answers do not have identical interpretations.
