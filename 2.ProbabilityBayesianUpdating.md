# Session 2: Probability and Bayesian Updating

## 1. Probability as Quantified Plausibility

In Bayesian data analysis, probability is not merely a long-run limiting frequency of infinite hypothetical repetitions; it is a mathematical language for quantifying and updating **epistemic uncertainty** given incomplete evidence.

Whether we are modeling the probability that a patient has a cognitive impairment, that an economic policy will reduce poverty, or that a coin is biased, probability represents our rational degree of belief conditional on our current state of knowledge.

## 2. The Core Rules of Probability

```{image} CatsDice.jpeg
:alt: Cats and Dice Probability
:width: 280px
:align: center
```

All of Bayesian probability is built from two foundational rules obeying the Kolmogorov axioms and Cox's consistency theorem:

### 1. The Product Rule (Joint Probability)
The probability that both proposition $A$ and proposition $B$ occur together is:

$$\mathbb{P}(A, B) = \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)\mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A)$$

### 2. The Sum Rule (Marginal Probability)
If a space is partitioned into mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities $\{B_1, B_2, \dots, B_K\}$, the total (marginal) probability of $A$ is:

$$\mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb{P}(A, B_k) = \sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb{P}(A \mid B_k)\mathbb{P}(B_k)$$

## 3. Deriving Bayes' Theorem: Three Essential Forms

From the symmetry of the product rule:

$$\mathbb{P}(H \mid D)\mathbb{P}(D) = \mathbb{P}(D \mid H)\mathbb{P}(H)$$

Dividing both sides by $\mathbb{P}(D)$ yields **Bayes' Theorem**:

$$\mathbb{P}(H \mid D) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(D \mid H)\mathbb{P}(H)}{\mathbb{P}(D)}$$

### Three Working Representations of Bayes' Rule

| Representation | Mathematical Formulation | Best Practical Use |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Standard Formula** | $\mathbb{P}(H \mid D) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(D \mid H)\mathbb{P}(H)}{\sum_k \mathbb{P}(D \mid H_k)\mathbb{P}(H_k)}$ | Formal analytical derivations and continuous models. |
| **Natural Frequencies (2x2)** | $\mathbb{P}(H \mid +) = \frac{\text{True Positives}}{\text{True Positives} + \text{False Positives}}$ | Clinical screening, fraud detection, and communicating risks to humans. |
| **Odds Form** | $\text{Posterior Odds} = \text{Prior Odds} \times \text{Bayes Factor}$ | Comparing two discrete hypotheses without computing marginal evidence $\mathbb{P}(D)$. |

## 4. The False Positive Paradox and Base-Rate Neglect

Consider a diagnostic test for a rare cognitive condition:
* **Prevalence / Base Rate**: $\mathbb{P}(C) = 0.01$ ($1\%$ of population).
* **Sensitivity (True Positive Rate)**: $\mathbb{P}(+ \mid C) = 0.95$.
* **Specificity (True Negative Rate)**: $\mathbb{P}(- \mid \neg C) = 0.90 \implies \mathbb{P}(+ \mid \neg C) = 0.10$ (False Positive Rate).

### Natural Frequency Table ($N = 10,000$ individuals):

| Condition Status | Positive Test ($+$) | Negative Test ($-$) | Group Subtotal |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **Has Condition ($C$)** | $100 \times 0.95 = \mathbf{95}$ | $100 \times 0.05 = 5$ | **100** |
| **Healthy ($\neg C$)** | $9,900 \times 0.10 = \mathbf{990}$ | $9,900 \times 0.90 = 8,910$ | **9,900** |
| **Total Tested** | $\mathbf{1,085}$ | **8,915** | **10,000** |

$$\mathbb{P}(C \mid +) = \frac{95}{95 + 990} = \frac{95}{1,085} \approx \mathbf{8.76\%}$$

:::{important}
**The Inverse Probability Fallacy**:  
Conflating test sensitivity $\mathbb{P}(+ \mid C) = 95\%$ with the posterior probability $\mathbb{P}(C \mid +) = 8.76\%$ is one of the most common errors in empirical science. When a condition is rare, false positives from the healthy majority easily overwhelm true positives from the small diseased group!
:::

## 5. Sequential Updating and Data Order Invariance

Bayesian learning is naturally cumulative: yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior. Suppose we observe two independent pieces of evidence $D_1$ and $D_2$:

1. **Step 1**: Use prior $p(\theta)$ to compute first posterior:
   $$p(\theta \mid D_1) = \frac{p(D_1 \mid \theta)p(\theta)}{p(D_1)}$$
2. **Step 2**: Use $p(\theta \mid D_1)$ as the **new prior** for updating on $D_2$:
   $$p(\theta \mid D_1, D_2) = \frac{p(D_2 \mid \theta)p(\theta \mid D_1)}{p(D_2 \mid D_1)}$$

Substituting the expression for $p(\theta \mid D_1)$:

$$p(\theta \mid D_1, D_2) = \frac{p(D_2 \mid \theta) \frac{p(D_1 \mid \theta)p(\theta)}{p(D_1)}}{\frac{p(D_1, D_2)}{p(D_1)}} = \frac{p(D_1, D_2 \mid \theta)p(\theta)}{p(D_1, D_2)}$$

:::{note}
**Order Invariance Principle**:  
Updating your beliefs sequentially ($D_1$ then $D_2$) produces the exact same posterior distribution as updating on all batch data at once, or receiving the evidence in reverse order ($D_2$ then $D_1$), provided data are conditionally independent given $\theta$.
:::

## 6. Likelihood Function vs. Probability Density

A critical conceptual distinction in statistical modeling:

* **Probability Density $p(D \mid \theta)$**: A function of **data $D$** for a **fixed parameter value $\theta$**. Its integral over data space equals 1: $\int_D p(D \mid \theta) dD = 1$.
* **Likelihood $\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid D) \equiv p(D \mid \theta)$**: A function of the **parameter $\theta$** for a **fixed, recorded dataset $D$**.
* **Crucial Property**: The likelihood is **not** a probability distribution over $\theta$. Its integral $\int_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta \mid D) d\theta \neq 1$. It only becomes a valid probability distribution after multiplication by a prior $p(\theta)$ and normalization.

---

## 7. Odds Form of Bayes' Rule and Bayes Factors

When comparing two discrete hypotheses $H_1$ and $H_2$:

$$\underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{P}(H_1 \mid D)}{\mathbb{P}(H_2 \mid D)}}_{\text{Posterior Odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{P}(H_1)}{\mathbb{P}(H_2)}}_{\text{Prior Odds}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{P}(D \mid H_1)}{\mathbb{P}(D \mid H_2)}}_{\text{Bayes Factor (Likelihood Ratio)}}$$

* **Key Advantage**: The denominator $\mathbb{P}(D)$ cancels out entirely.
* If prior odds are $1:1$ (equal plausibility), the posterior odds are determined solely by the Bayes Factor $\text{BF}_{12}$.

````{dropdown} 📝 Exercise 1: Base Rates and Rare Disease Screening (Click to expand solution)
**Question**: A diagnostic test for a rare disease has sensitivity $\mathbb{P}(+ \mid C) = 0.99$ and false positive rate $\mathbb{P}(+ \mid \neg C) = 0.05$.
1. Compute the posterior probability $\mathbb{P}(C \mid +)$ if the disease has a base rate prevalence of $\mathbb{P}(C) = 0.001$ ($0.1\%$, e.g. general population screening).
2. Compute the posterior probability $\mathbb{P}(C \mid +)$ if the patient attends a high-risk specialist clinic where prevalence is $\mathbb{P}(C) = 0.10$ ($10\%$).

**Solution & Step-by-Step Calculation**:

1. **General Population (Prevalence = 0.1%)**:

   $$\mathbb{P}(C \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.05 \times 0.999} = \frac{0.00099}{0.00099 + 0.04995} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx \mathbf{1.94}\%$$

2. **High-Risk Clinic (Prevalence = 10%)**:

   $$\mathbb{P}(C \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.10}{0.99 \times 0.10 + 0.05 \times 0.90} = \frac{0.099}{0.099 + 0.045} = \frac{0.099}{0.144} \approx \mathbf{68.75}\%$$
````

````{dropdown} 📝 Exercise 3: Bayesian Updating with Different Beta Priors (Click to expand interactive explorer)
**Question**: You observe a sample of $N = 5$ coin tosses resulting in $k = 3$ Heads and $2$ Tails. Compare how this identical evidence updates 4 different prior beliefs about coin bias $\theta$:
1. **Uniform Prior**: $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1)$ (Open-minded / flat)
2. **Strong Fair-Coin Prior**: $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(10, 10)$ (Skeptical of bias)
3. **Strong Heads-Biased Prior**: $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(8, 2)$ (Belief that coin favors heads)
4. **Strong Tails-Biased Prior**: $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(2, 8)$ (Belief that coin favors tails)

**Solution & Mathematical Comparison**:

Under Beta-Binomial conjugacy ($\theta \mid k \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + N - k)$ with $k=3, N-k=2$):

1. **Uniform Prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$** (Prior Mean $= 0.500$):

   $$\text{Posterior} = \text{Beta}(1+3, 1+2) = \mathbf{\text{Beta}(4, 3)}, \quad \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D] = \frac{4}{4+3} \approx \mathbf{0.571}$$

   *Takeaway*: With an uninformative prior, the posterior is completely shaped by the observed data ($60\%$ heads).

2. **Fair-Coin Prior $\text{Beta}(10, 10)$** (Prior Mean $= 0.500$, Equivalent sample weight $= 20$ flips):

   $$\text{Posterior} = \text{Beta}(10+3, 10+2) = \mathbf{\text{Beta}(13, 12)}, \quad \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D] = \frac{13}{13+12} = \mathbf{0.520}$$

   *Takeaway*: The strong prior resists 5 flips, shifting only slightly from $0.500$ to $0.520$.

3. **Heads-Biased Prior $\text{Beta}(8, 2)$** (Prior Mean $= 0.800$):

   $$\text{Posterior} = \text{Beta}(8+3, 2+2) = \mathbf{\text{Beta}(11, 4)}, \quad \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D] = \frac{11}{11+4} \approx \mathbf{0.733}$$

   *Takeaway*: Because the data ($3/5 = 0.60$) has a lower proportion of heads than the prior ($0.80$), the posterior is pulled downward toward $0.733$.

4. **Tails-Biased Prior $\text{Beta}(2, 8)$** (Prior Mean $= 0.200$):

   $$\text{Posterior} = \text{Beta}(2+3, 8+2) = \mathbf{\text{Beta}(5, 10)}, \quad \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D] = \frac{5}{5+10} \approx \mathbf{0.333}$$

   *Takeaway*: The data ($60\%$ heads) pulls the skeptical tails-prior upward toward $0.333$.
````

## 9. Concept Check

````{dropdown} ❓ Is likelihood a probability distribution over parameters?
**No.** The likelihood function $\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid D)$ is evaluated across parameter values $\theta$ for a fixed observed dataset $D$. It does not integrate to 1 and cannot be interpreted as a probability distribution until multiplied by a prior $p(\theta)$ and normalized.
````

````{dropdown} ❓ Does changing the order of evidence affect the Bayesian posterior?
**No.** Provided observations are conditionally independent (exchangeable) given parameter $\theta$, Bayesian updating is strictly commutative and order-invariant.
````

````{dropdown} ❓ What is the Prosecutor's Fallacy?
It is the cognitive error of equating $\mathbb{P}(\text{Evidence} \mid \text{Innocent})$ with $\mathbb{P}(\text{Innocent} \mid \text{Evidence})$. A DNA match rarity of 1 in 100,000 does not mean there is a 99.999% probability of guilt if drawn from a large population without independent corroborating evidence.
````
