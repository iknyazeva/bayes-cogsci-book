In [Lesson 1](1.ResearchQuestionsDataMeasurement.md), we specified what we want to learn and what the data represent. Now we ask how observations change uncertainty about that quantity. The central idea is **prior information × compatibility with the new data → updated uncertainty**. Bayes' rule tells us how to combine those ingredients mathematically.

## 1. The Rules of Probability

```{image} CatsDice.jpeg
:alt: Cats and Dice Probability
:width: 280px
:align: center
```

Before we can update beliefs with data, we need a mathematical language for uncertainty. The foundation of probability relies on two core rules.

### The Product Rule (Joint Probability)
The probability that both proposition $A$ and proposition $B$ occur together (their joint probability) is:

$$\mathbb{P}(A, B) = \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)\mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A)$$

Here, $\mathbb{P}(A \mid B)$ is the **conditional probability** of $A$ given that $B$ is true. 

### The Sum Rule (Marginal Probability)
If a space is partitioned into mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities $\{B_1, B_2, \dots, B_K\}$, the total (marginal) probability of $A$ is the sum of its joint probabilities across all possible states of $B$:

$$\mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb{P}(A, B_k) = \sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb{P}(A \mid B_k)\mathbb{P}(B_k)$$

## 2. Bayes' Theorem and The False Positive Paradox

If we take the symmetry of the product rule:
$$\mathbb{P}(H \mid D)\mathbb{P}(D) = \mathbb{P}(D \mid H)\mathbb{P}(H)$$

And divide both sides by $\mathbb{P}(D)>0$, we get **Bayes' Theorem**:

$$\mathbb{P}(H \mid D) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(D \mid H)\mathbb{P}(H)}{\mathbb{P}(D)}$$

Where:
* **Prior $\mathbb{P}(H)$**: Our belief in the hypothesis before seeing the data.
* **Likelihood $\mathbb{P}(D \mid H)$**: How probable the observed data is, assuming the hypothesis is true.
* **Marginal Likelihood $\mathbb{P}(D)$**: The total probability of observing the data across all hypotheses (calculated via the Sum Rule).
* **Posterior $\mathbb{P}(H \mid D)$**: Our updated belief in the hypothesis after seeing the data.

### The False Positive Paradox
Consider an automated quality check that flags corrupted recordings. These are stipulated rates for a teaching example. 
* **Prior (Base Rate)**: 1% of recordings are corrupted. $\mathbb{P}(C) = 0.01$
* **Likelihood (Sensitivity)**: The system correctly flags 95% of corrupted recordings. $\mathbb{P}(F \mid C) = 0.95$
* **Likelihood (False Positives)**: The system incorrectly flags 10% of usable recordings. $\mathbb{P}(F \mid \neg C) = 0.10$

If a recording is flagged, what is the probability it is actually corrupted? $\mathbb{P}(C \mid F)$

Using Bayes Theorem:
$$\mathbb{P}(C \mid F) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(F \mid C)\mathbb{P}(C)}{\mathbb{P}(F \mid C)\mathbb{P}(C) + \mathbb{P}(F \mid \neg C)\mathbb{P}(\neg C)}$$
$$\mathbb{P}(C \mid F) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{(0.95 \times 0.01) + (0.10 \times 0.99)} = \frac{0.0095}{0.0095 + 0.0990} \approx 8.76\%$$

The same calculation is easier to inspect as expected counts among **10,000 recordings**:

| Recording status | Flagged | Not flagged | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Corrupted | 95 | 5 | 100 |
| Usable | 990 | 8,910 | 9,900 |
| Total | 1,085 | 8,915 | 10,000 |

Among the 1,085 flagged recordings, only 95 are corrupted: $95/1{,}085 \approx 8.76\%$. Before calculating, identify the group after “given that”: this group supplies the denominator.

**The Inverse Probability Fallacy**: Conflating the likelihood $\mathbb{P}(F \mid C) = 95\%$ with the posterior $\mathbb{P}(C \mid F) = 8.76\%$ is one of the most common errors in empirical science (often illustrated with rare disease or HIV screening). When a condition is rare (a low prior base rate), false positives from the many usable recordings easily overwhelm true positives.

## 3. The Monty Hall Paradox

The Monty Hall problem is a famous test of Bayesian reasoning.
1. You are on a game show. There are 3 doors. Behind one is a car; behind the other two are goats.
2. You pick a door (say, Door 1).
3. The host, who knows what is behind the doors, opens another door (say, Door 2) to reveal a goat.
4. He asks: "Do you want to switch to Door 3?"

**Host's rule:** The host knows where the car is, always opens an unchosen goat door, and always offers a switch. If both unchosen doors contain goats, the host chooses between them with equal probability. This rule determines the likelihoods below.

**Pause for discussion:** Predict your chance of winning by staying and by switching before inspecting the plot. Explain what the host's action tells you.

Should you switch? Bayes' Theorem gives us the exact answer by forcing us to define the likelihood of the *host's behavior*.

* **Prior**: Before any doors are opened, the car is equally likely to be behind any door. $\mathbb{P}(D_1) = \mathbb{P}(D_2) = \mathbb{P}(D_3) = 1/3$.
* **Likelihood**: You picked Door 1. What is the likelihood the host opens Door 2?
  - If the car is behind Door 1, the host can open 2 or 3 randomly. $\mathbb{P}(\text{Host 2} \mid \text{Car 1}) = 1/2$.
  - If the car is behind Door 2, the host *cannot* open Door 2. $\mathbb{P}(\text{Host 2} \mid \text{Car 2}) = 0$.
  - If the car is behind Door 3, the host *must* open Door 2 (since you picked 1). $\mathbb{P}(\text{Host 2} \mid \text{Car 3}) = 1$.

For the initial choice of Door 1 and the observed opening of Door 2:

| Hypothesis | Prior | Likelihood of opening Door 2 | Prior × likelihood | Posterior |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Car at Door 1 | 1/3 | 1/2 | 1/6 | 1/3 |
| Car at Door 2 | 1/3 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Car at Door 3 | 1/3 | 1 | 1/3 | 2/3 |

The marginal probability of this host action is $1/6 + 0 + 1/3 = 1/2$. Divide each entry in the fourth column by $1/2$ to obtain the posterior.

By switching to Door 3, you double your chances of winning!

**Discussion extension:** What changes if the host does not know where the car is and opens an unchosen door at random, and that door happens to contain a goat?

:::{dropdown} Compare the host policies
For that uninformed host, the likelihood of opening Door 2 **and revealing a goat** is $1/2$ if the car is at Door 1, $0$ if it is at Door 2, and $1/2$ if it is at Door 3. With equal priors, the two remaining doors each have posterior probability $1/2$. The observed goat is the same; the process that produced the observation is different. Always state the observation rule before assigning a likelihood.
:::

## 4. Likelihood vs. Probability & Continuous Updating

We've looked at discrete hypotheses (Corrupted/Usable, Door 1/2/3). The same principles apply to continuous parameters, like an unknown success probability $\theta$. 

### Likelihood is NOT a Probability Distribution
The likelihood function $\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid D) = \mathbb{P}(D \mid \theta)$ evaluates the compatibility of different candidate parameter values $\theta$ for a *fixed* observed dataset $D$. 

It is an index of relative compatibility. It is **not** required to be normalized over parameters (its integral does not have to equal 1), which is why we must multiply it by a prior and normalize it to get a valid posterior probability distribution $p(\theta \mid D)$.

*Figure 2.4: The Duality of Likelihood and Probability. Panel (a) shows Binomial probability mass across possible counts for a fixed parameter. Panel (b) shows the likelihood function for a fixed observation, which is not required to integrate to 1 over the parameter.*

### Sequential Updating
Bayesian learning is naturally cumulative: yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior. 

If we use a **Beta distribution** for our prior over $\theta$ and observe binary (Bernoulli) successes and failures, the update is conjugate: the posterior remains in the Beta family. Here we assume observations are conditionally independent given a common success probability $\theta$. 

$$ \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \text{ prior} + k \text{ successes and } (N-k) \text{ failures} \longrightarrow \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+k, \beta+N-k) \text{ posterior} $$

*Figure 2.5: Step-by-Step Bernoulli Updating Triad. Click the buttons to follow sequential coin flips ($y_1 = 1, y_2 = 0, y_3 = 1, y_4 = 1, y_5 = 0$). Watch how each step's posterior becomes the prior for the next observation.*

<iframe src="../_static/fig2_3_sequential_bernoulli_triad.html" width="100%" height="360px" style="border:none; border-radius:8px;"></iframe>

Under this same model, updating sequentially produces the exact same posterior as updating on all observations at once. Each update must use new evidence: reusing an observation as if it were new would count it twice.

### Exercise 2: Predict an update
Starting from a $\operatorname{Beta}(2,2)$ prior, you observe 3 correct responses in 5 independent comparable observations. State the posterior and its mean.

:::{dropdown} Answer
The posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(2+3, 2+2) = \operatorname{Beta}(5,4)$, with mean $5/9\approx0.556$. The prior centered at 0.5 pulls the posterior mean below the observed 0.6 proportion.
:::

### Exercise 3: Bayesian Updating with Different Beta Priors
You observe a sample of $N = 5$ coin tosses resulting in $k = 3$ Heads and $2$ Tails. Compare how this identical evidence updates 4 different prior beliefs about coin bias $\theta$.

:::{dropdown} Answer
Bayesian updating acts as a **weighted compromise** between the prior mean and the sample evidence. 
1. **Uniform $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$**: Posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(4,3)$. The mean (0.571) is pulled slightly toward 0.5 from the sample proportion (0.6).
2. **Fair-Coin $\operatorname{Beta}(10,10)$**: Posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(13,12)$. The strong prior resists the 5 flips, shifting the mean only to 0.520.
3. **Heads-Biased $\operatorname{Beta}(8,2)$**: Posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(11,4)$. The lower data proportion (0.6) pulls the prior mean (0.8) down to 0.733.
4. **Tails-Biased $\operatorname{Beta}(2,8)$**: Posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(5,10)$. The higher data proportion (0.6) pulls the prior mean (0.2) up to 0.333.
