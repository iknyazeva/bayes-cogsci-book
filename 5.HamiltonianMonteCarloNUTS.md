# Going Further: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS

*[Русская версия](/ru/hamiltonianmontecarlonuts)*

[Lesson 5](5.MonteCarloToMCMC.md), section 14, introduced the "skateboarder": a sampler that gets a random push and then rolls along the slope of the posterior instead of taking small random steps. This page writes that picture in formulas. It explains why `pm.sample()` in PyMC runs the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) by default and what its warnings mean. How to check a finished run is covered in [Lesson 6](6.RunningSamplerTrustChecks.md) and in [Convergence diagnostics and remedies](6.AssessingConvergence.md).

## 1. Why random walks slow down

Random-walk Metropolis proposes a step in a round ball around the current point ([Metropolis–Hastings](6.MetropolisHastings.md)). Two features of real posteriors make that inefficient:

- **correlated parameters:** when an intercept and a slope, or a group mean and a group scale, move together, the posterior is a long narrow ridge. A round step that is large enough to travel along the ridge usually falls off it and is rejected; a step that is small enough to be accepted moves only slowly along it;
- **many parameters:** with every added dimension a random step has more directions in which it can leave the region of high posterior mass, so the step size has to shrink and the number of iterations needed grows quickly.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) addresses both problems by using the **gradient** of the log posterior, which tells the sampler in which direction the density changes {cite}`neal2011mcmc`.

## 2. Where the probability mass is: the typical set

In many dimensions, probability mass is the product of density and volume:

$$
d\Pr = p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta .
$$

The density is highest at the posterior mode, but the volume of parameter space at a given distance from the mode grows rapidly with the dimension $D$. Almost all probability mass therefore lies in an intermediate shell called the **typical set**, not at the mode itself.

Mode-finding (optimisation, the MAP estimate) locates the point of highest density, which in high dimensions is surrounded by very little volume. A sampler must instead move around the typical set, where density and volume balance. This is also why the [Laplace approximation and variational inference](6.VariationalInference.md), which start from the mode or an optimised simple shape, can misrepresent a high-dimensional posterior.

## 3. Hamiltonian dynamics

HMC adds an auxiliary **momentum** variable $r$ of the same dimension as $\theta$ and treats the negative log posterior as a landscape:

$$
U(\theta)=-\log p(y,\theta),
\qquad
K(r)=\tfrac12\,r^\top M^{-1}r,
\qquad
H(\theta,r)=U(\theta)+K(r).
$$

$U$ is the **potential energy** (high where the posterior is low), $K$ is the **kinetic energy** and $M$ is the **mass matrix** (the metric). The unknown normalising constant $p(y)$ only shifts $U$ by a constant, so it never has to be computed. The target must still be **proper**: a posterior that does not integrate to a finite number has no stationary distribution to sample.

One HMC iteration:

1. **Push:** draw a fresh momentum $r\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,M)$.
2. **Roll:** simulate the motion for $L$ small steps of size $\epsilon$ with the **leapfrog** integrator:

   $$
   r \leftarrow r-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(\theta),
   \qquad
   \theta \leftarrow \theta+\epsilon M^{-1}r,
   \qquad
   r \leftarrow r-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(\theta).
   $$

3. **Correct:** the exact motion would keep $H$ constant, but the finite step size introduces a small error. A Metropolis correction accepts the end point $(\theta^*,r^*)$ with probability

   $$
   \min\left\{1,\exp\left[H(\theta,r)-H(\theta^*,r^*)\right]\right\}.
   $$

Because the trajectory follows the gradient, one iteration can travel a long way along the typical set while keeping a high acceptance probability.

## 4. NUTS: how far to roll

Plain HMC needs a trajectory length $L$. If $L$ is too short, HMC behaves like a random walk again; if it is too long, the trajectory turns around and wastes computation returning to where it started.

The **No-U-Turn Sampler** {cite}`hoffman2014nuts` chooses the length automatically at every iteration:

- it extends the trajectory forwards and backwards in time, doubling its length each time (a binary tree of leapfrog steps);
- it stops as soon as the trajectory starts to turn back towards its starting point (the U-turn criterion);
- it then selects the next state from the points of the trajectory with appropriate weights.

During **warm-up** (`tune` in PyMC), NUTS also adapts two settings:

- the step size $\epsilon$, so that the average acceptance probability reaches `target_accept` (0.8 by default in PyMC);
- the mass matrix $M$, so that the momentum matches the scale of each parameter.

After warm-up these two settings are **frozen**; only the trajectory length keeps changing from iteration to iteration. Warm-up draws are discarded and never used for inference.

## 5. What HMC needs, and what its warnings mean

**Requirements.** HMC needs the gradient $\nabla\log p(y,\theta)$, so it works on continuous parameters with a differentiable log density. PyMC computes the gradient automatically and first transforms bounded parameters to an unbounded scale (for example, a probability to the log-odds scale). Discrete unknowns cannot be updated by HMC; they are summed out of the model or updated by another step method, such as Metropolis or Gibbs steps, inside a compound sampler.

**Divergences.** A divergent transition happens when the leapfrog simulation becomes unstable and the energy error explodes. This usually means that the posterior curvature changes faster than the current step size can follow, as in the "funnel" of a hierarchical model. A divergence is a **warning about the geometry**: the sampler may be missing part of the posterior. A larger `target_accept` gives a smaller step size and sometimes helps; reparameterising the model (for example, a non-centred hierarchical model) often addresses the cause.

**Maximum tree depth.** If NUTS repeatedly hits its maximum tree depth, trajectories are being cut short. This is an efficiency warning: the posterior may be badly scaled or very correlated.

**Energy (E-BFMI).** A low E-BFMI means that the momentum refresh changes the energy too little for the sampler to move between energy levels. It often accompanies heavy tails or funnels.

## 6. Practice

````{dropdown} Question 1. Why not start from the mode?
A colleague suggests starting HMC at the MAP estimate "because that is where the posterior is". Why can that be misleading in a model with many parameters?

**Answer.** In high dimensions almost all posterior mass lies in the typical set, away from the mode. Starting at the mode is not wrong (warm-up moves the sampler into the typical set), but summaries computed near the mode do not represent the posterior.
````

````{dropdown} Question 2. A larger target_accept
A model reports a few divergences. Raising `target_accept` from 0.8 to 0.95 removes them, but sampling becomes much slower. Why slower, and what should you still examine?

**Answer.** A larger `target_accept` makes warm-up choose a smaller step size, so each trajectory needs more leapfrog steps. The divergences pointed to difficult geometry; check whether a reparameterisation, such as a non-centred hierarchical form, removes the cause instead of only shrinking the step.
````
