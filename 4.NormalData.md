# Bayesian Inference with the Normal Distribution


## Normal Distribution with Known Variance, Unknown Mean
In this example, we'll assume that the **variance** (or standard deviation) is known, and we're only interested in learning about the **mean** of the normal distribution. This scenario frequently occurs in industrial monitoring, where processes are tracked for consistency.

### Normal Distribution Likelihood
If the data $Y$ is normally distributed with a known variance $\sigma^2$ and an unknown mean $\mu$, the likelihood function is:

$$
Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

The likelihood of observing $Y$ given $\mu$ is:

$$
L(Y \mid \mu ) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{(Y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

### Conjugate Prior for the Normal Distribution
When dealing with the **normal likelihood** and unknown mean, a **normal prior** is conjugate. That is, if the prior distribution for $\mu$ is normally distributed, the posterior will also be normally distributed.

Suppose we specify a normal prior for $\mu$ :

$$
\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)
$$

where $\mu_0$ is the prior mean and $\tau_0^2$ is the prior variance.

#### Posterior Distribution
When we observe data $Y$, we can update our belief about $\mu$ by combining the prior and likelihood. The posterior distribution for $\mu$ will also be normally distributed with updated parameters:

```{important}
**Normal-Normal posterior (known variance).** With a $\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$ prior on the mean $\mu$ and $n$ observations from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with known $\sigma^2$:

- **Posterior mean** (weighted average of prior and data):

$$
\mu_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{1/\tau_0^2}{1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2} \mu_0 + \frac{n/\sigma^2}{1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2} \bar{Y}
$$

- **Posterior variance** (shrinks as more data is observed):

$$
\tau_{\text{posterior}}^2 = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}}
$$

The posterior precision (inverse variance) is the sum of the prior precision and data precision — information accumulates additively.
```

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example: Estimating Average IQ in a Patient Group
:class: tip
A neuropsychologist wants to estimate the average IQ ($\mu$) of patients with a specific lesion type. IQ tests have a known standard deviation of $\sigma = 15$ points. Based on prior literature, she expects these patients to score around $\mu_0 = 95$ with prior uncertainty $\tau_0 = 10$.

After testing $n = 8$ patients with sample mean $\bar{Y} = 88$, the posterior is:

$$
\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{1/100}{1/100 + 8/225} \cdot 95 + \frac{8/225}{1/100 + 8/225} \cdot 88 \approx 89.5
$$

The data (mean 88) pulls the estimate down from the prior (95), but the prior provides some regularization. The posterior is narrower than both the prior and the sampling distribution alone.
```

### Effective Sample Size for Normal Distribution with Known Variance

The **effective sample size** for a normal distribution with **known variance** quantifies the amount of information in the prior relative to the observed data. It represents how much weight the prior carries when updating our belief about the mean $\mu$.

 **Bayesian Framework**

In a Bayesian framework, we assume a **normal prior** for $\mu$ with mean $\mu_0$and variance $\tau_0^2$:

$$
\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)
$$

The **likelihood** of the observed data $Y_1, Y_2, \dots, Y_n$, which are normally distributed with **known variance** $\sigma^2$, is:

$$
Y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

Given the prior and likelihood, the **posterior distribution** for $\mu$is normally distributed with an updated mean and variance.

### Effective Sample Size Calculation

The **posterior mean** is a weighted average of the prior mean $\mu_0$and the sample mean $\bar{Y}$:

$$
\mu_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\frac{n}{\sigma^2} \bar{Y} + \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} \mu_0}{\frac{n}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}
$$

This can be rewritten as:

$$
\mu_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{n \bar{X} + W \mu_0}{n + W}
$$

where $W$ is the **effective sample size** of the prior, defined as:

```{important}
**Effective sample size of the Normal prior.** The prior $\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$ carries information equivalent to $W$ additional observations:

$$
W = \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau_0^2}
$$

- A **larger $W$** (smaller prior variance) means the prior carries more weight.
- A **smaller $W$** (larger prior variance) means the data dominates the posterior.
- With $W$ and $n$ data points, the posterior mean weights the prior by $W/(n+W)$ and the data by $n/(n+W)$.
```

### Example

If $\sigma^2 = 4$ and $\tau^2 = 1$, then:

$$
W = \frac{4}{1} = 4
$$

This means that the prior is equivalent to having 4 additional observations. If the observed data size $n = 10$, the total "effective" sample size is $10 + 4 = 14$.

Thus, the **effective sample size** provides a way to quantify the weight of the prior in relation to the actual data sample size.


### Example Calculation
Let's consider an example where:

- The prior mean is $\mu_0 = 50$
- The prior variance is $\tau_0^2 = 25$
- The known variance of the data is $\sigma^2 = 16$
- We collect a sample of $n = 10$ observations with a sample mean $\bar{Y} = 52$

#### Updating the Prior with Data
Using the formulas above, we first compute the precisions (inverse variances):

- Prior precision: $1/\tau_0^2 = 1/25 = 0.04$
- Data precision: $n/\sigma^2 = 10/16 = 0.625$
- Total precision: $0.04 + 0.625 = 0.665$

- **Posterior mean** (precision-weighted average):

$$
\mu_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{1/\tau_0^2}{1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2} \mu_0 + \frac{n/\sigma^2}{1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2} \bar{Y} = \frac{0.04}{0.665} \cdot 50 + \frac{0.625}{0.665} \cdot 52
$$

Simplifying, we get: $\mu_{\text{posterior}} \approx 3.01 + 48.87 = 51.88$

- **Posterior variance**:

$$
\tau_{\text{posterior}}^2 = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{25} + \frac{10}{16}} = \frac{1}{0.665} \approx 1.50
$$

Thus, the posterior distribution for $\mu$ is:

$$
\mu \mid Y \sim \mathcal{N}(51.88, \; 1.50)
$$

Note how the data (mean 52) dominates the posterior because the effective sample size of the prior is only $W = \sigma^2/\tau_0^2 = 16/25 = 0.64$ — much less than the $n = 10$ data points.

### Predictive distribution for new data

Consider again the model $x \mid \mu \sim N(\mu, \sigma_0^2)$, where $\mu \sim N(m_0, s_0^2)$ and $\sigma_0^2$ is known. Here we derive the marginal distribution for the data, given by:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(y \mid \mu) f(\mu) d\mu
$$

This represents the prior predictive distribution for a new data point $x$.

#### Step 1: Rewriting the model

We rewrite the model in a more convenient form:

$$
y = \mu + \epsilon
$$

where $\epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma_0^2)$, and

$$
\mu = m_0 + \eta
$$

where $\eta \sim N(0, s_0^2)$, with $\epsilon$ and $\eta$ independent.

#### Step 2: Substituting $\mu$ into the equation for $y$

Substitute $\mu = m_0 + \eta$ into the equation for $y$:

$$
y = m_0 + \eta + \epsilon
$$

#### Step 3: Finding the distribution of $y$

Recall that adding two normal random variables results in another normal random variable. So, $y$ is normal, and we can compute the mean and variance.

The expected value of $y$ is:

$$
E(y) = E(m_0 + \eta + \epsilon) = m_0 + E(\eta) + E(\epsilon) = m_0 + 0 + 0 = m_0
$$

The variance of $y$ is:

$$
\text{Var}(y) = \text{Var}(m_0 + \eta + \epsilon) = \text{Var}(m_0) + \text{Var}(\eta) + \text{Var}(\epsilon)
$$

Since $\text{Var}(m_0) = 0$, we have:

$$
\text{Var}(y) = s_0^2 + \sigma_0^2
$$

Thus, the marginal distribution for $y$ is:

$$
x \sim N(m_0, s_0^2 + \sigma_0^2)
$$

```{note}
**Predictive variance = prior variance + sampling variance.** The predictive distribution is always wider than the posterior because it accounts for two sources of uncertainty: uncertainty about the parameter $\mu$ (captured by $s_0^2$ or its posterior equivalent) and inherent sampling noise ($\sigma_0^2$). This distinction is crucial when making predictions about individual observations versus estimating the population mean.
```

#### Posterior Predictive Distribution

The posterior predictive distribution follows the same form, but with $m_0$ and $s_0^2$ replaced by their posterior updates based on the observed data.


## Bayesian Inference with Unknown Mean and Variance

When both the mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ are **unknown**, we can specify a **conjugate prior** in a hierarchical fashion.

### Hierarchical Model for Normal Data
Suppose we have data $Y_i$ that follows an independent, identically distributed (iid) normal distribution with **unknown** mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$:

$$
Y_i \mid \mu, \sigma^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

### Prior for $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$
We can specify the prior in two steps:

1. **Prior for $\mu$ given $\sigma^2$**:
   - $\mu$ is normally distributed, conditional on $\sigma^2$, with mean $M$ and variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{W}$.
   - $W$ represents the **effective sample size** of the prior and scales with $\sigma^2$:

$$
\mu \mid \sigma^2 \sim \mathcal{N}\left(M, \frac{\sigma^2}{W}\right)
$$

2. **Prior for $\sigma^2$**:
   - The conjugate prior for $\sigma^2$ is an **[inverse gamma distribution](4.InverseGammaDistribution.md)** with parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$:

 $$
\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)
$$

### Posterior Distributions
After observing data $Y = (Y_1, \dots, Y_n)$, we can compute the **posterior distributions** for both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ based on these priors.

```{important}
**Normal-Inverse-Gamma posterior (unknown mean and variance).**

1. **Posterior for $\sigma^2$** — Inverse-Gamma with updated parameters:

$$
\sigma^2 \mid X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(\alpha + \frac{N}{2}, \;\beta + \frac{1}{2} \left(\sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2 + \frac{N W}{N + W} (\bar{Y} - M)^2\right)\right)
$$

2. **Posterior for $\mu$ given $\sigma^2$** — Normal with updated parameters:

$$
\mu \mid \sigma^2, Y \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\frac{N \bar{Y} + W M}{N + W}, \;\frac{\sigma^2}{N + W}\right)
$$

The posterior mean of $\mu$ is again a weighted average of the prior mean $M$ and data mean $\bar{Y}$, with weights $W$ and $N$.
```

### Marginalizing Over $\sigma^2$
In many cases, we're primarily interested in inference for $\mu$ and may not care about $\sigma^2$. We can **marginalize out** $\sigma^2$, integrating over its posterior distribution. This results in the marginal posterior for $\mu$, which follows a **Student's T-distribution**.

- **Marginal posterior for $\mu$**:

$$
\mu \mid Y \sim t\left(\text{loc} = \frac{N \bar{Y} + W M}{N + W}, \text{scale} = \sqrt{\frac{\beta + \frac{1}{2} \left(\sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2 + \frac{N W}{N + W} (\bar{Y} - M)^2\right)}{(N + W)(\alpha + \frac{N}{2})}}, \text{df} = 2\alpha + N \right)
$$

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example: Stroop Interference Effect
:class: tip
A cognitive psychologist studies whether the Stroop interference effect differs between young adults and older adults. In the Stroop task, participants name the ink color of color words (e.g., the word "RED" printed in blue ink). The **interference effect** is the difference in response time between incongruent and congruent trials, measured in milliseconds.

She models these RT differences as normally distributed with unknown mean $\mu$ (the true interference effect) and unknown variance $\sigma^2$ (individual variability). Her prior guess for the interference effect is $M = 100$ ms with low confidence ($W = 0.1$), and $\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(3, 5000)$.

After testing $n = 25$ older adults with $\bar{y} = 142$ ms and $s^2 = 2800$, she updates the posterior for both parameters. She runs the same analysis for $n = 30$ young adults ($\bar{y} = 85$ ms, $s^2 = 1600$). By drawing samples from the marginal posterior of $\mu$ for each group, she can compute $P(\mu_{\text{old}} > \mu_{\text{young}})$ — a direct Bayesian answer that quantifies the evidence for age-related differences without requiring p-values or null hypotheses.
```

### Posterior Predictive Distribution
The **posterior predictive distribution**, which gives the distribution of future observations based on the data, also follows a **T-distribution**.

### Extensions
This hierarchical model can be extended in several ways:

1. **Multivariate normal case**: This requires vector and matrix notation to model multiple dimensions.
2. **Hierarchical priors**: We can specify hierarchical priors for parameters such as $M$, $W$, and $\beta$ for more complex modeling scenarios.

## Key Takeaways

- The **normal distribution** is the workhorse of continuous Bayesian inference, appearing frequently in cognitive science (test scores, neural measurements, physical measurements).
- If the **variance** is known and only the **mean** is unknown, the **normal distribution** is a conjugate prior. The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and sample mean, weighted by precision.
- The **effective sample size** $W = \sigma^2 / \tau_0^2$ quantifies how much information the prior contributes relative to the data.
- When both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are unknown, the **Normal-Inverse-Gamma** conjugate prior yields tractable posteriors: Inverse-Gamma for $\sigma^2$ and Normal for $\mu \mid \sigma^2$.
- **Marginalizing** over $\sigma^2$ results in a **T-distribution** for $\mu$, which has heavier tails than a Normal — reflecting the additional uncertainty from estimating the variance.
- The **predictive distribution** is always wider than the posterior because it accounts for both parameter uncertainty and sampling variability.
