## Beyond Total Sample Size: The Allocation Problem

In experimental psychology, cognitive neuroscience, and computational social science, datasets rarely consist of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations. Instead, observations are structured hierarchically:
* **Repeated-Measures Experiments**: $J$ human participants each complete $K$ reaction-time trials.
* **Stimulus-Sampled Experiments**: $J$ participants evaluate $I$ words, faces, or insight problem triads.
* **Clustered Field Trials**: $J$ schools or classrooms each contain $K$ students.

A researcher with a fixed budget or observation cap (e.g. $N_{\text{total}} = 2{,}000$ trials) faces a foundational structural decision:  
**Is it better to recruit $J = 20$ participants and test them on $K = 100$ trials, or recruit $J = 100$ participants and test them on $K = 20$ trials?**

Total sample size ($N = J \times K$) does not answer this question. This chapter provides the mathematical principles and simulation workflows to optimize sample allocation under real-world constraints {cite}`westfall2014statistical,brysbaert2018power,gelman2021regression`.

---

## 1. Participants ($J$) vs. Repeated Trials ($K$): The Precision Ceiling

Consider a balanced random-intercept design where participant $j \in \{1, \dots, J\}$ completes trial $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$:

$$
y_{jk} = \mu + u_j + \epsilon_{jk}, \qquad u_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma_u^2), \qquad \epsilon_{jk} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma_e^2),
$$

where $\sigma_u^2$ is between-participant heterogeneity and $\sigma_e^2$ is trial-level measurement noise. The sampling variance of the estimated population grand mean $\hat{\mu}$ is:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mu}) = \frac{\sigma_u^2}{J} + \frac{\sigma_e^2}{J \times K} \implies \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\mu}) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_u^2}{J} + \frac{\sigma_e^2}{J \times K}}.
$$

### The Impassable Precision Floor

Notice the asymptotic behavior as the number of repeated trials per participant approaches infinity ($K \to \infty$):

$$
\lim_{K \to \infty} \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\mu}) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_u^2}{J} + 0} = \frac{\sigma_u}{\sqrt{J}}.
$$

```{important}
**The Law of Diminishing Trial Returns:**  
Increasing trials $K$ reduces within-person measurement noise $\sigma_e^2 / (JK)$, but it cannot reduce between-person uncertainty $\sigma_u^2 / J$. If between-person variation is substantial, testing the same $20$ participants for $1{,}000$ trials provides almost zero additional information about the human population! To improve scientific precision, researchers **must recruit more participants ($J$)**.
```

### Numerical Comparison ($N_{\text{total}} = 2{,}000$, $\sigma_u = 10$, $\sigma_e = 20$)

| Design Strategy | Participants ($J$) | Trials/Participant ($K$) | Total Obs ($N$) | Between Term $\sigma_u^2/J$ | Within Term $\sigma_e^2/(JK)$ | Grand Mean $\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\mu})$ |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **Few People, Many Trials** | $20$ | $100$ | $2{,}000$ | $5.00$ | $0.20$ | **$2.28$** |
| **Balanced Allocation** | $50$ | $40$ | $2{,}000$ | $2.00$ | $0.20$ | **$1.48$** |
| **Many People, Few Trials** | $100$ | $20$ | $2{,}000$ | $1.00$ | $0.20$ | **$1.10$** |
| **Broad Sampling** | $200$ | $10$ | $2{,}000$ | $0.50$ | $0.20$ | **$0.84$** |

With the exact same total data volume ($N = 2{,}000$), shifting allocation from $J = 20$ to $J = 200$ reduces the standard error by **nearly $65\%$**!

## 2. Crossed Random Effects: Participants ($J$) and Items ($I$)

In language processing, cognitive psychology, and social perception, stimuli (words, sentences, faces, problem triads) are not fixed constants; they are sampled from a broader linguistic or visual population {cite}`westfall2014statistical,judd2012treating`.

In a **crossed random-effects model**:

$$
y_{ji} = \mu + u_j + v_i + \epsilon_{ji}, \qquad u_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma_{\text{person}}^2), \quad v_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma_{\text{item}}^2), \quad \epsilon_{ji} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma_e^2).
$$

The standard error of the grand mean now depends on two distinct sample sizes:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\mu}) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\text{person}}^2}{J} + \frac{\sigma_{\text{item}}^2}{I} + \frac{\sigma_e^2}{J \times I}}.
$$

### The Stimulus Fallacy
If an experiment uses only $I = 8$ problem triads, the second term $\sigma_{\text{item}}^2 / 8$ establishes an unyielding precision bottleneck. Even if the researcher tests $J = 10{,}000$ participants, the study cannot generalize findings to new items!

```{tip}
**Generalizability Requires Dual Sampling:**  
If a scientific claim asserts that an effect applies to both **people** and **stimuli**, candidate designs must vary both $J$ (participants) and $I$ (items) simultaneously during simulation-based design.
```

## 3. Clustered Field Trials: The Intra-Class Correlation (ICC)

When observations are sampled in geographic or institutional clusters (e.g. patients within clinics, or students within classrooms), observations within the same cluster correlate.

### The Electric Company Educational TV Trial (*ROS* Chapter 16 & 19)
Consider the randomized trial evaluating the *Electric Company* educational television program across $J = 192$ classrooms in 4 cities. Because students in the same classroom share the same teacher, peer environment, and classroom culture, student outcomes are clustered:

$$
y_{jk} = \beta_0 + \tau T_j + u_j + \epsilon_{jk}, \qquad u_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma_{\text{class}}^2), \quad \epsilon_{jk} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0, \sigma_{\text{student}}^2).
$$

The **Intra-Class Correlation (ICC)** measures the proportion of variance attributable to clusters:

$$
\text{ICC} \equiv \rho = \frac{\sigma_{\text{class}}^2}{\sigma_{\text{class}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{student}}^2}.
$$

### The Design Effect (Variance Inflation Factor)
For a cluster size of $m$ students per classroom, the effective sample size is diminished by the **Design Effect (DE)**:

$$
\text{DE} = 1 + (m - 1)\rho \implies N_{\text{effective}} = \frac{N_{\text{total}}}{\text{DE}} = \frac{J \times m}{1 + (m - 1)\rho}.
$$

* If $\rho = 0.15$ and classrooms have $m = 25$ students, $\text{DE} = 1 + (24 \times 0.15) = 4.6$.
* A sample of $1{,}000$ clustered students provides the statistical precision of only $1{,}000 / 4.6 \approx \mathbf{217}$ independent students!
* In clustered designs, adding more schools or classrooms ($J$) is far more informative than adding more students per classroom ($m$).

## 4. Optimal Allocation Under a Fixed Budget

In empirical research, recruiting a new participant incurs an administrative setup cost $C_J$ (recruitment, screening, scheduling, compensation), while administering an additional trial incurs a marginal cost $C_K$ (task time, software overhead):

$$
\text{Total Cost} = C_0 + C_J \cdot J + C_K \cdot (J \times K).
$$

Given a fixed total budget $B$, what ratio of $J$ and $K$ minimizes $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mu})$? Using Lagrange multipliers, the optimal number of trials per participant $K^*$ is:

$$
K^* = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_e^2}{\sigma_u^2} \times \frac{C_J}{C_K}} = \left( \frac{\sigma_e}{\sigma_u} \right) \sqrt{\frac{C_J}{C_K}}.
$$

* When participant recruitment is expensive ($C_J \gg C_K$), participants should complete more trials ($K^* \uparrow$).
* When between-person heterogeneity dominates ($\sigma_u \gg \sigma_e$), recruit more participants and keep individual sessions short ($K^* \downarrow$).

## 5. Guided Practice & Concept Checks

````{dropdown} Question 1: Adding trials vs. adding people
A visual perception study tests $J = 15$ participants on $K = 500$ trials each (7,500 trials total). The posterior interval for the population reaction time is still too wide. Will doubling trials to $K = 1{,}000$ solve the problem?

**Answer.** Almost certainly not. Because $K = 500$ is already large, trial-level noise $\sigma_e^2 / (JK)$ has already been compressed. The remaining uncertainty is dominated by $\sigma_{\text{person}}^2 / J$. Doubling to $K = 1{,}000$ reduces total variance by less than $1\%$. The researcher must recruit more participants ($J$).
````

````{dropdown} Question 2: The item sampling dilemma
A researcher finds a highly significant effect ($p < 0.001$) of emotional tone on memory using 4 positive words and 4 negative words across $N = 400$ participants. What is the principal design vulnerability?

**Answer.** Stimulus sampling limitation (the item fallacy). With only 4 words per condition, the effect could easily be driven by idiosyncratic lexical features (e.g. word length, concreteness, or frequency) rather than emotional tone. A mixed-effects model with crossed random effects for items ($I$) would reveal substantial uncertainty about whether the effect generalizes to the language as a whole.
````
