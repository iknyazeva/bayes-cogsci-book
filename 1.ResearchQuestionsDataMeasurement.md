# Session 1: Research Questions, Data and Measurement

Bayesian analysis is sometimes introduced as a sequence of distributions and algorithms: choose a likelihood, assign priors, run a sampler, and summarize the posterior. In empirical research, however, many consequential decisions have already been made before a statistical model is written. A researcher has decided what phenomenon matters, how it will be observed, which units will enter the dataset, and to which population the conclusions should apply.

These decisions determine what the posterior can mean. A carefully fitted model cannot repair an indicator that does not represent the intended construct, a sample that excludes an important part of the target population, or a causal claim unsupported by the study design. This chapter therefore begins with the relation between a substantive question and the quantity that an analysis is intended to estimate.

## 1. From a substantive question to a target quantity

Consider the question, “Does meditation improve wellbeing?” It names a topic, but it does not yet define an analysis. We need to know which form of meditation is being considered, what “wellbeing” means, who is being studied, when it is measured, and what comparison is intended. Different answers lead to different target quantities and may require different data.

The sequence from a substantive question to an estimand can be illustrated with a sociological study of a four-day working week:

Three distinctions recur throughout the course:

| Analytic aim | Example question | Target quantity | What the result does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | What is the distribution of life satisfaction among first-year students at a specified university in 2026? | A population mean, proportion, quantile, or distribution | A relationship outside the defined population or period |
| Predictive | What is the probability that a student with a given history will leave the programme before the second year? | A predictive distribution for a new outcome | The effect of changing any predictor |
| Causal | How would average life satisfaction change if eligible students were offered a defined programme rather than the current provision? | A contrast between potential outcomes under specified interventions | An effect in populations or settings not covered by the design |

These categories are not a claim that every scientific question fits neatly into one box. They are a practical way to prevent a descriptive comparison, a prediction, and an intervention effect from being treated as interchangeable.

An **estimand** is the population-level quantity the analysis is intended to learn. For example, a descriptive estimand might be

$$
\theta_{\text{mean}} = \mathbb{E}_{i \in \mathcal{P}}[Y_i],
$$

where $\mathcal{P}$ is the declared target population. A causal estimand might be the average treatment effect

$$
\theta_{\text{ATE}} = \mathbb{E}_{i \in \mathcal{P}}[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)].
$$

A predictive analysis instead targets a distribution such as

$$
p(\widetilde{Y} \mid X=x, D),
$$

where $D$ denotes the observed data and $\widetilde{Y}$ is a future or otherwise unobserved outcome.

The estimand should not be confused with an **estimator**, which is a rule or model used to learn the estimand from data, or with an **estimate**, which is the result obtained in a particular analysis. Changing a model may change the estimate even when the estimand remains fixed. Changing the estimand means that the scientific question itself has changed.

## 2. Constructs, indicators, and measurement occasions

Many central concepts in the social and cognitive sciences are not observed directly. Political trust, social exclusion, working-memory capacity, and insight are theoretical constructs. A survey response, task score, or reaction time is an observed indicator whose interpretation depends on the measurement procedure.

| Element | Question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Construct | What theoretical attribute or process is of interest? | Political trust |
| Indicator | What value is actually recorded? | Response to a five-category trust item |
| Observational unit | What entity contributes an observation? | Respondent |
| Measurement occasion | When or under what condition is it observed? | Survey wave or experimental trial |
| Level of analysis | At what level is the target quantity defined? | Person, school, organization, district, or country |

An observed variable need not always be interpreted as a fallible measure of a latent construct. Age recorded from an administrative register, the number of completed applications, and experimental assignment can sometimes be treated more directly. The important point is to state what interpretation is being made and what features of the observation process could invalidate it.

Repeated measurements also change the structure of the data. If each participant completes several trials, trials are nested within participants; if the same stimuli are shown to many participants, observations may be crossed by participant and stimulus. The number of rows in a dataset is therefore not necessarily the number of independent observational units. The corresponding multilevel models are developed in Session 10, but the structure must be recognized when the research question is formulated.

## 3. Reliability, validity, and measurement error

Reliability concerns the consistency of measurements under a stated set of conditions. Validity concerns whether the proposed interpretation and use of those measurements are supported. Neither is an intrinsic property of a variable name: both depend on the population, procedure, setting, and intended inference.

A simple classical measurement model writes an observed score as

$$
X_i = T_i + \epsilon_i,
$$

where $T_i$ is a stable true score within the model and $\epsilon_i$ is additive measurement error with mean zero. Under the additional assumption that the error is independent of the true score, reliability can be written as

$$
\rho_{XX}=
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(T)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)}
=
\frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2+\sigma_\epsilon^2}.
$$

This expression is useful for studying one particular error process; it is not a universal definition suitable for every measurement design. Rater disagreement, learning across trials, differential item functioning, changes in the construct over time, and systematic response styles require richer models.

:::{important}
Reliability is not evidence of validity by itself. Reaction time may be recorded very consistently while remaining a poor indicator of abstract reasoning ability. Conversely, a task may be theoretically relevant to a construct but yield unstable scores because it contains too few trials or uses an inconsistent scoring procedure.
:::

The appropriate response is not simply to label a measure “reliable” or “valid.” The researcher should state which evidence supports the proposed interpretation, which sources of error are represented in the model, and which remain outside it.

## 4. When measurement error attenuates an association

The familiar attenuation result is informative precisely because its assumptions are clear. Suppose an outcome is related to a latent predictor $T_i$:

$$
Y_i=\alpha+\beta T_i+\nu_i,
$$

but the analysis uses a noisy proxy

$$
X_i=T_i+\epsilon_i.
$$

Assume that the errors have mean zero and that $\epsilon_i$ is independent of both $T_i$ and $\nu_i$. If a naive linear regression of $Y$ on $X$ is fitted, its slope converges to

$$
\beta_{\text{naive}}
=
\beta\frac{\operatorname{Var}(T)}
{\operatorname{Var}(T)+\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)}
=
\beta\rho_{XX}.
$$

Under this classical model, measurement error in the predictor moves the slope toward zero. If $\rho_{XX}=0.60$, for example, the limiting naive slope is $0.60\beta$.

The direction is not guaranteed under every form of measurement error. Error that depends on the outcome, differs across groups, is correlated with other predictors, or affects categorical classifications can attenuate, inflate, or even reverse an association. Outcome measurement error has different consequences from predictor measurement error. The classical formula should therefore be presented as a result derived from assumptions, not as a general law.

:::{warning}
A larger sample reduces sampling uncertainty under the assumed sampling process; it does not remove systematic measurement error, selection bias, dependence, or model misspecification. With enough observations, a naive model can estimate a biased target very precisely.
:::

## 5. Case study: what does the Human Development Index measure?

In applied cognitive, social, and biomedical research, we rarely measure theoretical constructs directly. Instead, we construct **composite indicators** that combine multiple observed variables into a single score. But what does a composite index actually measure, and how do our aggregation choices affect substantive scientific conclusions?

Gelman, Hill, and Vehtari (*Regression and Other Stories*, Chapter 2) examine the **Human Development Index (HDI)** across the 50 U.S. states and Washington, D.C., to illustrate how looking at data in multiple representations exposes hidden measurement assumptions.

### 5.1 The Theoretical Construct vs. Operational Formula

The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) originally devised the HDI (developed by economists Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen) to provide a counterweight to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita as the sole benchmark of national progress. The index operationalizes "human capabilities" across three core dimensions:

1. **Health & Longevity**: Measured by life expectancy at birth ($\text{LE}$):

$$
I_{\text{health}} = \frac{\text{LE} - 20}{85 - 20}
$$

2. **Knowledge & Education**: Measured by adult literacy (weighted $2/3$) and gross educational enrollment (weighted $1/3$):

$$
I_{\text{education}} = \frac{2}{3}I_{\text{literacy}} + \frac{1}{3}I_{\text{enrollment}}
$$

3. **Standard of Living**: Measured by the natural logarithm of per capita income/GDP at purchasing power parity:

$$
I_{\text{income}} = \frac{\ln(\text{GDP}) - \ln(100)}{\ln(40000) - \ln(100)}
$$

In the classic formulation used in the original state comparison, the three dimensional scores are combined via an arithmetic mean:

$$
\text{HDI} = \frac{1}{3}I_{\text{health}} + \frac{1}{3}I_{\text{education}} + \frac{1}{3}I_{\text{income}}
$$

---

### 5.2 The Measurement Trap: Variance Dominance

On paper, the index assigns equal nominal weight ($1/3$) to health, education, and income. But what happens when this formula is applied across administrative units within an already-developed, high-income country like the United States?

* **Narrow Variation in Health and Schooling**: Across the 50 states, life expectancy spans a narrow range (approximately $75$ to $81$ years), and adult literacy and basic school enrollment are nearly saturated near $95\%\text{--}99\%$.
* **Wide Dynamic Range in Income**: In contrast, average state personal income in 2000 varied by nearly a factor of two: from $\$19,141$ in Mississippi to $\$37,808$ in Connecticut.
* **The Mathematical Consequence**: When normalized variables are summed with equal weights, the component with the **largest relative variance dominates the composite score**. 

As Gelman pointed out, the resulting map of "human development" across U.S. states is effectively a map of state income with a logarithmic transformation and a catchy title.

### 5.5 Core Measurement Lessons for Bayesian Modellers

1. **Mathematical Elegance Does Not Guarantee Construct Validity**:
   A formula may look balanced on paper (equal $1/3$ weights), but empirical variance determines which dimension actually drives the output.
2. **Look for Outliers and Internal Inconsistencies**:
   Scatterplots and rank comparisons (such as South Carolina vs. Kentucky, or Utah's outperformance) are critical diagnostic tools to see whether the model captures real distinctions or merely aggregates noise.
3. **Beware the Ecological Fallacy**:
   Aggregate indices measured at the state, school, or county level cannot be assumed to hold for individuals within those groups. Modeling hierarchical variation (Session 10) is the Bayesian remedy for separating group-level and individual-level effects.
4. **Data Representations Must Complement Each Other**:
   Maps show spatial clustering; scatterplots show continuous associations; rank plots show ordinal fidelity. No single graph tells the whole story.

## 6. From the observed sample to a target population

Statistical inference relates observed data to a declared population. Four objects should be kept separate:

- the **target population**, to which the conclusion is intended to apply;
- the **sampling frame**, or operational list and procedure through which units could be reached;
- the **realized sample**, consisting of the units that were actually observed;
- the **measurement occasions**, which determine how many observations each sampled unit contributes.

```{mermaid}
flowchart TD
    P["Target population"] --> F["Sampling frame"]
    F --> S["Realized sample"]
    S --> O["Observed measurement occasions"]
```

Coverage error arises when relevant members of the target population are absent from the sampling frame. Nonresponse becomes consequential when participation is related to the outcome or to variables that remain inadequately represented after adjustment. Dependence arises when observations share participants, households, schools, organizations, locations, or periods.

These problems are not solved by Bayesian computation alone. Priors and hierarchical models can represent uncertainty and structure, but generalization still depends on assumptions connecting the observed sample to the target population. Missing data, poststratification, and limited transportability are examined in Session 13.

## 7. Data provenance and ethical constraints

Before modelling, create a short provenance record. It should answer:

- Who collected or generated the data, for what original purpose, and in what period?
- What inclusion, exclusion, and preprocessing decisions produced the analysis table?
- Are variable definitions and measurement units documented?
- Are the data real, adapted from a published case, or synthetic?
- What licence or permission governs teaching and redistribution?
- Could combining variables expose participants or reproduce a harmful classification?
- Which groups or events are systematically absent from the data?

Provenance is part of the analysis rather than administrative decoration. Without it, an apparently precise posterior may be attached to an ambiguous population, an undocumented transformation, or a measure whose meaning has changed.

2. What is the probability that a currently employed person with a specified covariate profile will leave within one year?

:::{dropdown} Show answer and reasoning
1. **Descriptive.** The target is the proportion of employees in the declared population who would report an intention to leave within twelve months. The population must be stated; a proportion in the realized sample is not automatically the population estimand.
2. **Predictive.** The target is a predictive probability for a new or otherwise unobserved employee outcome, $P(\widetilde{Y}=1\mid X=x,D)$. The predictor profile and the population from which the new case is drawn must be specified.

### Exercise 2: When does “difficulty” mean the same thing?

*This work was conducted in the **Laboratory of Cognitive Research in IHB**; project materials, task protocols, and preregistration are available on [OSF](https://osf.io/vmt5z/overview).*

**Question.** In a semantic-triads cognitive experiment, each participant evaluates 60 semantic word triads across two sequential experimental stages:

```{image} triads_stage1_prospective_design.png
:alt: Stage 1 prospective difficulty sequence
:align: center
:width: 90%
```

1. **Stage 1 (Prospective Rating & Spontaneous Candidate)**:
   - A fixation cross is displayed for 500 ms.
   - The three-word triad and a 0–100 difficulty slider are displayed together for 3 seconds under the explicit instruction *not* to deliberately solve the task.
   - The triad disappears and the slider remains available for another 3 seconds (up to 6 seconds total) for the participant to rate expected difficulty.
   - The participant can enter a candidate answer that appeared spontaneously (limited to 12 seconds).
   - This sequence is repeated for all 60 triads.

```{image} triads_stage2_solution_aha_retrospective_design.png
:alt: Stage 2 solution, Aha report, and retrospective difficulty sequence
:align: center
:width: 90%
```

2. **Stage 2 (Intentional Solution & Retrospective Rating)**:
   - A fixation cross is displayed for 500 ms.
   - The same triad is presented for an intentional solution attempt lasting up to 30 seconds.
   - The participant enters an answer.
   - The participant reports whether an "Aha!" insight experience occurred (`yes` / `no`).
   - The participant rates retrospective difficulty on the same 0–100 slider, without the Stage 1 time limit.
   - This sequence is repeated for all 60 triads.

**Tasks to consider before opening the answer:**
1. Identify the theoretical constructs, recorded indicators, observational unit, measurement occasions, and repeated-measures structure.
2. Which sources of variation are psychologically meaningful, and which represent measurement error or nuisance variation?
3. Do the two ratings measure exactly the same construct? Why or why not?

:::{dropdown} Show answer and reasoning
- **Constructs**: Anticipated difficulty before solving (prospective difficulty) and experienced or retrospectively judged difficulty after the attempt (retrospective difficulty). They are related cognitive assessments, but they are **not identical theoretical constructs**.
- **Indicators**: `comp_1` (the prospective 0–100 rating) and `post_comp_2` (the retrospective 0–100 rating).
- **Observational Unit**: The participant–triad assessment $(i, j)$. A row in this dataset is neither an independent person nor an independent item alone.
- **Measurement Occasions**: The pre-solution Stage 1 rating and the post-attempt Stage 2 rating for each triad.
- **Repeated Structure**: Crossed design — each participant evaluates multiple triads and each triad is evaluated by multiple participants (crossed random effects for participants and items).
- **Potentially Meaningful Variation**: Triad semantic distance/structure, spontaneous candidate-answer availability, candidate correctness, solution success, deliberate search latency, Aha! phenomenological insight, practice/fatigue over the 60 trials, and stable participant cognitive differences.
- **Possible Measurement Error or Nuisance Variation**: Accidental slider clicks, response heaping and midpoint anchoring (e.g. at 50), missing ratings, timing or recording lag, and undocumented exclusions.
- **Validity Boundary**: If the target construct is *current perceived subjective difficulty*, candidate-answer availability is an integral part of the cognitive phenomenon. If the target is *stable objective item difficulty*, spontaneous guesses introduce occasion-specific variation. "Error" cannot be categorized without first declaring the intended theoretical construct!

> **Key Takeaway**: The same label—"difficulty"—can denote fundamentally different constructs when the rating is collected at different stages of a cognitive process. Matching response scales (both 0–100) do not make indicators interchangeable!
:::

### Exercise 3: Simulate classical attenuation

**Question.** The following synthetic example generates a latent predictor, an outcome, and proxy measurements with different reliabilities. Before opening the answer, predict what will happen to the estimated slope when reliability decreases. Then run the simulation with several seeds and sample sizes and determine which setting changes sampling variability and which determines the limiting attenuation.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n = 2_000
true_beta = 0.8
latent_x = rng.normal(size=n)
outcome = true_beta * latent_x + rng.normal(scale=1.0, size=n)

for reliability in (0.9, 0.6, 0.3):
    error_sd = np.sqrt((1 - reliability) / reliability)
    observed_x = latent_x + rng.normal(scale=error_sd, size=n)
    naive_slope = np.polyfit(observed_x, outcome, deg=1)[0]
    print(f"Reliability: {reliability:.1f} -> Observed Slope: {naive_slope:.2f}")
```

:::{dropdown} Show answer and reasoning
Under the classical additive-error assumptions used here, lower reliability produces stronger attenuation toward zero. Increasing $N$ reduces sampling variability but does not change the limiting naive slope:

$$
\operatorname*{plim}_{N\to\infty}\widehat{\beta}_{\text{naive}}
=\beta\rho_{XX}.
$$

With $\rho_{XX}=0.60$ and $\beta=0.80$, the limiting slope is $0.48$. Residual outcome noise and sample size affect how much the fitted slope varies across simulated datasets. Reliability determines the attenuation in this particular model. This conclusion does not automatically extend to differential, correlated, or categorical measurement error.
:::

### Quick checks

````{dropdown} Q4. Can a very large sample compensate for a weak indicator?
**Answer.** Not in general. A large sample can reduce sampling uncertainty for the quantity defined by the observed indicator. It cannot establish that the indicator measures the intended construct, and it does not automatically correct systematic measurement or selection errors.
````

````{dropdown} Q5. Can an indicator be reliable but invalid?
**Answer.** Yes. A procedure can produce consistent values while supporting the wrong interpretation. Reliability is usually necessary for precise individual differences, but it is not sufficient evidence that the intended construct is being measured.
````

````{dropdown} Q6. Does measurement error always move an association toward zero?
**Answer.** No. Attenuation toward zero follows from the classical additive, independent error model for a predictor. Differential, correlated, categorical, or otherwise nonclassical errors can produce other forms and directions of bias.
````

## 9. Research protocol milestone 1

Prepare the first section of your course project using the following headings:

1. **Substantive question:** State the question in one or two sentences.
2. **Analytic aim:** Identify the immediate descriptive, predictive, or causal aim and explain the classification.
3. **Constructs and indicators:** Name the theoretical concepts and the exact recorded variables used to represent them.
4. **Units and occasions:** Specify the observational unit, measurement occasions, and any clustering or crossed structure.
5. **Sample and target population:** Describe the realized sample, sampling frame if known, and the population to which the conclusion is intended to apply.
6. **Target quantity:** State the estimand or predictive target in words and, where possible, notation.
7. **Provenance and ethics:** Record the data source, transformations, permissions, exclusions, and one important ethical or coverage limitation.

The milestone should be concrete enough that another researcher could determine whether the available data are capable of addressing the stated question. No statistical model is required yet.
