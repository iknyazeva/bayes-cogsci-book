# Session 1: Research Questions, Data and Measurement

## 1. Introduction: Starting from Substantive Problems

Bayesian data analysis is often taught as an algorithmic sequence of probability distributions, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samplers, and posterior density plots. In empirical research across the cognitive, social, and behavioural sciences, however, the most consequential errors occur **before** any likelihood or prior is formulated:

1. Conflating an abstract **theoretical construct** (e.g. cognitive load, political trust, generalized intelligence) with a convenient but noisy or biased **observed indicator** (e.g. reaction time, a 5-point Likert survey item, IQ score).
2. Ignoring **measurement error** and **construct validity**, leading to severe **attenuation bias** where estimated associations are systematically compressed toward zero—regardless of sample size.
3. Defining sample properties rather than a well-specified **target population** and formal **mathematical estimand**.
4. Mistaking a descriptive or predictive correlation for a causal effect without defining the counterfactual generative process.

This opening chapter establishes the methodological foundation for the entire course. We learn how to translate substantive theories into formal measurement models, define precise estimands, audit empirical data provenance, and recognize why gathering more data ($N \to \infty$) only sharpens certainty around an invalid quantity if the measurement model is flawed.

## 2. Theoretical Questions vs. Empirical Estimands

Empirical science begins with a substantive question about the world, not with statistical software. A vague intuition ("Does meditation improve wellbeing?") must be structured into an operational question:
* Who is being studied?
* What specific variation in the world is being compared or intervened upon?
* Under what conditions would an empirical finding alter our prior beliefs?

We classify all empirical questions into three fundamental categories:

```{mermaid}

    Problem --> Pred["<b>2. Predictive Question</b><br><i>What value of Y is expected given covariates X?</i>"]
    Problem --> Causal["<b>3. Causal Question</b><br><i>How would Y change if we intervened on X?</i>"]
```

1. **Descriptive Questions**: Summarizing characteristics of a defined population (e.g. *"What is the average sleep duration among first-year university students in 2026?"*).
2. **Predictive Questions**: Forecasting unobserved outcomes using observed covariates without requiring a mechanistic or causal claim (e.g. *"Given a patient's cognitive battery scores and age, what is the probability of clinical diagnosis within 24 months?"*).
3. **Causal Questions**: Quantifying how an outcome would change under an explicit intervention or counterfactual assignment (e.g. *"What is the average change in working memory capacity if individuals complete 20 hours of training versus an active control?"*).

## 3. Constructs, Operationalization, and Indicators

In cognitive and social sciences, the central concepts of interest are rarely directly observable:
* **Latent Construct ($T_i$)**: The unobserved theoretical variable (e.g. latent verbal ability, political alienation, working memory capacity).
* **Observed Indicator ($Y_i$)**: The recorded empirical score (e.g. number of words recalled on a list, score on a 7-point Likert agreement scale, response latency in milliseconds).
* **Measurement Error ($\epsilon_i$)**: The discrepancy between the true construct state and the recorded measurement.

Under the classical test theory model:

$$Y_i = T_i + \epsilon_i$$

where $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i \mid T_i] = 0$ and $\text{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma_\epsilon^2$.

### The Two Dimensions of Measurement Quality

1. **Reliability (Precision / Consistency)**: The ratio of true construct variance to total observed variance:
   $$\rho_{YY} = \frac{\text{Var}(T)}{\text{Var}(Y)} = \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2} \in [0, 1]$$
   High reliability means repeated measurements on the same stable unit yield nearly identical numbers.
2. **Validity (Construct Authenticity / Truth)**: The degree to which the indicator measures the *intended* theoretical construct rather than an irrelevant confounder.

:::{important}
**High Reliability Does Not Imply High Validity!**  
A digital millisecond timer measuring reaction time on a touch screen may have $\rho \approx 0.99$ (hyper-reliable), but if used as a proxy for abstract reasoning ability, it has near-zero construct validity (it measures motor dexterity and visual reflexes).
:::

## 4. Classical Measurement Error & Attenuation Bias

What happens when an independent predictor variable $X$ is measured with noise?

Suppose the true relationship between a latent predictor $T_i$ and an outcome $Y_i$ is linear:

$$Y_i = \alpha + \beta_{\text{true}} T_i + \nu_i, \quad \nu_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\nu^2)$$

Instead of observing $T_i$, the researcher observes a noisy proxy indicator:

$$X_i = T_i + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2), \quad \epsilon_i \perp T_i, \nu_i$$

When we regress $Y$ on the observed $X$, the estimated regression slope converges to:

$$\beta_{\text{obs}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\text{Var}(X)} = \frac{\text{Cov}(T + \epsilon, \alpha + \beta_{\text{true}} T + \nu)}{\text{Var}(T + \epsilon)}$$

Since $\text{Cov}(\epsilon, T) = 0$ and $\text{Cov}(\epsilon, \nu) = 0$:

$$\beta_{\text{obs}} = \frac{\beta_{\text{true}} \text{Var}(T)}{\text{Var}(T) + \sigma_\epsilon^2} = \beta_{\text{true}} \cdot \left(\frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2}\right) = \beta_{\text{true}} \cdot \rho_{XX}$$

:::{warning}
**The "Big Data" Fallacy**:  
If predictor reliability is $\rho_{XX} = 0.60$, the observed regression coefficient is attenuated by **40% toward zero**. Gathering $N = 10,000,000$ observations does **not** cure this bias—it only gives an extraordinarily narrow confidence interval around the *wrong* (attenuated) number.
:::

## 5. Case Study: Composite Indicators & The Human Development Index

```{note}
**Case Reference:** Gelman, Hill, Vehtari (2021), *Regression and Other Stories*, Section 2.2.
```

The Human Development Index (HDI) is a composite statistic summarizing health, education, and standard of living across nations:

$$\text{HDI} = \left( I_{\text{Health}} \cdot I_{\text{Education}} \cdot I_{\text{Income}} \right)^{1/3}$$

Where:
* $I_{\text{Health}} = \frac{\text{Life Expectancy} - 20}{85 - 20}$
* $I_{\text{Education}} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{\text{Mean Schooling}}{15} + \frac{\text{Expected Schooling}}{18} \right)$
* $I_{\text{Income}} = \frac{\ln(\text{GNI per capita}) - \ln(100)}{\ln(75,000) - \ln(100)}$

### Key Methodological Insights:
1. **Mathematical Transformations Reflect Value Choices**: Using $\ln(\text{Income})$ assumes diminishing marginal returns to wealth for human flourishing.
2. **Geometric vs. Arithmetic Mean**: A geometric mean penalizes extreme imbalance between health, education, and income, preventing a rich nation with zero schooling from scoring high.
3. **Measurement Level**: An aggregate country index obscures internal inequality and regional disparities within nations.

## 6. Observational Units, Sampling Frames & Target Populations

Statistical inference is never about a dataset in isolation; it is about generalizing from an observed sample to a broader population:

1. **Target Population ($\mathcal{P}_{\text{target}}$)**: The universe of units to which scientific conclusions apply.
2. **Sampling Frame ($\mathcal{S}_{\text{frame}}$)**: The operational mechanism used to access units (e.g. voter registration lists, mobile numbers).
3. **Realized Sample ($n$)**: The units successfully measured.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Target Population P_target (All adults aged 18–65)     │
│   ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ Sampling Frame S_frame (Registered numbers)    │   │
│   │   ┌────────────────────────────────────────┐   │   │
│   │   │ Realized Sample n (Completed surveys)  │   │   │
│   │   └────────────────────────────────────────┘   │   │
│   └────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

* **Coverage Error**: Key segments of $\mathcal{P}_{\text{target}}$ absent from $\mathcal{S}_{\text{frame}}$.
* **Nonresponse Selection Bias**: When participation probability depends on the outcome variable ($\mathbb{P}(R=1 \mid Y) \neq \mathbb{P}(R=1)$).

## 7. Concept Check Questions

```{dropdown} ❓ Question 1: The Large Sample Fallacy
**Question**: An algorithm analyzes 5 million social media posts to measure depression ($\rho_{XX} = 0.30$). The analyst claims the huge sample size eliminates measurement noise. Is this correct?

**Answer**: **No.** Large $N$ eliminates sampling variance ($\text{SE} \to 0$), but has zero impact on attenuation bias. The estimated slope will converge with extreme precision to $0.30 \times \beta_{\text{true}}$ (a 70% underestimation).
```

```{dropdown} ❓ Question 2: Reliability vs. Validity
**Question**: Can an indicator have high reliability but low validity? Can it have high validity but low reliability?

**Answer**: 
* **High Reliability, Low Validity**: Yes. A precise timer measuring reaction time is highly reliable ($\rho \approx 0.99$), but invalid for measuring abstract morality.
* **High Validity, Low Reliability**: Conceptually an indicator might target the true construct (e.g. an essay prompt testing deep thinking), but if scoring is wildly inconsistent across raters, its observed reliability is low.
```

## 8. Cumulative Research Protocol: Task 1

In your final research project, you will build a complete Bayesian research protocol. Complete **Milestone 1** for your proposed dataset:

1. **Substantive Question**: State your core scientific question in 1–2 sentences.
2. **Inferential Mode**: Declare whether your inquiry is *Descriptive*, *Predictive*, or *Causal*.
3. **Theoretical Constructs**: Name the unobserved construct for both predictor ($T_X$) and outcome ($T_Y$).
4. **Empirical Indicators**: Name the exact observed variables, scale types, and measurement units.
5. **Observational Unit**: Specify the unit $i$ (person, trial, clinic) and any clustering structure $j$.
6. **Target Population**: Define the boundary of $\mathcal{P}_{\text{target}}$ and evaluate potential coverage/selection errors.
7. **Mathematical Estimand**: Write the formal mathematical quantity $\theta$ to be learned.
