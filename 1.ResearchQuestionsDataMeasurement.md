# Lesson 1: Research questions, hypothesis tests and uncertainty

Before choosing a statistical method, ask what you want to learn and whether your data can answer that question. A research question identifies the scientific aim. A hypothesis test is one possible tool for investigating it. Estimation, prediction and decision analysis are other tools, and a study may need more than one.

This lesson connects a question to a quantity we can estimate, introduces two ways to describe uncertainty, and shows why the quantity of interest need not be a mean. You do not need to choose a probability distribution or fit a model yet.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Turn a broad research question into a specified outcome, comparison, population and target quantity.
2. Distinguish variation between observations, uncertainty about a quantity, and uncertainty about a future observation.
3. Explain what an estimate, a hypothesis test and an uncertainty interval contribute to an answer—and identify one limitation of the available data.

## 1. Start with the research question

Consider: **“Does meditation improve wellbeing?”** Before calculating anything, clarify the programme, the comparison, the measurement and the people to whom the answer should apply.

| Decision | A possible specification for a teaching example |
|---|---|
| Population | Students eligible for the programme at a named university |
| Intervention and comparison | An offer of an eight-week meditation programme versus usual provision |
| Outcome | A specified wellbeing questionnaire, scored using its documented rules |
| Measurement time | Eight weeks after assignment, with baseline scores also recorded |
| Quantity of interest | The difference in average eight-week scores under the two assignment conditions |
| Study design | Random assignment of eligible volunteers, with follow-up in both groups |

The **estimand** is the quantity we want to learn. An **estimator** is the rule or method used to learn it from data; an **estimate** is the result for the dataset in hand. Here, an estimate might be a difference of 3 score points. That number needs an uncertainty interval and an explanation of what 3 points means on the questionnaire. {cite}`gelman2021regression`

Notice the wording *an offer of the programme*. Comparing assigned groups addresses a different question from comparing people who chose to attend every class. Attendance may be related to motivation, available time or initial wellbeing. Random assignment also does not make volunteers a random sample of all students.

### A research hypothesis is not yet a statistical test

A substantive hypothesis might be “the programme improves wellbeing.” We can translate part of that idea into a statistical hypothesis about a defined score difference, such as $H_0: \Delta=0$. A test then evaluates the data relative to that hypothesis and the other assumptions used in the calculation.

A *p*-value is not the probability that $H_0$ is true, and it does not measure the size or practical importance of a difference. A threshold-based test can support a prespecified decision rule, but “reject” and “do not reject” are not the same as “an effect exists” and “no effect exists.” These distinctions are emphasized in the [American Statistical Association's statement on p-values](https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/P-ValueStatement.pdf).

Keep three questions separate:

- **Size:** How large is the difference, and how uncertain is the estimate?
- **Compatibility:** How compatible are the observations with a specified hypothesis and model?
- **Practical relevance:** Would a difference of this size matter for the scientific or applied purpose?

For illustration, researchers might specify 5 questionnaire points as a meaningful improvement. That threshold needs a justification from the measurement scale and research context; it is not supplied by a statistical convention. Evidence for a positive difference does not automatically establish an improvement of at least 5 points.

Both frequentist and Bayesian methods can estimate quantities and test hypotheses. The choice of framework follows the question; it does not replace specifying it.

### Description, prediction and causation

| Aim | Question about the same topic | What an answer requires |
|---|---|---|
| Description | How do recorded wellbeing scores differ between attendees and non-attendees? | Defined groups, measurement and a justified scope of generalization |
| Prediction | What score might a new student have after eight weeks? | A prediction target, available predictors and evaluation on relevant new observations |
| Causation | How would scores change if students were offered the programme rather than usual provision? | A defined intervention and a design or assumptions supporting that causal comparison |

A well-estimated association is not automatically a causal effect. We will develop causal analysis later; recognizing the distinction is already part of Lesson 1.

## 2. Check what the data actually represent

A practicing statistician asks these questions before recommending a model:

1. **What does a row represent?** One participant, a questionnaire occasion, or one trial? Record participant and item identifiers when observations repeat. Forty people completing 100 trials produce 4,000 rows, not 4,000 independent people.
2. **What was measured?** Record the scale, units, timing and possible values. A consistently recorded score can still be a poor indicator of the intended construct: reliability alone does not establish validity.
3. **How were people and conditions selected?** Distinguish recruitment, random assignment, voluntary participation and the target population.
4. **What is missing or excluded?** Check missing-value codes, duplicates and exclusions. If students with the poorest outcomes do not return, complete follow-up cases may give a distorted answer.
5. **What does a plot reveal?** Inspect distributions and group patterns before interpreting a single summary. Look for impossible values, skewness, unequal spread and repeated observations.

A larger sample can reduce sampling uncertainty under a suitable model. It does not by itself correct biased recruitment, systematic measurement error or an inappropriate independence assumption. Document the data source and transformations so another analyst can understand the same dataset.

## 3. What is uncertain?

The word *uncertainty* can refer to different things.

| Object | Example | Useful description |
|---|---|---|
| Variation between observations | Students have different wellbeing scores | An outcome distribution, standard deviation or quantiles |
| Uncertainty about a target quantity | We do not know the population score difference | An interval or distribution describing uncertainty about that difference |
| Uncertainty about a future observation | We do not know a new student's score | A predictive distribution, incorporating individual variation as well as uncertainty about the model parameters |

More data may make the average difference more precise while substantial variation between individuals remains. A narrow interval for an average does not mean that individual outcomes are nearly identical.

### Random variables and distributions

A **random variable** represents a numerical outcome before we observe it under a probability model. For example, $Y$ could be the score of a student selected by a specified sampling procedure; $y$ is the value actually recorded. A **probability distribution** describes the possible values and their probabilities.

For discrete outcomes, probabilities are assigned to values. For continuous outcomes, a density describes probabilities through **areas over intervals**; the height of a density curve is not itself a probability.

A distribution has features such as its mean, standard deviation, median and upper quantiles. A sample has corresponding **statistics**, calculated from its observed values. The population mean and the sample mean are related but distinct objects. Their mathematical definitions are in the additional appendix.

### Frequentist and Bayesian uncertainty

In a standard frequentist analysis, the target parameter is fixed but unknown. The performance of an estimator or interval procedure is evaluated across repeated datasets generated under specified assumptions. A 95% confidence procedure covers the fixed target in 95% of repetitions when its coverage conditions hold; some procedures attain this only approximately. This is not a promise that exactly 95 out of the next 100 intervals will cover it.

In a Bayesian analysis, a prior distribution expresses uncertainty about parameters before the current data are used. Combining the prior with the likelihood produces a posterior distribution. A 95% credible interval contains 95% of the posterior probability **conditional on the observed data, model and prior**. Representing uncertainty about a fixed quantity with a distribution does not imply that the physical quantity changes whenever we draw another sample. {cite}`kruschke2015doing`

These interpretations differ even when numerical intervals are similar. Neither interval automatically accounts for an omitted source of bias or a poorly chosen measurement.

## 4. Choose a summary that answers the question

The mean is useful for questions about average scores, total burden or expected outcomes. A median, upper quantile or threshold probability may be more useful for a different question. Skewness alone does not decide which quantity matters.

Consider reaction times in a vigilance task:

| Scientific interest | Possible target quantity |
|---|---|
| Average time per response | Mean reaction time |
| The middle of the response-time distribution | Median reaction time |
| Particularly slow responses | 95th percentile of reaction time |
| Responses exceeding a specified delay | Probability that reaction time exceeds 2 seconds |

The 95th percentile is the **boundary above which about 5% of responses fall**, not the average of the slowest 5%. Calling a response over 2 seconds a “lapse” is an operational definition that needs justification; a long response alone does not establish a particular cognitive mechanism.

Frequentist methods also estimate medians, quantiles and nonlinear quantities, using methods such as quantile regression and appropriate bootstrap procedures. They are not restricted to means. See, for example, the [bootstrap teaching material by Cosma Shalizi](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~cshalizi/dst/20/lectures/16/lecture-16.html).

Bayesian computation provides a useful common workflow: for each **joint posterior draw** of the model parameters, calculate the target quantity. The resulting values describe its posterior uncertainty. For a reaction-time model, calculate the model's 95th percentile at every draw, then summarize those calculated percentiles. This differs from taking the 95th percentile of the posterior draws of a single parameter.

This workflow is flexible, but its answers depend on the observation model, prior and reliable computation. Sparse tail data can leave a tail estimate highly uncertain or sensitive to assumptions. A complete posterior distribution does not create information that the study did not collect.

## 5. Guided practice

### Exercise 1: Specify the question

“Does meditation work?” Write a more precise question that names a population, programme, comparison, outcome and time point. Then name one design feature needed to interpret the comparison causally.

:::{dropdown} Example answer
Among eligible volunteers at the named university, what is the average difference in the specified wellbeing score eight weeks after random assignment to an offer of the programme versus usual provision? Random assignment supports the assignment contrast, but missing follow-up and generalization beyond volunteers still require attention. Comparing only those who attended all classes answers a different question.
:::

### Exercise 2: Interpret an interval

A report gives a 95% interval for the mean score difference. What must you know before interpreting “95%”? Would repeating the study 100 times guarantee exactly 95 covering intervals?

:::{dropdown} Answer
Find out whether it is a confidence interval or a credible interval and what assumptions support it. Confidence refers to coverage of a procedure over repeated datasets; an actual set of 100 repetitions need not contain exactly 95 covering intervals. Credibility refers to posterior probability conditional on the model, prior and observed data. Neither is a prediction interval for an individual student's score.
:::

### Exercise 3: Choose the target and the unit

Forty participants complete 100 vigilance trials each. The question concerns responses longer than 2 seconds. What target would you report? What structure must the analysis retain?

:::{dropdown} Answer
Report a clearly defined exceedance probability, for example the probability of a response longer than 2 seconds for a new participant performing the specified task. Retain repeated observations within participants and shared items if items repeat across participants. Distinguish this new-participant target from an average across the recorded trials; weighting people or trials differently can change the estimand. The empirical proportion is a useful description, but uncertainty must reflect the sampling structure.
:::

## 6. Research protocol milestone 1

Prepare an **ungraded working record** for your course project:

1. **Question and aim:** What do you want to learn? Is the immediate aim descriptive, predictive or causal?
2. **Measurement and comparison:** What values are recorded, in what units and at what time? Which groups or conditions are compared?
3. **Units and population:** What is a row? Which participants or items repeat? Who was sampled, and to whom should the answer apply?
4. **Target quantity:** Mean, difference, proportion, quantile or prediction—and why that quantity answers the question.
5. **Practical relevance:** What magnitude or consequence would matter, and how would you justify it?
6. **Data limitations:** Source, missingness, exclusions and one measurement, selection or design limitation.

No fitted model, derivation or final choice of distribution is required. The purpose is to make the question precise enough to guide the next analysis decision.

## Additional appendix: mathematical detail and demonstrations

The following material is additional reading. It supports the lesson without adding required derivations or a graded task.

### A. Mean, spread and sample statistics

For a random variable $X$ with finite second moment,

$$
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad
\sigma^2=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2],\qquad
\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}.
$$

The expectation is a probability-weighted average. The variance measures spread in squared units, and the standard deviation returns to the original units. The sample mean $\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i$ is a statistic used to estimate a population mean under a suitable sampling model.

These moments need not exist for every distribution. A variance of outcomes is also not the same as the variance of an estimator or a posterior parameter distribution: always name what is varying.

### B. What the Central Limit Theorem says

In a standard version, if $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are independent and identically distributed with finite mean $\mu$ and finite, positive variance $\sigma^2$, then

$$
\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n-\mu)}{\sigma}
\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\operatorname{Normal}(0,1).
$$

For sufficiently large $n$, this motivates approximating the sampling distribution of the mean by $\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$. How accurate the approximation is depends on the distribution and the sample size; there is no universal “30 observations is enough” rule. See [Philip Stark's explanation and demonstrations](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/SticiGui/Text/clt.htm).

The theorem concerns the **sampling distribution of the mean**, not the distribution of individual observations. It does not make skewed reaction times Normally distributed or justify treating dependent trials as independent. Generalizations require their own conditions.

*Figure A.1. The left panel shows simulated individual observations for continuous (Uniform, Exponential) and discrete (Poisson) populations. The right panel shows means from 1,000 independent simulated studies at $n=30$ and $n=300$, alongside the corresponding Normal approximation. Notice that the sampling distribution of the mean approaches Normality regardless of whether the underlying distribution is continuous or discrete.*

The CLT is valuable in many methods, including analysis of Monte Carlo error under appropriate conditions. Bayesian models can describe parameter uncertainty without using a Normal sampling approximation for the data mean, but their validity is not guaranteed by the choice of framework.

### C. A posterior for a derived quantity

Suppose a supported model for reaction time is $Y\mid\mu,\sigma\sim\operatorname{LogNormal}(\mu,\sigma)$, with $Y$ expressed in seconds. At joint posterior draw $s$, the conditional 95th percentile is

$$
q_{0.95}^{(s)}=\exp\!\left(\mu^{(s)}+\sigma^{(s)}\Phi^{-1}(0.95)\right),
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard Normal CDF. Summarizing the collection of $q_{0.95}^{(s)}$ gives posterior uncertainty about that conditional quantile. Its credible interval is not a prediction interval for an individual response. With participants or items in a hierarchy, also specify whether the target is conditional on an existing unit or averaged over new units; a quantile of a mixture is not generally the average of the component quantiles.

For a different family, such as the Ex-Gaussian, use that family's quantile calculation at each joint draw. The model must plausibly describe the relevant tail, and simulation accuracy must be checked. Posterior computation and hierarchical prediction are developed later in the course.
