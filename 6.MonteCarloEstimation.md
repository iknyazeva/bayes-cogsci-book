# Part 6.1: Monte Carlo estimation

Monte Carlo estimation replaces an integral with an average over simulated draws. It is the foundational computational technique of Bayesian data analysis: whenever an analytical integral is difficult or impossible to evaluate, we can approximate it to arbitrary precision simply by simulating independent draws from the target distribution.

## 1. Geometric intuition: measuring area by throwing darts

The foundational intuition behind Monte Carlo methods—first developed by Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann in the 1940s—is that **calculating an integral is mathematically equivalent to measuring an area or volume**.

Suppose we want to compute the mathematical constant $\pi$. Consider a unit circle ($x^2 + y^2 \le 1$) inscribed inside a square spanning $[-1, 1] \times [-1, 1]$:
* Area of the bounding square: $2 \times 2 = 4$.
* Area of the inscribed unit circle: $\pi r^2 = \pi \times 1^2 = \pi$.

```
                       (-1, 1) ┌───────────────┐ (1, 1)
                               │     .---.     │
                               │   /       \   │
                               │  |    •    |  │  Unit Circle (Area = π)
                               │   \       /   │
                               │     '---'     │
                      (-1, -1) └───────────────┘ (1, -1)
                               Bounding Square (Area = 4)
```

If we scatter $S$ points uniformly at random across the bounding square, the probability that any single point lands inside the circle is proportional to its area:

$$
P(\text{inside circle}) = \frac{\text{Area of Circle}}{\text{Area of Bounding Square}} = \frac{\pi}{4}.
$$

By the Law of Large Numbers, the empirical proportion of points falling inside the circle converges to this probability as $S \to \infty$:

$$
\frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x_s^2 + y_s^2 \le 1) \xrightarrow{S \to \infty} \frac{\pi}{4}
\implies
\widehat\pi = 4 \times \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S \mathbb{I}(x_s^2 + y_s^2 \le 1).
$$

With $S = 10{,}000$ simulated draws, the estimate quickly concentrates within $\pm 0.03$ of the true mathematical value $\pi \approx 3.1416$. This proves the central premise of Monte Carlo: **continuous integration is transformed into discrete counting and averaging**.

## 2. Applied Bayesian case: the Beta–Binomial posterior

In cognitive science and empirical data analysis, we do not throw darts at circles; we estimate model parameters. 

Recall the conjugate Beta–Binomial model from Session 4: after observing $k = 7$ correct task responses out of $N = 10$ cognitive trials under a flat $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$ prior, the exact posterior distribution for accuracy $\theta$ was derived as:

$$
\theta \mid y \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha = 1 + 7 = 8, \; \beta = 1 + 3 = 4).
$$

Suppose a researcher needs to answer three concrete scientific questions:
1. **What is the expected task accuracy?** $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{1}{S}\sum \theta^{(s)} \approx 0.667$ (matching analytic truth $\frac{8}{12}$).
2. **What is the probability of competent performance?** $P(\theta > 0.60 \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum \mathbb{I}(\theta^{(s)} > 0.60) \approx 70.4\%$.
3. **What are the expected odds of success?** $\mathbb{E}[\omega \mid y]$, where $\omega = \frac{\theta}{1 - \theta}$.

Analytically, Question 2 requires incomplete beta integrals, and Question 3 requires deriving the Jacobian change-of-variables density $p(\omega)$ via calculus. With Monte Carlo simulation, answering all three questions requires **zero calculus**: we simply transform each posterior draw directly: $\omega^{(s)} = \frac{\theta^{(s)}}{1 - \theta^{(s)}}$.

```{important}
**Jensen's Inequality in Action:**  
The true expected odds is $\mathbb{E}[\omega] \approx 2.63$, whereas naively plugging the posterior mean into the formula gives $\frac{\mathbb{E}[\theta]}{1 - \mathbb{E}[\theta]} = \frac{0.667}{0.333} = 2.00$. Because odds is a convex function, **$g(\mathbb{E}[\theta]) \neq \mathbb{E}[g(\theta)]$**. Monte Carlo simulation automatically avoids this error by evaluating the transformation draw-by-draw!
```

## 3. How do computers sample from distributions, and does it matter?

### Does it matter which distribution we sample from?

**Yes, fundamentally.** In Monte Carlo estimation, every expectation is defined with respect to a **specific target probability density** $p(\theta)$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_p[g(\theta)] = \int g(\theta) \, p(\theta) \, d\theta \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S g(\theta^{(s)}), \qquad \text{where } \theta^{(s)} \sim p(\theta).
$$

If you mistakenly draw samples from another distribution $q(\theta)$, the unweighted sample average does **not** converge to $\mathbb{E}_p[g(\theta)]$:

$$
\frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S g(\theta^{(s)}) \xrightarrow{S \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_q[g(\theta)] \neq \mathbb{E}_p[g(\theta)].
$$

*Can you ever sample from a different distribution?* Yes, through **Importance Sampling**. If it is difficult to sample from $p(\theta)$ directly, but you can evaluate its density, you can draw samples from an alternative proposal distribution $q(\theta)$ and weight each draw by the importance ratio $w(\theta) = \frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_p[g(\theta)] = \int g(\theta) \frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)} q(\theta) \, d\theta \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S g(\theta^{(s)}) \cdot \frac{p(\theta^{(s)})}{q(\theta^{(s)})}, \qquad \theta^{(s)} \sim q(\theta).
$$

Without these importance weights, sampling from the wrong distribution produces systematically biased inferences.

### How do computers generate non-uniform random draws?

Physical computer hardware cannot generate continuous random variables; standard CPU algorithms (such as NumPy's 64-bit PCG64 pseudo-random generator) produce pseudo-random integers that are scaled into standard uniform numbers:

$$
U \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0, 1).
$$

To convert these uniform numbers into draws from continuous probability distributions (such as Normal, Exponential, or Beta), computers rely on three mathematical mechanisms:

1. **The Inverse CDF Method (Probability Integral Transform):**  
   If the cumulative distribution function $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ has an analytical inverse $F^{-1}(u)$, then setting $X = F^{-1}(U)$ guarantees that $X$ follows the distribution $F$.  
   *Example (Exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$):* $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x} \implies X = -\frac{\ln(1 - U)}{\lambda}$.
2. **Exact Mathematical Transformations:**  
   When the inverse CDF is not available in closed form (such as the standard Gaussian integral), special transformations are used. The **Box–Muller transform** converts two independent uniform numbers $(U_1, U_2)$ into two independent standard Normal draws $(Z_1, Z_2)$ using polar coordinates:
   $$Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\cos(2\pi U_2), \qquad Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\sin(2\pi U_2).$$
3. **Rejection Sampling:**  
   When neither the inverse CDF nor an exact transformation exists, algorithms envelop the target density $p(\theta)$ beneath an easy-to-sample proposal density $M \cdot q(\theta) \ge p(\theta)$.

### Interactive Demonstration: Rejection Sampling

**The Rejection Sampling Algorithm:**
1. Propose a candidate draw $\theta^* \sim q(\theta)$.
2. Propose a vertical height $u \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0, M \cdot q(\theta^*))$.
3. **Acceptance Rule**: If $u \le p(\theta^*)$, **accept** $\theta^*$; if $u > p(\theta^*)$, **reject** and discard it.
4. **Acceptance Rate**: The probability of accepting a proposal is exactly $\frac{1}{M}$.

### The Gateway to MCMC: Why standard sampling fails for Bayesian posteriors

Standard libraries like NumPy can easily generate draws because standard univariate distributions ($\operatorname{Normal}$, $\operatorname{Beta}$, $\operatorname{Gamma}$, $\operatorname{Poisson}$) have known mathematical forms, known normalizing constants, and optimized C-level samplers.

However, in realistic empirical Bayesian models, the joint posterior distribution:

$$
p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \, p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int p(y \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \, p(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, d\boldsymbol{\theta}}
$$

is **non-standard, multi-dimensional, and has an intractable normalizing denominator** (the marginal likelihood integral). Rejection sampling fails in high dimensions because the volume of the envelope $M \cdot q(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ grows exponentially relative to the target density (the curse of dimensionality), causing the acceptance rate $\frac{1}{M}$ to plummet to zero!

**This is the exact problem that motivates the rest of Session 6:**  
How can we generate representative draws from an arbitrary posterior distribution when we only know the unnormalized numerator $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$?  
The answer is **Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)**:
* **Metropolis–Hastings** (Part 6.4) and **Gibbs Sampling** (Part 6.5) navigate the parameter space via local random walks.
* **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and NUTS** (Part 6.2) use physical gradient trajectories to explore complex, high-dimensional posteriors efficiently.

## 4. The estimand is a function of the parameters

Let $\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(S)}$ be independent draws from a distribution $p(\theta)$. For any integrable function $g$,

$$
\mu_g=\mathbb E[g(\theta)]
\approx
\widehat\mu_g=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}g(\theta^{(s)}).
$$

Different choices of $g$ answer different questions:

| Question | Function of a draw |
|---|---|
| What is the mean? | $g(\theta)=\theta$ |
| What is $P(\theta>c)$? | $g(\theta)=\mathbb I(\theta>c)$ |
| What is a predicted outcome? | draw $\widetilde y^{(s)}\sim p(\widetilde y\mid\theta^{(s)})$ |
| What is a regional contrast? | $g(\theta)=\mu_A(\theta)-\mu_B(\theta)$ |
| What is a nonlinear policy summary? | evaluate the full transformation for every draw |

This draw-by-draw transformation is safer than inserting posterior means into a nonlinear function: in general, $\mathbb E[g(\theta)]\neq g(\mathbb E[\theta])$.

## 6. Monte Carlo standard error

Under the usual finite-variance central-limit conditions,

$$
\operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat\mu_g)
\approx \frac{s_g}{\sqrt S},
$$

where $s_g$ is the sample standard deviation of $g(\theta^{(s)})$. MCSE measures the numerical uncertainty of the estimate. It does not measure posterior uncertainty about $\theta$.

Useful checks include:

- plot the running estimate against the number of draws;
- repeat the entire simulation under several seeds;
- compare empirical error with the predicted $1/\sqrt S$ rate;
- report MCSE for the substantive estimand, not only for base parameters.

For MCMC draws, replace $S$ by an estimand-specific effective sample size. The elementary formula based on summed autocorrelations is useful for intuition, but modern software should estimate ESS robustly.

## 7. Probabilities as averages of indicators

For any event $A$,

$$
P(\theta\in A)=\mathbb E[\mathbb I(\theta\in A)]
\approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb I(\theta^{(s)}\in A).
$$

For example, the posterior probability that an intervention improves standardized trust by more than 0.10 is the proportion of draws satisfying $\delta^{(s)}>0.10$.

This is not the same as the probability that the intervention will improve trust for every individual. The latter is a predictive question requiring a model for individual outcomes.

Rare-event probabilities require particular care. If no event occurs among 2,000 draws, the probability has not been proved to be zero; the simulation may simply be too short or poorly targeted for that tail.

## 8. Quantiles and intervals

Posterior interval endpoints are sample quantiles. Quantiles are nonlinear summaries. Their Monte Carlo error cannot generally be obtained by substituting the posterior standard deviation into the mean formula. Use tail ESS and software estimates of quantile MCSE, or a suitable batch/bootstrap approximation when working with independent draws.

## 9. Predictive simulation is nested Monte Carlo

Posterior prediction has two layers:

1. draw plausible parameters $\theta^{(s)}$ from $p(\theta\mid y)$;
2. draw a replicated or future outcome $\widetilde y^{(s)}$ from $p(\widetilde y\mid\theta^{(s)})$.

For a survey model, one draw may contain region effects, regression coefficients, and residual variation. A predicted respondent score should retain all relevant layers rather than use only the posterior mean regression line.

This separates:

- uncertainty about parameters;
- variation among future observations conditional on parameters;
- Monte Carlo error from using a finite number of draws.
