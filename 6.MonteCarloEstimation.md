# Required practice: Monte Carlo estimation

Monte Carlo estimation replaces an expectation or probability with an average over simulated draws. This page develops the one computational idea needed throughout the rest of the course:

> **Transform every posterior draw into the quantity of interest, average or summarise those transformed draws, and quantify the numerical error of that summary.**

## 1. One analytic posterior, many questions

Use the same benchmark as the Session 6 core. Suppose

$$
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(2,2),
\qquad
y\mid\theta\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(80,\theta),
\qquad y=48.
$$

The exact posterior is

$$
\theta\mid y\sim\operatorname{Beta}(50,34).
$$

After drawing $\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(S)}$ from this posterior, different scientific questions require different draw-level functions:

| Question | Draw-level quantity |
|---|---|
| What is mean support? | $g(\theta^{(s)})=\theta^{(s)}$ |
| How probable is majority support? | $g(\theta^{(s)})=\mathbb I(\theta^{(s)}>0.5)$ |
| How probable is support above 0.6? | $g(\theta^{(s)})=\mathbb I(\theta^{(s)}>0.6)$ |
| What are the odds? | $g(\theta^{(s)})=\theta^{(s)}/(1-\theta^{(s)})$ |
| What interval contains the central 95%? | sample quantiles of the draws |

For any integrable $g$,

$$
\mathbb E[g(\theta)\mid y]
\approx
\widehat\mu_g
=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}g(\theta^{(s)}).
$$

Apply nonlinear transformations to every draw. In general,

$$
\mathbb E[g(\theta)\mid y]
\neq
g\!\left(\mathbb E[\theta\mid y]\right).
$$

For example, the mean of the posterior odds is not generally the odds calculated from the posterior mean.

## 2. Monte Carlo error is not posterior uncertainty

The posterior distribution describes uncertainty about $\theta$ under the model and data. Monte Carlo error describes numerical uncertainty caused by using only a finite number of draws to summarise that posterior.

For independent draws and a mean with finite variance,

$$
\operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat\mu_g)
\approx \frac{s_g}{\sqrt S},
$$

where $s_g$ is the sample standard deviation of $g(\theta^{(s)})$.

Consequently:

- increasing $S$ reduces MCSE but does not narrow the posterior;
- quadrupling $S$ approximately halves the MCSE of a mean;
- MCSE should be calculated for the reported estimand, not only for base parameters;
- the formula changes for dependent MCMC draws because autocorrelation reduces effective information.

## 4. Probabilities are averages of indicators

For any event $A$,

$$
P(\theta\in A\mid y)
=\mathbb E[\mathbb I(\theta\in A)\mid y]
\approx
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb I(\theta^{(s)}\in A).
$$

Thus, $P(\theta>0.6\mid y)$ is the proportion of posterior draws above $0.6$. For independent draws, the indicator values can also be used to estimate the probability's MCSE.

This parameter probability is not the probability that every future individual will show the outcome. The latter is a predictive question and must include the outcome model.

Rare tail probabilities need particular care. Observing no events among a finite set of draws does not prove that the probability is zero; the run may contain too little effective information about that tail.

## 5. Quantiles and intervals

Equal-tail posterior interval endpoints are sample quantiles. Quantiles are nonlinear summaries, so their MCSE is not obtained by inserting the posterior standard deviation into the formula for a mean. With MCMC output, inspect tail ESS and use software estimates of quantile MCSE when interval endpoints are substantively important.

The key comparison remains:

| Quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Width of posterior interval | Uncertainty about the parameter under the model and data |
| MCSE of an interval endpoint | Numerical uncertainty in the estimated endpoint |

## 6. Prediction adds outcome variation

Posterior prediction contains two steps:

1. draw $\theta^{(s)}$ from $p(\theta\mid y)$;
2. draw a replicated or future outcome $\widetilde y^{(s)}$ from $p(\widetilde y\mid\theta^{(s)})$.

This separates three quantities that should not be conflated:

- posterior uncertainty about parameters;
- variation among outcomes conditional on parameters;
- Monte Carlo error from a finite simulation.

For a cognitive experiment, a predicted response may also require participant and item effects. Those layers must be simulated according to the intended prediction target rather than discarded by averaging parameters first.

## 7. Optional mechanism: rejection sampling

Rejection sampling illustrates how an easy proposal can generate independent draws from a more difficult target. Choose $q(\theta)$ and $M$ so that

$$
p(\theta)\leq Mq(\theta)
$$

throughout the target support. Draw a candidate from $q$ and retain it with the corresponding acceptance probability. When $p$ and $q$ are normalised and the bound is valid, the average acceptance probability is $1/M$.

The figure is a low-dimensional teaching demonstration. Rejection sampling can become extremely inefficient when a proposal poorly covers the target, especially as dimension increases. That limitation motivates Markov-chain methods, but it does not imply that every posterior must be sampled with MCMC.

## 8. Guided practice

For posterior draws of a treatment contrast $\delta$, calculate:

1. $\mathbb E[\delta\mid y]$;
2. $P(\delta>0\mid y)$;
3. $P(\delta>0.10\mid y)$;
4. a 95% equal-tail interval;
5. MCSE for each probability using its indicator draws.

Then label each result as either a feature of the posterior or a statement about the numerical accuracy of its estimate.

````{dropdown} Check: quadrupling the draws
You increase the independent draw count from $S$ to $4S$. What happens to posterior uncertainty and to the MCSE of a mean?

**Answer.** The posterior distribution does not change. The MCSE of a mean is approximately halved, provided the relevant variance is finite.
````
