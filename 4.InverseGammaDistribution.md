# Inverse Gamma Distribution

The **Inverse Gamma distribution** is a continuous probability distribution often used as a **conjugate prior** for the variance of a normally distributed variable in Bayesian statistics. It is defined for positive real numbers and is the distribution of the inverse of a gamma-distributed random variable.

##  Probability Density Function (PDF)

```{important}
**Inverse Gamma PDF.** The Inverse Gamma distribution with shape parameter $\alpha$ and scale parameter $\beta$ has density:

$$
f(x \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{-\alpha - 1} \exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{x}\right), \quad x > 0
$$

where:
- $\alpha$ (shape) controls the shape of the distribution.
- $\beta$ (scale) controls the spread.
- $\Gamma(\alpha)$ is the gamma function.
```

##  Mean and Variance

For an inverse gamma distribution $X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$, the mean and variance are:

- **Mean** (exists only for $\alpha > 1$):

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1}
$$

- **Variance** (exists only for $\alpha > 2$):

$$
\text{Var}(X) = \frac{\beta^2}{(\alpha - 1)^2 (\alpha - 2)}
$$

```{note}
The mean exists only for $\alpha > 1$ and the variance only for $\alpha > 2$. Historically, analysts used "vague" priors with small parameters like $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(\epsilon, \epsilon)$ (e.g., $\epsilon = 0.001$). However, as shown by {cite}`gelman2006prior`, an infinite prior mean does not imply non-informativeness: setting $\epsilon \to 0$ concentrates a steep spike of prior mass near zero, which can severely distort variance estimates when the true variance is small or data are sparse. Conjugacy provides computational convenience, not guaranteed objectivity.
```

## Usage in Bayesian Inference

The Inverse Gamma distribution is primarily used as a **prior distribution** for the **variance** $\sigma^2$ of a normal distribution in Bayesian inference. It is conjugate to the normal likelihood with unknown variance, meaning the posterior distribution of $\sigma^2$ remains an inverse gamma when the prior is inverse gamma.

### When to Use the Inverse Gamma Distribution

The Inverse Gamma distribution is used in the following scenarios:

1. **Conjugate Prior for Variance**:
   In Bayesian statistics, if we have data $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ that are normally distributed with an unknown variance $\sigma^2$, the **Inverse Gamma distribution** is often used as a conjugate prior for $\sigma^2$. This makes posterior inference analytically tractable.

2. **Modeling Uncertainty in Scale Parameters**:
   It is also used to model uncertainty in **scale parameters** (e.g., variances, rates) in hierarchical models or other Bayesian settings.

### Example in Bayesian Inference

Suppose we are modeling data from a normal distribution with unknown variance $\sigma^2$. The likelihood is:

$$
X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

We place an **Inverse Gamma prior** on $\sigma^2$:

$$
\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)
$$

```{important}
**Inverse-Gamma conjugacy for Normal variance.** 
1. **Known mean $\mu$**: With an $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior on $\sigma^2$ and $n$ observations from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ where $\mu$ is known, the posterior is:

$$
\sigma^2 \mid X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(\alpha + \frac{n}{2}, \;\beta + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \mu)^2\right)
$$

2. **Unknown mean $\mu$**: If $\mu$ is also unknown and assigned an improper flat prior $p(\mu) \propto 1$, integrating out $\mu$ consumes 1 degree of freedom, replacing $n$ with $n-1$ and deviations around $\mu$ with deviations around the sample mean $\bar{X}$:

$$
\sigma^2 \mid X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(\alpha + \frac{n-1}{2}, \;\beta + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2\right)
$$

The shape increases by half the effective degrees of freedom and the scale increases by half the sum of squared deviations.
```

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example: Trial-to-Trial Variability in EEG Amplitude
:class: tip
A neuroscientist measures the amplitude of the P300 event-related potential (ERP) across $n = 20$ trials for a single participant. The amplitudes are assumed to be normally distributed with unknown mean $\mu$ and unknown variance $\sigma^2$ (reflecting trial-to-trial physiological variability and measurement noise). She places an $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(3, 50)$ prior on $\sigma^2$, encoding a belief that the variance is around $25\;\mu\text{V}^2$ (prior mean = $50/(3-1) = 25$).

After observing the data with sample variance sum $\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2 = 480$, estimating the unknown mean $\mu$ uses 1 degree of freedom ($n - 1 = 19$). Under an independent flat prior on $\mu$, the marginal posterior for $\sigma^2$ becomes:

$$
\sigma^2 \mid X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(3 + \frac{19}{2}, \; 50 + \frac{480}{2}\right) = \text{Inverse-Gamma}(12.5, \; 290)
$$

The posterior mean is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\sigma^2 \mid X] = \frac{290}{12.5 - 1} = \frac{290}{11.5} \approx 25.22\;\mu\text{V}^2
$$

*(Note: If baseline mean $\mu = \mu_0$ had been known theoretically in advance, the update would use all $n = 20$ observations: $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(13, 290)$ with mean $290/12 \approx 24.17\;\mu\text{V}^2$. Furthermore, trial-to-trial variance reflects within-subject variability; evaluating true measurement reliability additionally requires decomposing signal from noise across repeated sessions or stimuli).*
```

### Properties of the Inverse Gamma Distribution

- **Heavy-tailed**: The Inverse Gamma distribution is **heavy-tailed**, meaning it places non-negligible probability on very large values of the variance $\sigma^2$. Note, however, that heavy tails in the *prior* do not make the *likelihood* robust against data outliers (which requires a Student-$t$ observation model).
- **Non-negative Support**: The distribution is defined only for positive values, making it suitable for modeling variances and other scale parameters.

### Relationship to Gamma Distribution

If $X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$, then $Y = \frac{1}{X}$ follows a **Gamma distribution** with parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$:

$$
Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)
$$

This connection is why the distribution is called **Inverse Gamma**.

## Key Takeaways

- The **Inverse Gamma** distribution is the standard conjugate prior for the **variance** $\sigma^2$ of a Normal likelihood.
- Its parameters have clear interpretations: $\alpha$ controls shape (and determines whether the mean/variance exist), while $\beta$ controls scale.
- With known $\mu$, add $n/2$ to shape and $\frac{1}{2}\sum(X_i - \mu)^2$ to scale. When $\mu$ is unknown and integrated out, add $(n-1)/2$ to shape and $\frac{1}{2}\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2$ to scale.
- Setting "vague" priors with near-zero parameters ($\alpha = \beta = \epsilon$) is problematic: as {cite}`gelman2006prior` demonstrated, it induces an unintended spike near zero that distorts small-sample inferences.
- The Inverse Gamma is related to the Gamma by inversion: if $\sigma^2 \sim \text{IG}(\alpha, \beta)$, then the precision $\tau = 1/\sigma^2 \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$.
