# Inverse Gamma Distribution

The **Inverse Gamma distribution** is a continuous probability distribution often used as a **conjugate prior** for the variance of a normally distributed variable in Bayesian statistics. It is defined for positive real numbers and is the distribution of the inverse of a gamma-distributed random variable.

##  Probability Density Function (PDF)

```{important}
**Inverse Gamma PDF.** The Inverse Gamma distribution with shape parameter $\alpha$ and scale parameter $\beta$ has density:

$$
f(x \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{-\alpha - 1} \exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{x}\right), \quad x > 0
$$

where:
- $\alpha$ (shape) controls the shape of the distribution.
- $\beta$ (scale) controls the spread.
- $\Gamma(\alpha)$ is the gamma function.
```

##  Mean and Variance

For an inverse gamma distribution $X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$, the mean and variance are:

- **Mean** (exists only for $\alpha > 1$):

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1}
$$

- **Variance** (exists only for $\alpha > 2$):

$$
\text{Var}(X) = \frac{\beta^2}{(\alpha - 1)^2 (\alpha - 2)}
$$

```{note}
The mean only exists for $\alpha > 1$ and the variance only for $\alpha > 2$. When using vague priors with small $\alpha$ (e.g., $\alpha = 0.01$), the prior distribution has very heavy tails and no finite mean — this is intentional, expressing maximal ignorance about the scale.
```

## Usage in Bayesian Inference

The Inverse Gamma distribution is primarily used as a **prior distribution** for the **variance** $\sigma^2$ of a normal distribution in Bayesian inference. It is conjugate to the normal likelihood with unknown variance, meaning the posterior distribution of $\sigma^2$ remains an inverse gamma when the prior is inverse gamma.

### When to Use the Inverse Gamma Distribution

The Inverse Gamma distribution is used in the following scenarios:

1. **Conjugate Prior for Variance**:
   In Bayesian statistics, if we have data $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ that are normally distributed with an unknown variance $\sigma^2$, the **Inverse Gamma distribution** is often used as a conjugate prior for $\sigma^2$. This makes posterior inference easier.

2. **Modeling Uncertainty in Scale Parameters**:
   It is also used to model uncertainty in **scale parameters** (e.g., variances, rates) in hierarchical models or other Bayesian settings.

### Example in Bayesian Inference

Suppose we are modeling data from a normal distribution with unknown variance $\sigma^2$. The likelihood is:

$$
X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

We place an **Inverse Gamma prior** on $\sigma^2$:

$$
\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)
$$

```{important}
**Inverse-Gamma conjugacy for Normal variance.** With an $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior on $\sigma^2$ and $n$ observations from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ (with $\mu$ known), the posterior is:

$$
\sigma^2 \mid X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(\alpha + \frac{n}{2}, \;\beta + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \mu)^2\right)
$$

The shape increases by $n/2$ (half the sample size) and the scale increases by half the sum of squared deviations. This is the continuous analogue of how Beta parameters accumulate counts in the discrete case.
```

```{admonition} Cognitive Science Example: Variability in EEG Amplitude
:class: tip
A neuroscientist measures the amplitude of the P300 event-related potential (ERP) across $n = 20$ trials for a single participant. The amplitudes are assumed to be normally distributed with unknown variance $\sigma^2$ (reflecting trial-to-trial variability). She places an $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(3, 50)$ prior on $\sigma^2$, encoding a belief that the variance is around $25\;\mu V^2$ (prior mean = $50/(3-1) = 25$).

After observing the data with $\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2 = 480$, the posterior becomes $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(13, 290)$, with posterior mean $290/12 \approx 24.2\;\mu V^2$. This allows her to characterize not just the average P300 amplitude, but also the reliability (consistency) of the signal.
```

### Properties of the Inverse Gamma Distribution

- **Heavy-tailed**: The Inverse Gamma distribution is **heavy-tailed**, meaning it places non-negligible probability on very large values of the variance $\sigma^2$.
- **Non-negative Support**: The distribution is defined only for positive values, making it suitable for modeling variances and other scale parameters.

### Relationship to Gamma Distribution

If $X \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$, then $Y = \frac{1}{X}$ follows a **Gamma distribution** with parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$:

$$
Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)
$$

This connection is why the distribution is called **Inverse Gamma**.

## Key Takeaways

- The **Inverse Gamma** distribution is the standard conjugate prior for the **variance** $\sigma^2$ of a Normal likelihood.
- Its parameters have clear interpretations: $\alpha$ controls shape (and determines whether the mean/variance exist), while $\beta$ controls scale.
- The posterior update is straightforward: add $n/2$ to the shape and half the sum of squared deviations to the scale.
- It is **heavy-tailed**, which can be both an advantage (robustness to outliers) and a disadvantage (sensitivity to the prior with small samples).
- The Inverse Gamma is related to the Gamma by inversion: if $\sigma^2 \sim \text{IG}(\alpha, \beta)$, then the precision $1/\sigma^2 \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$.
